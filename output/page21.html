These golden-crusted tempeh balls are a variation on the ones I included in my last book. They’re nutritionally packed with beans, grated tempeh and shredded veggies, brightened with a splash of ponzu. You can enjoy them on their own, or as part of a larger meal. I had them here as part of a bento-style lunch alongside some wild rice, leftover baked quinoa patties, and edamame. I also like them as a stand-in for traditional meatballs with spaghetti and this mushroom ragù. 

Variations

I play around quite a bit with the make-up of these tempeh balls. Keep the ratio of beans to tempeh intact and then experiment with the rest. A few past successes include:

  • Spicy: If I’m going to go spicy, I tend to introduce a spicy component through something like this chili crisp. Opting for a drizzle of that in place of the ponzu. That said, you can absolutely spice the actual tempeh balls themselves if you like. Use a bit of chili powder, crushed red pepper flakes, or work a favorite curry paste into the mixture.
  • Herb-filled: Swap out the cabbage for chopped soft, green herbs. A blend of chives, dill, cilantro, and basil are all good candidates. I like to trade out the onion for chopped scallions in this version.
  • Super Green: Use finely chopped kale, spinach, and/or chard in place of the corn/carrots and cabbage.

Pan-Fried Tempeh Ponzu Balls: Step-by-step

These are a breeze to make. The first thing you’re going to do is puree the beans and then combine them in a big bowl with the other ingredients (photo below). As I mention above, I used corn for this version because it’s in season, but you can use carrots.

The key to the next step is getting in there with your hands. You really want to mash and smash and squeeze the mixture together so it’s uniform and all the ingredients are distributed equally (see below).

To shape the tempeh balls, use a tablespoon and your hands. If things get too tacky or messy, rinse your hands and reboot. Keeping your hands damp and clean-ish throughout the process is helpful.

The last step is pan-frying the tempeh balls. My one pro-tip is to let them get a good amount of color on each side before you start to move them around the pan. This way the tempeh mixture is less likely to stick to the pan.

I typically serve these with ponzu sauce. It is quite salty, so I under-season the ponzu balls a bit. If you’re going to serve them with something else, something not as assertive on the salt front, go ahead and adjust the tempeh mixture with more, until it tastes good to you.

Pasta FAQ

How long do pasta leftovers stay fresh in the fridge?

Most pasta leftovers will stay fresh in the fridge for up to 3-4 days in an airtight container, depending on the dish. You can typically store the sauce and the noodles together, no need to separate.

Can I make any pasta recipe gluten-free?

Yes, most all pasta recipes can easily be made gluten-free. Use your favorite gluten-free pasta noodles (any shape, any kind) and ensure that your other ingredients are 100% certified gluten-free.

How do I know pasta noodles are done cooking?

The best way to check if pasta is done cooking, or perfectly al dente, is by taste testing it 1-2 minutes before the timer goes off. It should have a slight bite to it.

Is pasta good for meal prep?

Yes, pasta is an amazing meal prep option! I like making Mediterranean Gnocchi for meal prep because besides gnocchi, it also has lentils and veggies in it for extra fiber and protein.

How much salt should I add to pasta water?

A generous spoonful, around a tablespoon, is plenty for a large pot of noodles. The water should taste salty like the sea!

Cooling and refreshing noodles are the Asian antidote for hot days. I have amassed a small collection of recipes for them and am adding this one from Japanese Comfort Cooking, a cookbook by Tadashi Ono and Harris Salat. They are two New Yorkers with good tastes and a realistic, cheffy approach to presenting doable Japanese recipes. I loved their past books, such as Japanese Soul Cooking, because I always learn from their approachable recipes. Moreover, they do not sugarcoat cultural truths.

This cold soba dish caught my eye because it's more substantial than the classic cold soba dish -- zaru soba. Whenever I eat zaru soba, I need to make other dishes as sides because the noodles and clear broth are not enough. Goma dare soba is loaded with veggies and seasoned with a protein-rich, salty-sweet broth. The broth is called "goma dare" and you may have had something similar with shabu shabu. Here it's plays a major role to define the flavor and texture of the noodle dish.

Cold sesame soba is easy to put together and delicious when you have homemade dashi in the fridge; make it vegan with shiitake dashi or use regular dashi. You can prep the broth, noodles and cucumber a couple days ahead, and then return them to room temperature and finish dish. Lunch or dinner is made. Below are some pointers for you to be extra successful with this comforting Japanese noodle dish.

Cold sesame soba broth done your way

Ono and Salat's original recipe is wonderful but in preparing the noodles, I made some minor tweaks. First, I halved the broth recipe. The authors say you'll have leftovers but so why over commit, in case you do not like the noodles? You can halve my halved recipe to serve two (or serve 4, if you're not brothy).

The bold broth for this cold noodle acts as a dressing of sorts. Like with many brothy Japanese noodles, you're not expected to drink all the broth. It is purposefully salty. Add as much or as little as you like to flavor your bowl. Any leftover broth will keep for days in the fridge so you can make more cold sesame soba! The adapted recipe below offers a range so you can have guardrails. The broth ingredients below are not exotic. Whenever you're in a pinch, make your own mirin with this recipe, because it will be leagues better than the sugary, bleh stuff at supermarkets.


Ono and Salat call for Japanese sesame paste which is not as readily available. Tahini is milder tasting and they permit it as sub. I used tahini and the flavor was very good. I opted for agave syrup for a rounder mouthfeel than sugar. When the vinegar and soy sauce hit the other broth ingredients, the broth curdles and separates a bit . That's the soy milk reacting to the acid and salt. Do not worry! Once the broth hits the soba, the starch in the noodles cancel out the separated soy milk and your noodles become wonderfully cloaked in silkiness. I used my homemade dashi for excellent flavor.

Noodle and veggie swaps

Aside from soba, Ono and Salat suggest featuring substituting ramen, somen, or udon. I imagine if you have capellini pasta, that would work too! The beauty of soba is that they are packaged as pre-portioned 3-ounce quantities. The Japanese are consistent!

Asparagus season is fleeting so when it's unavailable use green beans, snap peas, or carrots. I cut my green beans on a steep bias so they blend in with the noodles and be chopstickable. Blanch the veggie in the pot of water before cooking the soba. You'll save dishwashing time.

How to thinly slice avocado

The avocado lends richness to these noodles. If you want it to look good and not be mushy or clumpy. Here are my tips:

  • Make sure to use a firm-ripe avocado.
  • Halve, seed, then peel the avocado.
  • With the cut side facing down, quickly drag your knife through the avocado, spacing the cuts a good ⅛ inch (3mm) apart.
  • Use your hand to gently press, tilt and fan out the avocado slices to one side.
  • Transfer the slices to the bowls.
  • Do the avocado slicing before serving to avoid browned avocado.

Cold Sesame Soba | Goma Dare Soba

This recipe for 胡麻だれ蕎麦 has been adapted from the original published in Japanese Comfort Cooking: An Opinionated Guide toModern, Homey, Classic Japanese Recipes by Tadashi Ono and Harris Salat (© 2026, Ten Speed Press, an imprint of Penguin Random House). Course Main Course, noodles Cuisine Japanese Keyword cold noodles, soba, vegan Servings 4

Ingredients

Sesame broth

  • ¾ cup plus 2 tablespoon | 210ml Dashi, regular or shiitake (see Notes)
  • ¼ cup | 60ml Japanese sesame paste or tahini
  • ¼ cup | 60ml unseasoned rice vinegar
  • ¼ cup | 60ml mirin (see Notes)
  • 1 cup soy sauce
  • ¼ cup sugar or agave syrup
  • 2 cups unsweetened soy milk

Soba and toppings

  • 12 ounces | 360g dried soba noodles (4 portions)
  • 2 Persian or 1 small English cucumber, cut into thin strips
  • 4 ounces | 120g asparagus or green beans, blanched, cooled, and
  • 1 firm-ripe avocado, sliced
  • ½ small | 60g red onion, thinly sliced and soaked in cold water for 10 minutes
  • 2 teaspoons wasabi
  • 1 or 2 full sheets nori, snipped into narrow, short strips (see Note)

Instructions

  • Make the sesame broth: In a large bowl, whisk together the dashi, sesame paste, rice vinegar, mirin, soy sauce, sugar, and soy milk until smooth. (Depending on the package, you might need to knead the sesame paste a bit to get it all out.) Keep the broth in the refrigerator until you're ready to serve.
  • Cook the soba: To cook the soba, first prepare an ice water bath in a large bowl and set it in the sink. Bring a large pot of water to a boil over high heat. Add the soba and cook for about 3 minutes, or until it is cooked through but still retains some bite. Drain and dunk the noodles in the ice water bath. When the noodles are cool, drain them and set aside.
  • Assemble and serve: Divide the soba among four serving bowls. Pour ½ to 1 cup (120ml to 240ml) of the sesame broth over the noodles in each bowl. Top each bowl with equal portions of cucumber, asparagus, avocado, and drained red onion, arranging them in separate mounds. Add a small dab (about ½ teaspoon) of wasabi to each bowl. Sprinkle the nori over the top for garnish.
  • Serve immediately, encouraging everyone to mix everything together before diving in. Refrigerate leftover broth for up to a week to use again.

One of the first “recipes” on this blog was No-Recipe Cherry Jam, posted in 2005, which wasn’t quite a recipe. Nevertheless, a lot of people successfully used those guidelines to make cherry jam over the last fifteen years. The basis for it was how I’ve been making jam forever, using 3 parts sugar to 4 parts fruit puree. The standard ratio is to use equal parts sugar to fruit puree but I like to use less.

I often make cherry jam with sweet cherries, which are what one usually finds at the market. But if you can find sour cherries, those are wonderful in the mix, but when fresh, they have a short shelf life so should be used, pronto.

So feel free to mix things up, cherry-wise!

Unlike other recipes, making jam can require a bit of using your instincts, rather than relying on set times and temperatures. Checking the jam texture on a chilled plate is the best way to tell you when it’s done. (As an example, jam usually sets between 218-220ºF,103-104ºC, but this one set a few degrees higher.) Because cherries are low in pectin, this one often “sets” a few degrees higher, although don’t expect cherry jam to be as stiff as other jams that you might be used to, or buy. It’ll be a bit runny, which is just how it should be.

One tool I do recommend is a cherry pitter. Not everyone has one, and they do fall into the “single use” category, but come cherry season, mine gets a good workout. I wrote more about why you should get a cherry pitter here.

The biggest question I get about jam recipes is people asking about reducing the amount of sugar in them. Sugar in jam does a couple of things. One, it helps the mixture jell, along with any natural pectin in the fruit. And two, it preserves it. If you use less, the jam will be runnier and won’t last as long.

Europeans tend to like their jams runnier than their American counterparts, so in the morning, you can have a European experience when you spoon some of this jam on your morning toast, as we do.

Unlike other jams that wrinkle when you do the wrinkle test (putting a spoonful of jam on a frozen plate, returning the plate to the freezer and when the jam wrinkles with you nudge it, it’s done), this one will be more like thickened syrup. It’s done when it’s thick and sticky and holds its shape for a few moments after you drag a spoon through it (below.) The cherries will be shiny and glossy, like beautiful ruby-colored jewels.

Cherry Jam

If you only have 1 pound (450g) of cherries, feel free to cut the recipe in half. The liqueur is optional but heightens the flavors of the jam. Use only enough so it complements the fruit – you don't want to overwhelm it. As mentioned, this jam will be a bit on the runny side when "set." Sour cherries will produce a softer-set jam than sweet cherries. Course Breakfast Cuisine American Keyword cherries, cherry, jam

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds (900g) sweet or sour cherries or a mix
  • 2 1/2 cups (500g) sugar
  • freshly squeezed juice of one medium lemon plus additional juice if desired
  • 1-2 drops of almond extract or 1-2 teaspoons of kirsch or another liqueur, such as amaretto or vodka

Instructions

  • Remove the stems and pit the cherries. Put them in a large non-reactive pot. If using only sweet cherries, add 2-3 tablespoons of water to the pot. (Sour cherries will exude enough liquid on their own so no need to add additional water.) Put a small plate or saucer in the freezer.
  • Cover the pot and cook over medium heat, stirring occasionally, until the cherries are wilted and cooked through, about 10 minutes.
  • Remove the lid and stir in the sugar and lemon juice. Increase the heat to medium-high and cook, stirring frequently until the mixture thickens. It's hard to say how long it will take, but when the mixture resembles the sixth picture in the post, the one in the pot with the bubbles, turn off the heat and test the jam by putting a small spoonful on the frozen dish. Return the dish to the freezer for a few minutes until the jam on it is cool. Run a spoon through it and if the liquid is very thick and leaves a clear patch, as shown in the photo in the post, it's done. If not, return the plate to the freezer and continue to cook the jam, testing it at various intervals, until it's done. (If you want to use a thermometer to check the jam, it should be ready somewhere between 220º and 225ºF, 104º-107ºC.)
  • When ready, turn off the heat and stir in the almond extract or liqueur (or both, if you wish). If desired, you could add an extra squeeze of lemon juice to balance the sweetness. Ladle into clean jars.

Creamy Tahini Pasta Salad (plant-based!)

Your new go-to briny pasta salad, perfect for parties, meal prep, or weeknight dinner. Course dinner, Lunch Cuisine American Diet Vegan Prep Time 10 minutes minutes Cook Time 10 minutes minutes Total Time 20 minutes minutes Servings 6 Calories 378kcal Author Danielle Brown Cost $18

Equipment

  • 1 salad bowl
  • 1 set of salad tongs
  • 1 colander
  • 1 pot
  • 1 whisk

Ingredients

  • 8 oz pasta I use penne
  • 1 can chickpeas drained and rinsed
  • 1 tablespoon smoked paprika
  • Avocado oil spray
  • 2 cups cherry tomatoes halved
  • 1 red bell pepper diced
  • 1 can hearts of palm drained and chopped
  • ½ cup feta crumbled, I use dairy-free
  • 2 cups spinach chopped
  • ½ cup sun-dried tomatoes chopped
  • ½ cup Kalamata olives pitted and halved
  • ⅓ cup banana peppers chopped

Creamy Tahini Dressing

  • 3 tablespoon extra virgin olive oil can sub avocado oil
  • ⅓ cup tahini
  • 3 tablespoon red wine vinegar
  • Salt and pepper to taste
  • Juice of 1 lemon optional

Instructions

  • Cook the pasta according to package instructions. While pasta cooks, finely chop and prep all of the veggies and drain and rinse the chickpeas.
  • Make the spiced chickpeas: add them to a glass bowl, spray with avocado oil, and add smoked paprika. Toss to combine then, microwave for 45 seconds – 1 minute until warm and slightly crispy.
  • Once pasta is done cooking, strain and set aside.
  • In the bottom of a large bowl, add all of the dressing ingredients and whisk well to combine.
  • Add the pasta along with all of the veggies and chickpeas to the bowl.
  • Toss well to combine and serve!

Notes

  • Use gluten-free noodles to keep this pasta salad gluten-free friendly.
  • Leftovers will stay fresh in the fridge for up to 3 days. Pasta salad is eaten cold so no need to reheat!
  • Chop all of the veggies super small and similar sizes to make for an even salad.
