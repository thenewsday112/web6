👩🏽‍⚕️ Dietitian's Note

Delicata squash provides beta-carotene, which your body converts to vitamin A to support vision and immune function. Roasting helps make beta-carotene easier to absorb. While some heat-sensitive vitamin C is lost, the squash still provides valuable nutrients and fiber.

Roasting at 360°F helps the squash soften without burning the balsamic glaze. Keep it oil-free, or add a small drizzle of oil for more golden edges and to help your body absorb beta-carotene. You can also get that benefit by serving it with a tahini dressing or other fat-containing foods.

🌱 Substitutions

This recipe is versatile and easy to adapt. Below are some simple ingredient substitutions.

  • Dijon mustard: Use stone-ground mustard in the same amount.
  • Smoked paprika: Use sweet paprika for a milder flavor without the smokiness.
  • Maple syrup: Omit it for a less sweet marinade. The squash and balsamic vinegar already contribute sweetness.
  • Olive oil or avocado oil: Add a drizzle for more golden, crisp edges.

📝 How to Make Roasted Delicata Squash Oil-Free

How to Cut Delicata Squash:

  1. Prep the delicata squash: Wash and dry the squash-no peeling needed. Trim off both ends. Stand it on a flat end and carefully slice in half lengthwise. Scoop out the seeds with a spoon.
  1. Cut evenly: Place each half cut-side down and slice crosswise into ½-inch half-moons for even roasting.

Marinate the squash:

  1. Whisk the marinade in a wide bowl. Dry the squash well so the marinade coats it without becoming diluted.
  1. Toss thoroughly to coat both sides of each slice. No soaking time needed. Spoon any remaining marinade over the squash during baking.

Bake at a low temperature:

  1. Use high-quality nonstick parchment paper or a silicone baking mat to prevent sticking. Arrange in a single layer without overlapping. For oil-free roasting, use a moderate temperature of 360°F to help the squash become tender before the balsamic glaze burns.
  1. Flip halfway through for even cooking. Brush with the remaining balsamic glaze halfway through baking. For more golden, crisp edges, toss with a small drizzle of olive or avocado oil before roasting.

For serving:

  1. Serve warm or chilled. Add to salads or grain bowls. Finish with tahini dressing and toasted pumpkin seeds for extra flavor and crunch.
  1. Serve as an easy vegetable side for weeknight dinners or holiday meals, or pair with my White Beans in Cauliflower Cream Sauce for a satisfying plant-based meal.

✔️ Storage & Meal Prep Tips

Let the squash cool before transferring it to an airtight container in the refrigerator. Reheat on a lined baking sheet until warmed through, or use the microwave for convenience. The texture will soften during storage.

For meal prep, whisk the marinade separately and slice the squash ahead of time. Refrigerate both until ready to toss and roast.

🙋🏽‍♀️ Recipe FAQs

Do you need to peel delicata squash?

No. The thin skin is edible and softens during roasting. Wash the squash thoroughly before slicing.

Can you roast delicata squash without oil?

Yes. This recipe uses a balsamic-mustard marinade to coat the squash without oil. Expect tender squash with caramelized edges; a drizzle of oil helps create a more golden, crisp finish.

Why is my delicata squash sticking?

The sugars in the squash and marinade can stick as they caramelize. Use high-quality nonstick parchment paper or a silicone baking mat, and gently loosen the pieces with a thin spatula when flipping.

Do you need to flip delicata squash while roasting?

It's not required but flipping halfway through the cooking time helps both sides cook evenly.

Oil-Free Roasted Delicata Squash Recipe

This oil-free Crispy Roasted Delicata Squash has a sweet, tangy balsamic glaze and caramelized edges. No peeling required! An easy side for weeknight dinners, grain bowls, or holiday meals. Course Side Dish Cuisine Vegan Diet Vegan, Vegetarian Prep Time 10 minutes minutes Cook Time 30 minutes minutes Total Time 40 minutes minutes Servings 4 servings Calories 93kcal Author Nisha Melvani

Equipment

  • Large baking sheet nonstick or parchment-lined
  • Wide bowl
  • Sharp knife

Ingredients

  • 2 small delicata squash or 1 medium
  • 2 tablespoons balsamic vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon fresh lemon juice
  • 2 teaspoons Dijon mustard or stone-ground mustard
  • 1 tablespoon water
  • 1 teaspoon maple syrup optional, for extra caramelization
  • ½ teaspoon garlic powder
  • ½ teaspoon smoked paprika
  • Dash of salt (optional) use low-sodium or omit as desired
  • Freshly ground black pepper to taste

Instructions

  • Preheat the oven to 360°F. Line a large baking sheet with high-quality nonstick parchment paper or a silicone baking mat.
  • Prepare the squash: Wash and dry the squash thoroughly. Trim both ends, halve lengthwise, and scoop out the seeds. Place each half cut-side down and slice crosswise into even ½-inch half-moons. No peeling required.
  • Whisk the marinade: In a large bowl, whisk the balsamic vinegar, lemon juice, mustard, water, maple syrup, garlic powder, smoked paprika, salt (if using), and black pepper until smooth.
  • Marinate the squash. Add the squash and toss thoroughly to coat both sides. Optional: Add a small drizzle of olive or avocado oil and toss again.
  • Arrange in a single layer without overlapping, using two baking sheets if needed.
  • Roast for 20 minutes, then brush with any remaining marinade and gently flip. Bake for about 10 minutes more, or until fork-tender and caramelized around the edges. Check near the end, as the balsamic glaze can darken quickly.
  • Serve warm or chilled. Serve as a vegetable side, or add to salads or grain bowls.

Notes

  • For oil-free roasting, the moderate temperature of 360°F gives the squash time to soften while helping prevent the balsamic glaze from burning.
  • Dry the squash well before tossing so the marinade isn't diluted.
  • Use a nonstick liner-the sugars in the marinade can stick as they caramelize.
  • Leave space between the slices for better browning.
  • The oil-free version will be tender with caramelized edges. Adding a little oil encourages a more golden, crisp finish.

Publisher’s Platform: Twenty-two years of asking whether we should ban petting zoos: After Brookshire Farms, Bengtson’s and the State Fair of Texas, it is time I answered my own question

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I have been asking the same question in print since 2004. In an op-ed called “E. coli and the Fair,” I asked, “So what do we do? Banish state and county fairs? Eliminate Petting Zoos?” and answered myself in three words: “Of course not.” In 2012 the headline was “Ban Petting Zoos?” In 2015, after a toddler died following a small-town Maine fair, it was “As I have said before – is it time to ban petting zoos?” In 2019, after a two-year-old died following the San Diego County Fair, it was “Seriously, time to ban petting zoos?”

A question asked four times over twenty-two years is not really a question anymore. It is a way of not answering. This week three health departments in three states opened investigations into E. coli infections tied to animal attractions at fall events. So here is my answer.

This week, in three states

At Bengtson’s Pumpkin Farm in Homer Glen, Illinois, five people with confirmed Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC), ages 4 to 38, four of them hospitalized. The petting zoo closed on October 23; the rest of the farm stayed open, and more than 200 visitors have filled out the county’s symptom survey. At Brookshire Farms in San Luis Obispo, California, five confirmed cases and an exposure window of October 16 through 26; the farm closed itself down. And in Dallas, the county is investigating up to seven possible cases in children, some treated in intensive care, and whether they trace to the petting zoo at the State Fair of Texas. The county is not yet calling it an outbreak.

I laid out what we know about each investigation yesterday, and this morning I posted twenty-five years of outbreaks just like them and the prevention playbook that everyone already has. This post is about something else: what I have been telling fairs, legislators and parents for two decades, what came of it, and what I think we should do now.

Read in a row, those posts tell a story I am not especially proud of. I have been right about the risk for twenty-two years and mostly wrong about how much signs and guidelines would fix it.

What two decades taught me

Signs alone have never been enough – but the right sign helps. Every post on that list asked for signs. The State Fair of Texas’s old Children’s Barnyard relied on hand-sanitizing gel and signs before the fair scrapped it in the mid-2000s. A sign that says “Please wash your hands” is wallpaper. The Compendium found that awareness of the risk actually protected visitors at the 2004 North Carolina State Fair. The lesson is that a sign has to tell parents what is at stake, in plain words and in the languages families at that fair speak: animals in this area can carry E. coli, it can cause kidney failure in young children, and soap and water – not sanitizer – is how you protect them.

Guidelines are not requirements. The CDC issued recommendations in 2001. State public health veterinarians have published the Compendium since 2005. As I wrote this morning, there is still no federal law on disease risk where animals and the public mix, and most states rely on voluntary guidance. Twenty-five years of outbreaks is the result of voluntary.

Laws work only as well as their weakest line. North Carolina did the most after its 2004 outbreak – Aedin’s Law, a permit and inspection for every animal exhibition at a sanctioned fair, and detailed rules that put soap-and-water stations within ten feet of exhibit exits and kept sanitizer dispensers out of the transition areas. It still had outbreaks in 2011 and 2012. Washington’s rule lets operators substitute an “alternative hand sanitizing method” approved by the local health officer. That is the loophole the Arizona State Fair walked through in 2025, and the Big Fresno Fair walked through in 2005.

The market is already banning petting zoos – one sick child at a time. Texas scrapped its fair petting zoo when insurers raised rates. By 2015, North Carolina Health News was reporting that petting zoos were becoming a thing of the past at the N.C. State Fair. After last year, the Arizona State Fair dropped its petting zoo for 2026 and the Maricopa County Fair dropped direct animal contact. Each of those decisions came after children were hurt. A ban by lawsuit and insurance premium is the most expensive, least predictable ban there is.

I was wrong about testing. In 2004 and again in 2015 I suggested testing animals before they arrive. The veterinarians have persuaded me that a negative test means little: these animals shed intermittently, and in San Diego in 2019 every animal and environmental sample came back negative after a child had died. Prevention has to assume every goat, calf and lamb is positive.

So – should we ban petting zoos?

Not all of them. I said in 2015 that petting zoos were not going to be banned and perhaps should not be, and I still believe a child who learns where food comes from by standing next to a cow is better off for it. But “not all of them” is not “none of them.” Here are the bans I now support:

1.     No direct contact for children under five with the highest-risk animals. Preweaned calves, lambs and goat kids, and animals that have recently given birth, are exactly what draws toddlers and exactly what sheds the most. The 2023 Compendium already says they are not appropriate where high-risk groups are expected. Children under five can see them over a double barrier. They should not be hand-feeding them.

2.    No animal contact anywhere that cannot provide soap and running water at the exit. No sanitizer-only petting zoos. If a pumpkin patch, a festival or a traveling operator cannot plumb a sink, the exhibit is look-don’t-touch.

3.    No permit, no petting zoo. Every operator, every location, every season – including traveling operators who work several fairs a year. The same operator name turned up at the Big Fresno Fair in 2005 and the Arizona State Fair in 2025.

The Petting Zoo Preservation Act – the short version

In 2015 I promised a Model Act. Here it is in outline, built from Pennsylvania’s law, North Carolina’s Aedin’s Law and its rules, Washington’s rule, the Arizona Public Health Association’s proposal after last year’s outbreak, and the 2023 Compendium:

Even for Desperate Restaurants, Fake Reviews Just Aren&#8217;t Worth It

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Yelp was first launched in 2004, it flopped. The company’s initial model of an email-based referral service failed to take off until its creators realized users were taking the platform into their own hands; in lieu of sending one another recommendations via email chain, they began using a “hidden” link at the bottom of the startup’s website to write unsolicited reviews of everything from doctors to restaurants. Yelp 2.0 launched in 2005, and gave those reviews a more visible place to live online — one that emboldened users to feel more authentic stewardship, by way of direct authorship, over hyping up the businesses they enjoyed (or didn’t). The startup bought a whopping 20-million-business database, and amplified a world of reviews outside of the restaurant- critic echo chamber. It was a fresh democratization of information, which was itself one of the more optimistic legacies of the Y2K internet. 

It’s been over 20 years since Yelp was founded. At its best, the platform still feels like a place where the voices of hungry, curious diners can share their personal experiences and offer recommendations and tips; at its worst, it feels like a bloated repository for reviews filled with misdirected anger, unreasonable expectations, or else completely fake customer reviews. Initially, unsubstantiated and unreliable reviews were more often written by a person — either someone from the business itself, or paid by the business; now, a fake review can be penned by a paid influencer, a detailed generative AI prompt, or purchased from third-party agencies that will produce fake bot reviews in bulk.

Fake reviews were perhaps inevitable. Bot-driven comments became ubiquitous during an overripe moment in the untamed internet’s lifespan. But how does a restaurant shamed for gamifying its reviews — even if it was, presumably, out of desperation in a tough economic landscape — regain customers’ broken trust? 

… The offending restaurant’s foot traffic went down by 8 percent, and dipped even lower around the holidays, a time when would-be diners rely even more on Yelp.

In 2026’s AI-addled digital ecosystem, researchers are starting to understand even more about how paid-for, planted reviews impact the restaurants behind them, and whether trying to gamify platforms to achieve high ratings is worth it at all for businesses. A recently published working paper on the consequences of restaurants flagged by Yelp for fake (planted, incentivized) reviews found that since 2023, more than 4,900 businesses have been flagged with Yelp’s “Consumer Alert” banner, which exists on the penalized business’ page for a 90-day period and lets Yelp users know that the business has been caught peddling fake reviews. In 2020, the fast-casual chain Burgerim was found guilty of planting fake reviews, and received over 200 digital warning banners across its various locations’ Yelp pages.

The impacts of that digital pillory extend far beyond its 90-day duration. Yi Cao is an assistant professor of accounting at George Mason University, and the co-author of the paper; almost immediately, Cao noticed the offending restaurant’s foot traffic went down by 8 percent, and dipped even lower around the holidays, a time when would-be diners rely even more on Yelp. Cao’s team cross-analyzed the business’ performance on Yelp with real-life foot traffic reports (from global location data firm SafeGraph) and credit card transaction data (courtesy of Consumer Edge), and discovered that most of the penalized restaurants struggled to recover to pre-alert traffic even after a year and a half. The stink of chicanery lingered. 

While Cao says his team doesn’t know every signal that Yelp uses to identify fake reviews, they do know that it relies “substantially on reports from consumers and business owners,” and that “they also have an algorithm running in the background to screen reviews for AI-generated language — which is a sign of possible review inflation.” At this point, the cost of planting a good review is only getting steeper as the technology capable of detecting it improves. “This is a classical theory of a trade-off between the benefits of manipulating reputation and the risks of getting caught,” Cao explains, “When the risk is relatively low, the business owners decide the incremental benefits are worth the risk.”

Yelp’s real restaurant reviews don’t necessarily need to be etched in stone, but as AI-penned reviews become less detectable and more sophisticated, what diners want now, more than ever, is a human touch and a sense of authenticity.

The Coating Fix That Makes This Recipe Work

I still vividly remember developing this baked fried chicken recipe. We were newly married and didn’t have a lot of money to eat out, and I wanted crispy, restaurant-worthy chicken, but at home and somewhat healthier. This seasoned and breaded chicken that’s oven-baked until crispy came to life, and I’ve been making it ever since. It’s one of my husband’s favorite recipes to this day, and now it’s a favorite for our two boys too.

Almost every fried chicken recipe you’ll find fries the chicken in oil. This one bakes on a thin layer of melted butter instead. The butter crisps the bottom of the crust while the oven browns the top, so you get that crunchy shell without the splatter, the oil thermometer, or the leftover pot of grease. If you’d rather use your air fryer, Air Fryer Fried Chicken gets a crunchy cornflake crust with no oil bath either.

I soak the chicken in buttermilk first, which is a common step in Southern fried chicken. Its mild acidity helps tenderize the meat so it stays juicy. Some recipes call for an overnight soak, but 20 minutes is enough for this quick version. You can absolutely go longer if you plan ahead.

After hearing from some of you who had trouble with the breading falling off, I retested the recipe to find a simple, reliable way to help the coating stick. Looking closely at your feedback, one pattern stood out. The pieces that kept their breading were the ones that went into the flour still damp with buttermilk. That buttermilk is the glue, and drying it off is what makes the coating slide away at the flip. So the recipe now leaves the chicken damp, gives the coating a short rest to set, and tells you exactly how to flip it.

Why Did My Breading Fall Off?

This is the most common question on this recipe, and the fix comes down to three small moments.

  • Before you dredge. If you blot the chicken dry, the flour has nothing to stick to. Let the extra buttermilk drip off for a few seconds, but leave the surface damp. That thin wet layer turns into the paste that holds the crust on.
  • After you dredge. Flour that goes straight into the oven stays dusty and flakes off. Let the coated chicken sit for 10 minutes. You’ll see the flour darken and turn paste-like, and that’s your sign it’s ready to bake.
  • At the flip. If the crust sticks to the pan, it isn’t ready yet. Hot butter helps the bottom set quickly, so heat the butter in the pan while the oven preheats. Then use a thin metal spatula and slide it all the way under each piece before you turn it. A fork or tongs will pull the coating right off.

Tips for Beginners

  • Leave the chicken damp, not dry. This is the step that matters most. A damp surface helps the seasoned flour grab on and bake into one solid crust. If the chicken is too dry, the coating won’t stick, and you’ll lose it the moment you flip.
  • Give the coating time to set. Ten minutes on a plate after dredging makes a big difference. The flour soaks up a little buttermilk and turns into a paste, so it bakes into a crunchy shell instead of a powdery layer that falls away.
  • Trust a thermometer, not the clock. Thin cutlets can cook through before the crust looks fully golden, and thicker ones can need a few extra minutes. The chicken is done when the thickest part reads 165°F. Pull it then, and it stays juicy.

Baked Fried Chicken

This oven-fried chicken breast bakes up perfectly crispy on the outside, tender inside, and is an easy choice for quick, delicious dinners! Course Main Course Cuisine American Difficulty Easy Prep Time 25 minutes minutes Cook Time 40 minutes minutes Total Time 1 hour hour 5 minutes minutes Servings 4 servings Calories 402kcal Author Becky Hardin

Equipment

  • Baking Sheet

Ingredients

  • 2 boneless, skinless chicken breasts
  • 3 cups buttermilk
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tbsp ground paprika
  • 1 tbsp all-purpose seasoning such as Lawry's
  • 1 tsp kosher salt
  • 1 tsp ground black pepper
  • 4 tbsp unsalted butter melted (½ stick)
  • ¼ cup fresh flat leaf parsley optional

Instructions

  • Cut the chicken breasts in half, lengthwise, to create 4 thinner, even portions.
  • Place chicken in a large bowl and marinate in the buttermilk for at least 20 minutes, or up to overnight in the fridge.
  • Mix together the flour, paprika, all-purpose seasoning, salt, and pepper in a medium-sized bowl. Set aside.
  • Preheat oven to 400ºF. Place parchment paper on a 9×13-inch baking sheet, add the butter, and set the pan in the oven until the butter melts and bubbles. Tilt to coat the bottom of the pan.
  • Lift each piece of chicken from the buttermilk and let the excess drip off, but do not pat it dry. Press both sides firmly into the flour mixture and set on a plate.
  • Let the coated chicken rest for 10 minutes, until the flour looks damp and paste-like.
  • Place the chicken in the hot butter, making sure there is a small amount of room between each piece. This will allow them to crisp while baking.
  • Bake for 20 minutes. Flip each piece with a thin metal spatula, sliding it fully under the crust. Bake 15-20 minutes more, until golden brown and the thickest part reaches 165°F.
  • Let rest 5 minutes, then transfer to a serving dish. Garnish with parsley if desired. Enjoy!

Notes

  • The buttermilk left on the chicken is what holds the coating in place, so let the excess drip off but don’t blot it dry. A dry surface gives the flour nothing to grab, and that’s when the breading slides off in the pan.
  • Flour that goes straight into the oven tends to flake off at the flip, so rest the coated chicken for 10 minutes before baking. That short rest turns the flour into a paste that bakes into one crunchy crust.
  • A crust that sticks to the pan usually isn’t ready to turn yet. Slide a thin metal spatula fully under each piece before flipping, and if it resists, give it a few more minutes. Heating the butter in the pan first also helps the bottom set faster.
  • Thin cutlets can finish cooking before the crust looks done, so check for 165°F in the thickest part instead of going by color alone. Pulling the chicken right at 165°F keeps it from drying out.
  • All-purpose seasoning is a savory blend of garlic, onion, paprika, parsley, salt, and pepper, like Lawry’s. If you can’t find it, use 1 tsp each of garlic powder, onion powder, and dried parsley instead. If your store-bought blend is mostly salt, cut the kosher salt in half to keep the sodium in check.
  • To make your own buttermilk, stir 3 tbsp of lemon juice or white vinegar into just under 3 cups of milk and let it sit for 5 minutes. It thickens slightly and works the same way in the soak.
  • Thighs, drumsticks, and wings all work with this coating. Bone-in pieces need more time, so bake until the thickest part reaches 165°F, which usually takes 45-50 minutes for thighs and drumsticks.
  • Leftovers keep in an airtight container in the fridge for up to 3 days and in the freezer for up to 3 months. To keep the crust crisp, reheat in the oven or air fryer instead of the microwave, which turns the breading soft.

How to Make Oven-Fried Chicken Breasts Step by Step

Gather all the ingredients together.

Prepare the chicken: Cut 2 boneless, skinless chicken breasts in half lengthwise so you get 4 even, thinner portions about ½ inch thick.

Soak in buttermilk: Place the chicken in a large bowl and pour 3 cups of buttermilk over it. Marinate for at least 20 minutes, or cover and refrigerate up to overnight.

Mix the coating: In a shallow bowl, whisk together 1 cup of all-purpose flour, 1 tbsp of ground paprika, 1 tbsp of all-purpose seasoning, 1 tsp of kosher salt, and 1 tsp of ground black pepper. The mixture should look evenly rusty orange with no streaks of plain flour.

Heat the butter: Preheat the oven to 400°F. Line a 9×13-inch baking sheet with parchment paper, add 4 tbsp of unsalted butter, and set the pan in the oven while it heats. Once the butter melts and starts to bubble, tilt the pan so it coats the whole surface.

Bread the chicken: Lift each piece from the buttermilk and let the excess drip off for a few seconds, but don’t pat it dry. Press it firmly into the flour mixture on both sides, then set it on a plate.

Let the coating set: Rest the coated chicken for 10 minutes. The flour should look damp and paste-like with almost no dry white spots. If you see a dry patch, press on a little more flour.

Bake: Carefully place the chicken in the hot butter, leaving a little space between each piece so it can crisp instead of steam. You should hear a soft sizzle. Bake for 20 minutes, until the bottom is golden and releases easily from the pan. Slide a thin metal spatula all the way under each piece and turn it over in one motion. If a piece sticks, give it 2-3 more minutes and try again. Bake for another 15-20 minutes, until the crust is deep golden and crunchy and the thickest part reads 165°F.

Garnish and serve: Transfer the chicken to a serving dish and let it rest for a few minutes. Sprinkle with ¼ cup of chopped fresh flat-leaf parsley, if you like, and enjoy!

How to Store Baked Fried Chicken

Store leftovers in an airtight container or resealable bag, and keep them in the refrigerator for up to 3 days. I recommend wrapping each piece in foil or layering pieces with paper towels to soak up any moisture that might collect in the fridge. This helps keep the breading nice and crispy.

Freezer

To freeze, wrap each piece of chicken in foil, then place the pieces in a freezer-safe, resealable bag. Freeze for up to 3 months. Thaw in the fridge overnight before reheating. The crust won’t be quite as crunchy as the day you made it, but a hot oven brings most of the crispness back.

Reheating

  • Oven. This is my favorite way. Place the chicken on a baking sheet and reheat at 350°F for about 15 minutes, until it’s hot all the way through.
  • Air fryer. Reheat at 350°F for 5 to 7 minutes, flipping once. This is the fastest way to get the crust crunchy again.
  • Microwave. It works in a pinch, but the breading will turn soft. Use it only if you’re slicing the chicken for a wrap or salad.

Make Ahead

You can soak the chicken in buttermilk up to 24 hours ahead and keep it covered in the fridge. Coat it right before baking, since the flour gets gummy if the breaded chicken sits too long. You can also mix the seasoned flour a few days ahead and store it in an airtight container.

What to Serve With Baked Fried Chicken

This easy chicken breast recipe goes with any and all of your favorite side dishes, but when I’m in the mood for a classic fried chicken dinner, I go all in.

My go-to is a big bowl of Easy Mashed Potatoes. I boil the potatoes in milk, so they come out extra creamy, and that soft, buttery texture is the perfect match for the crunchy crust. I start the potatoes right after the chicken goes into the oven, and everything is ready at the same time.

How I Build Big Flavor in Less Time

Traditional ham and bean soups often start with dried beans and a ham bone that simmer together for hours. And there’s definitely a time and place for that! But I wanted an easy version that didn’t require soaking beans or spending all afternoon waiting for dinner. So I use canned cannellini beans and fully cooked ham to get a head start. They’re already cooked, which means I can spend my time building flavor instead.

One thing I do differently is brown the ham before adding anything else. This gives the edges a little color and leaves behind tasty browned bits on the bottom of the pot (called fond). When I add the onion, carrots, celery, and garlic, their natural moisture loosens those bits and brings that extra flavor into the soup. Sautéing the vegetables also softens them and brings out a little natural sweetness. For the broth, I borrowed a trick from my white bean soup and purée one can of beans with its liquid. This thickens the soup and adds a lovely creaminess without flour, cornstarch, or heavy cream. I leave the other two cans whole for a chunky texture, and after the initial flavor-building stages, everything comes together with just 15 minutes of simmering!

Recipe Success Tips

  1. Give the ham a chance to brown. I cook the diced ham in oil over medium heat for 3-5 minutes, until the edges are lightly browned and golden-brown bits form on the bottom of the pot. This builds the salty ham flavor you’d normally get from simmering a ham bone for hours, just in much less time! If those bits start turning very dark, lower the heat so they don’t burn.
  2. Blend one can of beans, liquid and all! Purée ONE can of cannellini beans with its canning liquid, then drain the other two. The smooth beans thicken the broth, and the whole beans give the soup its chunky texture. No blender? You can thoroughly mash that first can with a potato masher or fork instead. It won’t be as smooth, but this is a chunky soup anyway!
  3. Let the soup boil and thicken. The 15-minute boil allows some of the broth to evaporate while the puréed beans thicken the soup. I give the pot a good stir every few minutes, making sure to scrape along the bottom, to keep the beans from sticking. If it gets thicker than you’d like, stir in a little more broth or water until you reach your preferred consistency.
  4. Taste before adding salt. Cooked ham is pretty salty, especially when it’s cured, and chicken broth adds even more seasoning. That’s why I wait until the ham goes back into the pot before tasting and adjusting. I didn’t need any extra salt in my batch, but I finished each bowl with a little extra black pepper.

What Kind of Ham Can I Use?

For this quick ham and bean soup, I recommend fully cooked ham (or leftover cooked ham from another meal) rather than a ham hock. We’re using canned beans and other ingredients that cook quickly, so there’s no need to spend hours simmering a ham bone to flavor the broth! Thicker pieces work best because they hold their shape and develop nicely browned edges when sautéed.

Boneless vs. Bone-In Ham Steaks

Both of these ham steaks are fully cooked and work well in this recipe:

  • Boneless ham steak: My easiest choice! There’s no bone to work around, and the thick slices are easy to cut into bite-sized pieces. You can buy ham steaks in smaller packs (shown above) or use a package of fully cooked, boneless, sliced ham (as I did in the recipe below).
  • Bone-in ham steak: This works just as well once you’ve removed the bone and any tough rind. Just remember that you need 1 lb. of usable ham, so you’ll need to buy a little extra to account for the weight of the bone.

What About Other Types of Ham?

I saw a LOT of ham options at the store when shopping for this recipe, and you probably will too, especially around the holidays. The fully cooked, boneless spiral-cut ham and smoked shank portion ham shown above can both work, BUT I’d save those bigger hams for a holiday centerpiece (like our baked ham) and use the leftovers for this soup.

I wouldn’t splurge on a big ham for this recipe unless you plan to use it for several meals. Ham slices are cheaper, easier, and all you need for this quickie soup.

Ham hocks are a different story! They’re better suited to traditional bean soups that simmer for hours. The long simmer gives the connective tissue time to soften and the smoky flavor time to develop. How to Make Ham and Bean Soup Step-by-Step Photos

Gather all of your ingredients: This is the type of ham I used (honey cured boneless ham). You can use any cooked ham, but a thicker slice works better than thin sandwich slices. You can also use cooked holiday ham. You’ll need 1 lb. of cooked ham.

Prep the veggies: Dice 1 yellow onion, peel and slice 3 carrots, slice 3 celery ribs, and mince 2 cloves of garlic. I recommend keeping the vegetables fairly uniform in size so they soften evenly during the short cooking time.

Brown the ham: Start by dicing 1 lb. of fully cooked ham into bite-sized pieces.

Add the ham and 1 Tbsp cooking oil to a large soup pot or Dutch oven. Sauté over medium heat for 3-5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the edges turn golden brown. You should also see some browned bits developing on the bottom of the pot. Keep an eye on the heat so they don’t burn. Remove the ham to a clean bowl and leave those browned bits behind.

Sauté the vegetables: Add the prepared onion, carrots, celery, and garlic to the same pot. Sauté over medium heat for about 5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the onions look translucent and the vegetables begin to soften. They’ll release some moisture as they cook, so use your spoon to gently scrape up the browned bits left by the ham.

Purée one can of beans: As the vegetables cook, add one 15.5 oz. can of cannellini beans to a blender, including all the liquid from the can. Blend until smooth and creamy. This mixture will help thicken the soup without adding cream!

Drain the other two cans: Now drain the remaining two cans of beans, but don’t purée them. We want those beans to stay whole for texture.

This is what the veggies should look like after sautéing for about 5 minutes. The bottom of the pot should also look noticeably cleaner as those bits dissolve into the vegetables.

Add the beans and seasonings: Add the puréed beans and both cans of drained beans to the pot with the softened vegetables. Sprinkle in ¼ tsp dried thyme and ¼ tsp freshly cracked black pepper. Stir gently until the creamy bean mixture coats the vegetables and whole beans. Try not to mash the whole beans as you stir.

Pour in the broth: Add 2 cups chicken broth and stir until everything is combined. The soup will look fairly watery at this point, but that’s normal! The puréed beans will give the broth more body as the liquid reduces during cooking.

Increase the heat to medium-high and bring the soup to a boil. Reduce the heat to medium and let it continue boiling for 15 minutes, stirring every few minutes to prevent the beans from sticking to the bottom of the pot. The broth should gradually reduce and become thicker and creamier. The vegetables should also be tender when pierced with a fork. If the soup gets too thick, add a splash of chicken broth or water until it reaches your preferred consistency.

Add the ham and serve: Once the soup has thickened, stir the browned ham back into the pot to warm through.

Give everything a taste before adding any salt, as the ham and chicken broth already contribute quite a bit! I didn’t need any extra salt in my batch, but I did add a little freshly cracked black pepper to each bowl. Serve hot and enjoy!

Storage & Reheating

This ham and bean soup makes great leftovers. Let it cool quickly, then transfer it to airtight containers and refrigerate within 2 hours. It’ll keep in the refrigerator for 3-4 days. The soup will naturally thicken as it sits because the beans will keep absorbing moisture.

Reheating

I like to reheat this soup on the stovetop over medium heat, stirring occasionally until it comes to a boil and is heated all the way through (165°F). Add a splash of chicken broth or water as it warms to loosen the consistency. You can also reheat individual servings in the microwave, stirring partway through so they heat evenly.

Freezing

This soup freezes really well, too! Once cooled, divide it into individual portions in freezer-safe containers or bags. I prefer to divide my soup into single servings before freezing, so they can be reheated as needed and in the amount needed. Quart-sized freezer bags are great for freezing soup, as are the small blue-top Ziploc food storage containers. Freeze for up to 3 months for the best quality.
