Third Utah restaurant continues the brand’s growth, opening in 2027

Sandy, UT  (RestaurantNews.com)  Rodizio Grill, America’s First Brazilian Steakhouse, today announced plans to open its third Utah restaurant in Ogden at 2309 Washington Blvd. in 2027. The new location will bring Rodizio Grill’s signature churrasco experience to Northern Utah – rotisserie-grilled meats carved tableside by Rodizio Gauchos, a fresh gourmet salad bar, a Brazilian hot side bar and the warm, welcoming hospitality for which Rodizio is known.

“In 1996, we opened the first Brazilian steakhouse in the United States. When we came to Utah three years later, we were introducing many guests to Brazilian churrasco for the very first time,” said Ivan Utrera, founder of Rodizio Grill. “Utah welcomed us like family, and we have never taken that for granted. Ogden gives us the chance to share our heritage with a new community – the food, the music and the alegria of a Brazilian experience. I couldn’t be prouder to see this next chapter begin.”

“Ogden will be our third Utah restaurant, with a fourth already in the works, and it reflects the momentum behind our brand,” said Steven Staples, CEO of Rodizio Grill. “Utah is home for Rodizio Grill. Our headquarters is here, and Utah guests have embraced us for 27 years. Ogden’s strong sense of community makes it a natural next step. We’re focused on thoughtful growth, bringing the Rodizio experience to communities that are ready to celebrate with us.”

Leading up to opening day, Rodizio Grill will share its “Road to Rodizio” countdown with the Ogden community, including sneak peeks, behind-the-scenes moments and grand opening celebrations. Guests can join Club Rodizio at Rodizio.com to receive opening updates, invitations and exclusive offers – plus a free birthday meal once the Ogden location opens.

More details on the grand opening will be announced in the coming months.

About Rodizio Grill

Established in 1995, Rodizio Grill® The Brazilian Steakhouse is the first authentic Brazilian steakhouse in the U.S., founded by Ivan Utrera, who brought the popular Brazilian churrascaria concept and cherished family recipes to the United States from his home country of Brazil. Rodizio Grill’s all-inclusive menu offers unlimited Brazilian sides, more than 30 gourmet salads, and rotisserie-grilled meats and grilled items carved tableside by Rodizio Gauchos. Newport Beach, CA  (RestaurantNews.com)  Chipotle Mexican Grill (NYSE: CMG) today announced it is bringing back Smoked Brisket to restaurants across the U.S. and Canada beginning November 5. The limited-time protein, which has been known to disappear from menus quickly, returns this fall for football weekends, rivalry games and watch parties. Hours of smoking over mesquite wood underscore the craftsmanship behind the returning fan favorite.

“We’re bringing guests a responsibly sourced brisket that delivers big flavor and lives up to our industry-leading Food with Integrity standards,” said Stephanie Perdue, Senior Vice President, Brand Marketing. “That care starts at the source, with beef raised to our standards, and carries through every step of the cooking process – from the smoke to the sear to the final sauce. You can taste that commitment in every bite. Bringing it back in the heart of football season gives fans one more reason to make Chipotle part of their game-day lineup.”

Real Ingredients, Real Smoked Brisket

Chipotle’s Smoked Brisket starts with Responsibly Raised® beef. The brand estimates that roughly 5% of U.S. beef meets its animal welfare and quality standards, which require cattle to be raised on a strictly vegetarian diet and without added growth hormones or routine antibiotics, with traceability back to the ranch of origin.

The beef is seasoned with spices including smoked serrano and chipotle chili peppers, then smoked over mesquite wood to develop its deep, smoky flavor. Once in restaurants, team members sear the brisket on the plancha, finish it with spices, toss it in a savory sauce made with smoky chili peppers and hand-chop it in small batches throughout the day.

“Our Smoked Brisket is defined by the classic culinary techniques that go into every batch,” said Nevielle Panthaky, Senior Vice President, Culinary. “A signature blend of spices and a low-and-slow smoking process give the brisket its bold, beefy, smoky character, while the plancha sear and savory-tangy sauce create a tender bite with a little kick that’s uniquely Chipotle.”

A Game-Day Go-To

For a game-day entrée, Panthaky recommends a burrito bowl with white rice, pinto beans, Smoked Brisket, fresh tomato salsa, light tomatillo-red chili salsa, cheese and Queso Blanco. The bowl delivers 45 grams of protein and 11 grams of fiber, pairing the rich, smoky brisket with creamy Queso Blanco and a touch of heat.

Brisket Fans Take Over the Kitchen

To celebrate the return of Smoked Brisket, Chipotle invited superfans into its restaurant kitchens to experience firsthand how the protein is prepared. The group includes food creators Max Greb (Max the Meat Guy) and Newton Nguyen (Newt), along with reality star and longtime Chipotle fan Carl Schmidt.

Building on the brand’s 100 Creators campaign that launched in August, the new creative, developed with agency partner KIDS, follows Smoked Brisket fans as they join Chipotle team members in the kitchen to prepare the protein and capture the experience from their own perspectives.

Review: Pienaar & Son Tiny Batch Medium Char

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

South African whisky has come a long way in recent years, and Pienaar and Son’s Tiny Batch is one of the bottles that has helped put local whisky on the radar, particularly in the main market with fun gifting campaigns and great social media work.

Medium Char is part of a two-part release (the other being Heavy Char) both single grain, single cask whiskies matured in virgin American oak and bottled at 43% abv. Each cask only produces around 550 bottles, so I would have personally preferred to see “Single Cask” on the label, as there isn’t much “batch” about it.

Pienaar & Son Tiny Batch Medium Char Review

The nose is sweet and fairly delicate. Vanilla, honey, and fresh oak come through first, followed by caramel and a distinct grassy note. There’s a touch of coconut and white chocolate. It’s pleasant, although the oak is definitely doing a lot of the talking.

The palate is soft, sweet, and easy-drinking. Again, vanilla, caramel, and toasted oak lead the way, later being met with baking spice and a little pepper. There’s a nice creamy quality to it, but is overall pretty straightforward. At 43% abv, there’s no heat to speak of, although I’d have liked a little more weight and concentration, however I understand the decision to keep the abv on the more accessible side for this one.

The finish is medium-short, with more vanilla and spice. It’s clean and pleasant, but doesn’t exactly hang around.

Overall, I like what Pienaar & Son are doing here, and I think Tiny Batch is an important whisky for South Africa. I can’t say it’s a bottle I’ll show off in my collection, but I don’t think that’s it’s purpose. Rather, it is a very approachable young whisky with a clear identity. The virgin oak gives it plenty of vanilla and sweetness, while the medium char keeps things from getting too heavy. It’s uncomplicated, and while I would love to see what more age and other casks can bring here, it’s clear that the distillers know what they’re doing – they’ve produced a clean and well-made spirit.

For someone wanting to explore South African whisky, it’s a good place to start. For a serious whisky nerd looking for layers of depth, I’d probably reach for something else.

The Heavy Char is an expression I’m excited to compare side-by-side with this – I’m keen to see what some additional oak influence could contribute to the spirit.

86 proof.

B / $51

Dim sum translates, with poetic license to “touch the heart.” Indeed, a brunch-hour parade of Cantonese comfort snacks will make hearts young and old become “a flailing fawn,” the expression for hearts going pitter-patter in the dim sum heartland of Guangzhou. In the U.S., dumplings and buns tend to dominate, but they’re only part of the appeal; turnip cake, bone-in spareribs, and gelatinous chicken feet bring a range of textures and tastes that can further liven up the table.

Though tea is the typical pairing, wine has a way of enhancing the social aspect of dim sum while adding layers of flavor to the sprawling spread. If the restaurant allows, bring a few bottles to find your own mix-and-match favorites.

Har Gow (Shrimp)

The best har gow dumplings are stuffed with shrimp and only lightly seasoned to bring out the shrimp’s intrinsic sweetness. “Shellfish wines” like Muscadet, Albariño, and Chablis can work here, but try Carricante from Sicily. Its high acidity, notes of citrus zest and blossom, and a flinty or sea-spray minerality are a slam dunk with all kinds of seafood-based dim sum (like shrimp siu mai, scallop dumplings, and crab-filled soup dumplings).

Tornatore 2024 Carricante (Etna)

An herbaceous, floral nose unfurls aromas of lemongrass, pine needles, wild thyme and honeysuckle. The lemon expands on the palate, before the herbs and forest floor elements return to create a full, round sensation. 93 Points — Danielle Callegari

Inquire with the importer

Char Siu Bao (Pork)

These baked or steamed buns with BBQ pork in an unctuously sweet and savory sauce are often paired with pu-erh, a fermented and aged dark black tea. For wine, Mencía from Galicia is lighter and fresher than the more common Mencía from Bierzo, an intriguing combination of fine tannins, tart red fruit, and stony minerality on a lightweight, almost ethereal, frame. Like the char siu filling, it hints at sweet and savory with a touch of star anise. 

Alvaredos-Hobbs 2022 Mencía (Ribeira Sacra)

Inviting aromas of strawberry-rhubarb, dark chocolate and sagebrush open on the nose along with blackberry jam and hibiscus. The supple palate acknowledges svelte tannins that swoon on the finish like a boy band finishing their final ballad. Decant and drink now, or enjoy from 2027 onwards. Cellar Selection. 94 Points — Reggie Solomon

$40 Vine Republic

Potstickers (Vegetable)

Stuffed with greens, cabbage, mushrooms, and/or chives, crisp-bottomed veggie potstickers can be even more complex than their meaty counterparts, with underlying notes of ginger, garlic, and Shaoxing wine. All these pungent notes can be tricky to pair with wine, but dry Furmint brings its own complementary notes of citrus, ginger, smoke, and green chile, with light body and brisk, cleansing acidity. It has personality without obscuring the dumplings themselves.

Tempos Vega Sicilia 2021 Oremus Mandolás Dry Furmint (Tokaji)

White peach and cantaloupe flavors complement the tart lemon juice on the palate of this nuanced dry Furmint, which also has delicate floral and complex minerally notes. The finish is long and will have you reaching for another sip immediately if not sooner. 92 Points — Emily Saladino

$38 Wine.com

Rice Rolls (Beef)

A textural wonder, these slick, slurpy rolls are filled with chewy, richly seasoned beef; the two elements seem to call for opposing wines. Grignolino, a Piemontese grape with a growing presence in California, produces very light wines with surprisingly firm tannins. It can show flavors that play well with Chinese food: cherry, hibiscus, goji berry, roses, white pepper, and clove. It’s a good red option for all sorts of Cantonese dishes.

Braida di Giacomo Bologna 2023 Limonte (Grignolino d’Asti)

Grignolio, it is in the name, you cannot help but grin from this wine. Opening with aromas of drying raspberries, strawberries and cranberries that merge with notes of sage, thyme, and dusty earth on the joyful nose. Crunchy red berries pop on the palate, with blood orange and citrus notes adding a zestiness to the palate. The finish brings a savory twist of rosemary and mint, all elevated by vibrant acidity. A wine that also likes a chill. 93 Points — Jeff Porter

It has the body and ripe-fruit expression to pair with delicate cabbage or shrimp dumplings and sweet-and-smoky pork and beef preparations, and it harmonizes with salty, vinegary, and spicy condiments. And of course, Riesling’s trademark acidity cleanses the palate not just from fattier meats, but from the sheer quantity of food that is irresistible at a dim sum feast.

Full of flavor and confidently expressive, with guava and mangosteen lending an exotic edge to a core of yellow apple. The fruit is generous yet clearly defined, kept in check by bright, well-judged acidity that brings energy and structure. Food-Themed Fashion Is Starting to Feel Gluttonous

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Loewe | Loewe

There was a time, not too long ago, when a purse shaped like a baguette felt like a thrilling — even subversive — novelty. Now, we hardly bat an eye when we see that Moschino has made a trompe l’oeil celery purse, or that Big Bud Press is making a hot-dog-and-pretzel-print jumpsuit in collaboration with a Los Angeles bar. How did we get here? And has it all become a little too much? 

Fashion has long found inspiration from the culinary world, but the influx of food-fueled prints, graphics, and trompe l’oeil accessories that picked up steam during the pandemic have now reached a cultural tipping point. 

Today, there are truly countless examples of these food motifs and edible, wearable trompe l’oeil creations, at all price points. Loewe’s summer collection featured a $4,150 lambskin tomato clutch, while Walmart’s included a line of rotisserie chicken bag-like purses and charms starting at $5.97. As with many trends, this trajectory started in high fashion and worked its way down to mass-market ubiquity. In 2020, a chocolate chip cookie went down one of New York Fashion Week’s most buzzed-about fall runways. It protruded from a black purse by the designer Puppets and Puppets, a design that quickly went viral for its whimsical inclusion of a classic dessert on a high-end handbag. 

The then-emerging brand made food a central theme of its vision: Its cake-topped aviator caps, Ferrero Rocher heels, and beeswax-sculpted cheese hats — hyperrealistic, absurdist food designs capable of achieving instant social media virality. Prior to working in fashion, Puppets designer Carly Mark was working as an artist whose pieces revolved around candies and snacks. As she told Business of Fashion in 2023, the Puppets universe felt like the ideal pairing given what she considers food and fashion’s motivators: pleasure and money. 

View this post on Instagram

During the pandemic, the much-duped “strawberry dress” took over TikTok, and Tombolo Company found a hit on its hands with its vongole shirt, which debuted in 2019, but found peak popularity in 2021. The shirt appeared in Vogue in 2020, and InsideHook called it an “obvious bedfellow to day-drinks, rooftops and weekenders” — in other words, a status symbol. Katie Kimmel’s T-shirts made chicken-parm enthusiasm akin to being a devout sports fan, and in 2022, this very publication called “seafood kitsch” the look of the summer, as items like Rachel Antonoff’s super-popular seafood-tower dress flew off the digital shelves. 

Why was food so primed to become a motif in clothing at this point in culture? One could make the argument that it has roots in the millennial optimism years: Earlier in the 2000s and 2010s, the concept of “foodies” was new. Brands printed tongue-in-cheek food- and coffee-related motifs and slogans (a fervent love of avocados comes to mind) on hipster apparel, as the idea that “you are what you eat” developed growing fervor. That period was a key stepping stone in the evolution toward the far more elaborate and pervasive marketability of food-as-identity we see today. Content creator Cole Poska, who is a partner and creative director at Beyond Studios, tells Eater that “food brands have something most brands would kill for: decades of nostalgia, iconic identities, and consumers who are borderline obsessed with them.” Poska’s own Instagram often reimagines restaurant merch just for fun; his designs for Cheesecake Factory apparel went viral, such as a hat that resembles the iconic brown bread. He points out that people will defend their favorite fast-food order like it’s a family member, and with the kind of loyalty that’s hard to manufacture. The same could be said about their martini order, or favorite taco spot; it’s all a way of telling others who we are through what we like.

View this post on Instagram

But the fatigue is undoubtedly here. In 2024, Bon Appétit asked “Why Does Every Dress Have Pasta and Cocktail Shrimp on It?” And as the novelty factor of a shrimp-print dress has worn off, the next question might be: Will there be any true longevity for these food-as-fashion moments? Our feeds overflow with products that are hurriedly designed for flash-in-the-pan viral trends and quickly destined for a landfill; arguably more so if the object in question prioritizes shock value and gimmicks over function and brand identity. 

This is undoubtedly all related to the accelerated speed of trend cycles and shortened consumer attention spans. However, as Leah Asmelash wrote last year for CNN, the rising costs of food also casts an increasingly sinister shadow over food-themed fashion. In 2026, a $425 cardigan emblazoned with oysters has waning originality compared to a few years ago; it falls into banality at best and the Hunger Games at worst; its wearer indicating that they’re financially comfortable and, more importantly, well-fed. In that light, beyond its role in identity, food has become more of a status symbol than ever. A sweater touting one’s love of caviar is shorthand for their access to and appreciation of luxury, culinary and otherwise. But sporting the mass-market Temu version? It’s more akin to wearing a faux Gucci belt.  

“Food brands have something most brands would kill for: decades of nostalgia, iconic identities, and consumers who are borderline obsessed with them.”

Cole Poska

If it’s possible to escape the sense of accelerated-trend-cycle disposability, it may have to be through a more subtle approach than, say, a bag that looks like a giant stick of butter. Sam Salad is the co-founder of Meals, a Los Angeles-based, workwear-inspired apparel company that, by its own description, “bakes ‘food culture’ into apparel, blurring the lines between the clothes we eat and the food we wear.” Utility is at the forefront of Meals, which offers a classic chef pant with a slightly fuller cut, in both basic colors and subdued aesthetic homages to blue cheese and watermelon. When Meals launched in 2019, Salad was selling premium denim along Venice’s Abbot Kinney Boulevard, a hotbed of Instagrammy food culture and trendy restaurants like Gjelina that helped inspire his own brand. “Design today has to translate across languages and platforms,” Salad tells Eater. 

Food is hardly a new bedfellow for fashion designers, even if today’s hyper-fast trend cycle has kicked it into overdrive. “Food is totally universal,” Salad says. “Issey Miyake once merchandised their pleats collection as little omakase sets.”  He cites that collection among other fashion-meets-food moments that he considers successful, such as the iconic Salvador Dalí- and Elsa Schiaparelli-designed lobster dress. At the time of its 1937 creation, the dress was a scandal: It assumed a conformity-defying sartorial stance and was fittingly worn by Wallace Simpson. Today, its highly devolved spawn are available on Shein for $13. 

As it stands, one clear way out of corny, food motif purgatory is for brands — fashion or food — to focus less on quantity and more on storytelling and quality. “We do a few things really well, and that’s enough,” Salad says, “Like In-N-Out [Burger].” 

Poska’s philosophy is similar. “I’d rather make one thing people talk about for a year than 20 things people forget about tomorrow,” he says. “The first person to make a candle look like a stick of butter is a genius. The hundredth person is just making a candle.”
