Chocolate olive oil spread

📅 Sep 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

This recipe, from The Great Book of Chocolate, is one of my favorite recipes in the book. It’s a deep-dark chocolate spread that’s just as welcome on the breakfast table with warm croissants or toast, as it is as an afternoon snack, swiped on a slice of baguette.

The recipe comes from my friend Deb Perelman, of Smitten Kitchen, who came to Paris and made it in my kitchen with me. You can watch the full video here.

Since you’re making it at home, unlike store-bought spreads, you know what’s in this delectable. This recipe uses olive oil, which is healthy and delicious with the dark chocolate. I like to use one that’s fruity, which compliments the chocolate perfectly.

Depending on the type of chocolate you use, and it’s percentage of cacao solids, as well as the ambient temperature of your kitchen, the spread can be a bit firm, or soft, at room temperature. If it’s too firm, you can zap it for a few seconds in the microwave to make it more spreadable. If it’s too soft, it can be chilled briefly. In my kitchen, it’s perfect just as it is at room temperature…and I think you’ll love it too.

Chocolate Olive Oil Spread

A delicious dark chocolate spread with olive oil that you're whole family will enjoy. The recipe is adapted from my book, The Great Book of Chocolate – now available! Course Breakfast, Snack Keyword chocolate, olive oil, spread Servings 8

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup (70g) powdered sugar
  • 1/4 cup (25g) unsweetened cocoa powder preferably Dutch process
  • 4 ounces (115g) bittersweet or semisweet chocolate chopped
  • 1/2 cup (125ml) olive oil
  • Flaky sea salt such as Maldon or fleur de sel

Instructions

  • Sift together the powdered sugar and cocoa powder in a small bowl.
  • Warm the chopped chocolate and olive oil over low heat in a medium saucepan, stirring until the chocolate is melted and smooth. With a whisk, stir in the powdered sugar and cocoa powder mixture.
  • Transfer the mixture to small bowl and refrigerate until it's spreadable, which can take 2 to 3 hours. Check it every 15 minutes until it's the consistency of chocolate pudding. Scrape the mixture into a jar and store at room temperature until ready to serve.

Notes

The spread can be stored at room temperature for up to two weeks. It can be refrigerated for up to one month. Let it come to room temperature before serving.  For more information about cocoa powder, check out my Cocoa powder FAQs.

While you could use vanilla ice cream, Margot likes chocolate as much as I do, so I churned up a batch of chocolate sorbet, which also happens to be dairy-free, and she used that. Affogato

A delicious dessert with ice cream drowned in coffee liqueur, rum, and amaro Course Drinks Cuisine Italian Keyword affogato, amaro, coffee liqueur, ice cream, rum Servings 2

Ingredients

  • 3/4 ounce (20ml) dark rum
  • 3/4 ounce (20ml) amaro
  • 3/4 ounce (20ml) coffee liqueur
  • Ice cream or sorbet
  • 2 shots espresso (cooled slightly)

Instructions

  • In a small pitcher or measuring cup, mix together the rum, amaro, and coffee liqueur.
  • Put one generous scoop, or several smaller scoops, of ice cream or sorbet into two ice cream dishes or small bowls. Douse each serving with the mix of liqueurs.
  • Add a shot of espresso to each portion and serve.

Notes

You can use your favorite brands of liqueur here. In this recipe, Margot used Rabarbaro Nardini amaro, but others, such as Fernet-Branca or Ramazzotti, would work. Nowadays there are non-alcoholic versions of most liqueurs, including the ones used here. If you’re avoiding alcohol, you can use non-alcoholic alternatives.  For serving, feel free to shave some dark chocolate over the top of each affogato.

Banana Pepper & Goat Cheese Tart

📅 Sep 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This savory tart is something I threw together with all the odds and ends of a recent farmers’ market haul – herbs, scallions, a few banana peppers, zucchini blossoms, and some goat cheese. All that yellow – that’s me hanging on to summer! I keep pie dough in the freezer, especially this time of year, for impromptu tarts and pies, and because of that, this tart came together in no time. Highly recommend.

Why This Tart is So Simple

This isn’t a custard tart. It’s simply a market tart, where ingredients are piled onto a rolled-out piece of dough and baked once until deeply golden. You don’t have to par-bake the crust, and you don’t have to worry about the filling setting before the crust gets too dark. The process is a lot like making homemade pizza. Add your favorite toppings and bake. I like to eat a wedge of tart like this one alongside a bright, fresh, leafy salad.

Banana Pepper & Goat Cheese Tart: Ingredients

  • Banana Peppers: I jump on banana peppers whenever I see them, but they can be tricky to find. They have a little bite and add a fantastic zip to whatever you’re making – tart, pizza, salad, etc. Swap in another pepper you enjoy if banana peppers are elusive. I picked up some gorgeous, mild Cornito Giallo peppers the other day as well – or  even a yellow bell pepper would work! Can you use the pickled banana peppers in jars? Sure! They’ll be tangier than fresh peppers, but equally fun to experiment with on tarts like this one.
  • Zucchini Blossoms: Grab a few if you see them. They’re so special and beautiful, and I cut them into ribbon-like flecks here. If you can’t find them, just add another herb you like, or another edible flower – nasturtium or basil flowers would be nice!
  • Herbs: I opted for chives and basil, but if you can imagine another herb working well with goat cheese, by all means, go for it.
  • Goat cheese: I like using creamy, spreadable cheeses for tarts like this one. Labneh is another favorite in this sort of tart.

Shaping the Tart

The whole goal with a tart like this one is minimal fuss. And this includes shaping. You can bake it in a plan if you like but more often a free-form shape is in order. You can fold over the edges of your dough into a galette shape, or do what I’ve done here – fold the edge of the pie dough onto itself and then do a deep crimp using the thumb and pointer finger on one hand and the pointer finger on the other – see photo above.

I skipped the freezer step on this one (above), and you can see the issue. My crimping lost its definition, but aside from that it was really tasty and hit the lunchtime mark.

Making fluffy whipped cream without dairy seems like a foolish project but I've spent a total of about three (3!) weeks of my life trying to do it. The first two weeks were for a Vietnamese mocha cake recipe in my 2023 cookbook, Ever-Green Vietnamese. Many online sources pointed to using well chilled coconut cream but that method never worked for me. Nothing ever got fluffy, as you expect. I stuck with regular whipped cream for that favorite recipe in the book.

So, what was I looking for in the ideal whipped cream topping? It should be billowy and ethereal in texture, with a delicate creamy, rich flavor. That is easy to achieve with cow whipping cream (heavy cream) but using plant-based whipping creams can be tricky. I've tried a few and they did not work or had an off taste. Recently, I came across a method in Miyoko Schinner's super-useful book, The Homemade Vegan Pantry, that used coconut oil and soy milk. She's the vegan dairy expert behind Miyoko's plant-based butter and other products!

I tried Miyoko's recipe and the whipped toping was wondrously light, albeit a bit unstable after whipping. So, I manipulated it for a sturdy frosting to decorate a vegan strawberry and cream cake. Developing the vegan whipped cream frosting taught me so much that I returned to Miyoko's original recipe to refine and tweak it so you get this vegan whipped topping recipe -- which is lighter than frosting but sturdy enough to keep in the fridge for days! My husband, who loves regular whipped cream, adores this vegan version. He deemed it just as good as the conventional one. I hope you concur!

A vegan whipped topping for everyone

If you're a vegan, this recipe is for your dessert pleasure! Dip fresh fruit in for a simple treat. Spoon it onto a cake or pie for a rich garnish. If you're not a vegan -- this recipe is great for when you need a whipped topping but don't have regular whipping cream handy. It's a remarkable substitute.

What if you're allergic to soy milk? Miyoko suggests using water instead of soy milk and adding 1 teaspoon of guar or xanthan gum to the mixture. The result is a Cool Whip-style of topping. Below are my recipe development and testing notes for you to go at it yourself.

Why this vegan whipped cream topping works

Many recipes called for aerating chilled coconut cream but it's too dense to get fluffy enough. Here, the key is combining coconut oil with soy milk and cashews! Emulsifying them allows you to make a thick enough vegan whipping cream that you can successfully aerate into a whipped cream topping. Soy milk has enough fat and protein to support the coconut oil. Similarly, raw cashews add body and creaminess.

  • Use unsweetened soy milk made from soybeans and water. Keep it simple.
  • Select a soy milk that has a decent amount of protein -- about 9 grams per cup. I tested with West Life and Trader Joe's soy milk. Edensoy should work well too.
  • Select refined coconut oil because it is flavor neutral. When I made this with virgin, unrefined coconut oil, the whipped cream tasted like tanning lotion. GROSS.
  • Miyoko's ratio is 1:2 coconut oil with soy milk in terms of volume. I weighed the coconut oil to be consistent. If you weigh the soymilk, it should be about 240g for 1 cup.
  • To save money, use cashew halves and pieces.

Add-ins for vegan whipped cream topping

Aside from the coconut oil and soy milk, here's what the other ingredients do for this vegan whipped topping:

  • Sugar lends body and adds sweetness to the whipping cream. Do not cut back on the sugar because the cream will not whip up and thicken well without a certain amount of sugar.
  • Vanilla and salt add aroma and flavor. Salt amplifies the mildly sweet vegan whipped cream. I used vanilla extract. When I tried vanilla paste, the mixture barely whipped up.
  • Instant vanilla pudding mix or Dr. Oetker's Whip It stabilize the whipped cream so it will sit well in the fridge for days. They contain powerful modified cornstarch and other ingredients to absorb moisture and set the liquid mixture. Dr. Oetker's will yield a firmer, more stable whipped topping than the pudding mix. The pudding mix adds a pale yellow cast to the whipped topping. Buy Dr. Oetker's Whip It online or at Cost Plus (like I did!).

Watch me make vegan whipped cream topping

It's a miracle that these everyday ingredients come together for form a fluffy, light vegan whipped cream topping.

One final note . . .

Regular cow's whipping cream increases in volume as you whip it and aerate it. Vegan whipped cream does not increase in volume much. It does thicken and get billowy. It's rather magical. I hope you enjoy this recipe. Share your insights if you tweak it and how you serve it!

Vegan Whipped Cream Topping

One batch makes 2 cups. Use as much as you like per serving but for dipping or spooning, about ¼ cup (60ml) seems like a nice amount of richness. Weigh ingredients, whenever possible, for consistent results. Servings 8

Ingredients

  • ½ cup | 110g refined coconut oil
  • 1 cup | 240ml unsweetened soy milk, at room temperature
  • ¼ cup | 30g raw cashews (whole or pieces)
  • 2 ½ oz | 75g granulated sugar (about ⅓ cup organic or 6 tablespoons white)
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
  • ⅛ teaspoon fine sea salt
  • 2 tablespoons instant vanilla pudding mix or Dr. Oetker Whip It

Instructions

  • Make vegan whipping cream: Measure the coconut oil into a microwave safe measuring cup. Heat in the microwave on high power until just clear and melted, 30 to 45 seconds. Let cool to when it feels warm (about 100F). Pour the coconut oil into a blender jar. Add the soy milk, cashews, sugar, and vanilla. Whirl on high speed until pureed and creamy white, 30 to 60 seconds.
  • To ensure it is very cold and will whip up nicely, the cream should be 35 to 40F. Pour into a bowl, cover well, and at least 4 hours or better yet, overnight. (When in a hurry, transfer to a metal bowl and partially freeze for 2 hours, stirring 1 or 2 times.)
  • Whip it well: Use an electric mixer on medium-low speed to whip the vegan whipping cream. After a few minutes, when it starts to thicken (the beater tracks are a little deeper), sprinkle in the pudding mix (or Whip It). Continue beating, increasing the speed to medium or medium high. Beat until until soft peaks form, 5 to 8 minutes total. (If the cream doesn't start thickening during this time period, it's not cold enough! Freeze it for an hour or so then resume whipping.) Use immediately or refrigerate until serving time. Briefly rewhip if the cream seems a bit flat.

Multiple times a year I look around and declare that this is my favorite time of year to cook.  I love it in the winter when citrus season peaks. I love it when fava beans are the brightest green with the silkiest, creamiest texture (before they go too starchy). And I love it when the pluots are sweetest and the heirloom tomatoes most concentrated.  This was the feeling I had earlier in the week when I got home from the farmers’ market with the most inspiring ingredients I’ve had in years. I wrote everything down in a list —lemongrass, banana peppers, fresh cranberry beans, baby romaine, etc.. — and then started daydreaming about what I might make. The first thing was this summer-maxxing lunch salad. I tapped my pantry for mung beans, whisked together a simple vinaigrette, and then assembled everything into a wildly colorful, substantial salad where every bite is a bit different. 

High Salad Lunch Salad: Ingredients

The key here is assembling peak everything. If the tomatoes where you are look a little sad, skip them and bump up one of the other ingredients. Or, swap in another ingredient you love – this salad is very forgiving.

  • Tomatoes: My neighbor Emily tipped me off to just how good the Green Zebra tomatoes were this week, and she was spot on. I love their concentrated flavor and beautiful lime green and yellow striped markings. Keep an eye out for heirloom tomatoes where you are, and go for the ones with the best flavor.
  • Pluots: I love the complexity of a perfect, ripe pluot, and while a lot of people I know wait for peach season, I’m actually team pluot. You can use either here.
  • Crispy Shallots: A lot of people buy these, but I like to make my own. Fry thinly sliced shallots in a good amount of oil until deeply browned, then use a spider to transfer them to a paper towel. It’s one of the assertive elements that also brings crunch and savoriness to this salad.
  • Banana Peppers: Keep an eye out! I love their color and mild bite and spiciness. If you can’t find them and like bell peppers, swap in some yellow or red bell peppers. Quite different, but reasonable!
  • Vinegar: The vinegar here is important and should taste delicious. I’ve been using a red wine vinegar that is agrodolce – a bit sweet, but still plenty acidic (it’s this one). If you don’t have an agrodolce vinegar, consider adding a bit of honey or sugar to your vinaigrette to round it out a bit. Just enough to get it to your liking. 

A few of the other ingredients and flowers that were at the market at the same time. On the ingredient front there were: perfect carrots, yellow beans, fresh cranberry beans, shallots, lemongrass, and some little lettuces hiding in there. Marigolds made an appearance after weeks and weeks of show-stopping dahlias. It’s one of the first things I notice as we round the corner into fall. 

High Summer Lunch Salad: Serving Ideas

I served this salad basically as a meal on its own, with a bit of Zanzibari Sesame Flatbread from The Food of Oman (page 177), published in 2015. Sidenote: this is a wonderful book that Ken at Now Serving turned me on to a few years back. The flatbread has a rich coconut milk dough, and I’ve been making variations of it ever since I brought the book home. It seems like the book might be out of print, but poke around for a used copy – they’re out there. I also added a little plate of sheep’s milk feta drizzled with olive oil, more of a creamy bonus than an essential. 

As far as drinks go, I boosted some sparkling water with lemon, a bit of saffron, and plenty of ice, then called it a day.

A few months ago, someone asked about nampla prik if Chef Pim Techamuanvivit included it in our new book, Cooking Thai. Yes she does, and it's tucked away in the Bougie Pad Gaprao, a holy basil stir-fry with beef steak that's one of my go-to Thai recipes. Always read recipe endnotes in the book to unmask little hidden gems!

Nampla prik literally means fish sauce with chilies spiked with lime juice. I'd had it before at restaurants where it was lackluster, but Pim adds thinly sliced shallot and the sauce dazzles with complex flavor. You can have it as you like - with or without chilies - as much as you like, sometimes with garlic. Add extra lime juice to boost the tanginess for your taste.

How does Thai nampla prik compare to Vietnamese nuoc cham?

Nampla prik is more intensely tart, salty, spicy and pungent. There's no sugar and no water involved like what's in my master nuoc cham recipes in my newsletter and on this website. Also, nampla prik is enjoyed in relatively small, yet potent, quantities. You stir together it together and serve with a small spoon like you see in these photos from Cooking Thai. The above is of the bougie pad gaprao only and below are two fried rices. No, there is no shallot in this version for the rice. Yes, you can have nampla prik your way.

Nampla prik isn't super multipurpose like nuoc cham. In the book, we present it as a Thai "serving sauce" to accompany dishes like the holy basil stir-fries, salted egg yolk-shrimp fried rice and northern curry and mushroom fried rice. But ubiquitous holy basil stir fries (pad gaprao) can be made with practically any kind of meat or tofu so you're apt to see the same sauce on many tables.

How to make Pim's nampla prik

Nampla prik brightens and enlivens anything it touches. Use a small spoon to sprinkle the sauce onto your food at the table.

Nampla Prik

One batch of this fish sauce and chilies serving sauce yields about ¼ cup to serve 4. Recipe source: Cooking Thai by Pim Techamuanvivit with Andrea Nguyen (2026, Ten Speed Press) Course basics Cuisine Thai Keyword nampla prik, Thai basic sauce Servings 4

Ingredients

  • 1 ½ tablespoons lime juice
  • 1 ½ tablespoons fish sauce or Thai thin soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon chopped fresh Thai chilies
  • 1 tablespoon very thinly sliced shallot

Instructions

  • In a small bowl, mix together the lime juice, fish sauce (or Thai thin soy sauce), and Thai chilies. If using, stir in the shallot too. Let sit for a few minutes before serving with stir-fries, fried rice and fried foods. Dispense it with a small spoon because a little goes a long way.

Enjoy nampla prik your way

I always pack my tablespoon with the shallot because it tastes really good after sitting in the lime juice and fish sauce. For a primer on Thai fish sauce and soy sauce, check page 22 of Cooking Thai or visit the VWK guide to salty, spicy Thai condiments and seasonings.

Once you've experienced nampla prik on Thai food, you may be putting it on other things. For instance, I liked it for the Pad Kee Mao (drunken rice noodles stir-fry) in the book. And do note in the photo below that I left the chilies out of that bowl. My Viet taste buds can't handle as much chili heat as Thai taste buds can. I'd put a decent amount in the stir-fried noodles!

Shopping for condiments to prepare great Thai dishes has always tripped me up. For decades, I couldn't figure out what was what at a Thai grocery store because I don't read Thai. Additionally, I've always wondered -- if you use certain condiments made in Thailand or for Thai cooking, will that make a difference in the outcome? No cookbook satisfied me with the answers. After spending about three years writing a cookbook with Michelin-starred Chef Pim Techamuanvivit (also a good friend), I have this to report: The answer is yes and yes! If you want spot on flavors, use Thai ingredients. But there is leeway too.

While developing recipes and writing Cooking Thai with chef Pim, I got to have all my Thai cooking questions answered. Additionally, I was able to stress test the recipes when made with non-Thai ingredients. Together, Pim and I put together a comprehensive guide to Thai ingredients for the book (preview it, if you have not).

This blog post features an excerpt from our book, Cooking Thai. It's accompanied by the photo shot by Ed Anderson. The photo in the book (scroll on down) includes numbers so you can identify exactly what the label looks like. I have slightly tweaked things for internet layout and visibility. Savory, spicy, and salty Thai condiments

When a recipe ingredient is labeled "Thai," such as Thai thin soy sauce, use a brand from Thailand. It makes a huge difference in how the recipe comes out. Stock your pantry with these condiments to craft great Thai food.

[1] Fish sauce น้ำำ˝ˇปลา is a liquid made from fermented fish and salt and is widely sold. The two brands I use the most are Thai- produced Trachang (scale logo) and Megachef. Two other commonly available Thai brands, Squid and Tiparos, are OK too. Those four Thai brands contain between 1,500 and 1,700 mg of sodium per tablespoon. If your brand differs from mine significantly, adjust as needed.

[2] Golden Mountain Seasoning Sauce ซอสภููเขา ฝาเขีียว has a distinctly savory, slightly sweet flavor that's akin to lighter Maggi Seasoning sauce. It harmonizes well with Thai ingredients, whereas Maggi easily overwhelms. If unavailable, substitute Thai thin soy sauce. 

[3] Sea salt เกลืือทะเล , particularly fine sea salt that clocks in at about 550 mg per ¼ teaspoon, is what I often use in recipes. Check the nutrition label on your sea salt and adjust the amounts called for in recipes if needed.

[4] Thai black soy sauce ซีีอิ๊๊วดำำ˝ˇ is sweet, salty, and slightly syrupy- like diluted Indonesian kecap manis; this condiment is sometimes labeled sweet black soy sauce. I like the sauces by Megachef and Dragonfly, which is made by the Kwong Hung Seng company in Thailand. 

[5] Thai oyster sauce น้ำำ˝ˇมัันหอย tastes salty-sweet with a hint of brininess to evoke the piscine origin of the original condiment long ago. I prefer a Thai brand with relatively low sodium. Maekrua brand is mild and fine, but harder- to-find Megachef brand is punchier and better (check online, including Weee!). If you use non- Thai oyster sauce, use about 25 percent less. For vegan oyster sauce, we use Lee Kum Kee brand. 

[6] Thai sriracha ศรีีราชา is a chili-based hot, slightly garlicky, and mildly sweet condiment that originated in the 1930s in Si Racha, a town on the Gulf of Thailand. Generally available in two heat strengths, medium and strong, Thai-style sriracha works best for my dishes, like the Kin Khao Wings on page 78. I favor Sriraja Panich and Shark brands.

[7] Thai thin soy sauce ซีีอิ๊๊วขาว is not as dark as Chinese, Japanese, or Korean soy sauce, but it has a strong salinity and deep umami flavor so it pairs well with and can stand in for fish sauce in Thai vegetarian recipes. Healthy Boy thin soy sauce and Megachef gluten-free soy sauce work well. 

Umami from land and sea for Thai dishes

Along with sauces and salt, these umami-laden seasonings help build exciting Thai dishes.

[8] Dried shrimp กุ้้˝ˇงแห้้ง are used whole or chopped to add savory depth. Check the cold case of a Chinese or Southeast Asian market for small or medium ones and keep them frozen to preserve flavor and freshness. When possible, buy dried shrimp without food coloring; those may not be the brightest orange, but they'll be better for you. 

[9] Gapi กะปิิ (Thai shrimp paste) is the grounding umami in many Thai dishes. The dark mauve paste is typically sold in plastic jars, shelved in the fermented condiment or Thai section of Southeast Asian and Chinese markets. It is also sold online. Gapi is made from fermented shrimp (krill to be exact) and salt, plus maybe some MSG and sugar. Buy true, pure gapi made from just shrimp and salt, if you can. Brands such as Prateepthong (shrimp logo) and Trachang (scale logo) are good. Store it in the fridge, where it will last forever. Artisanal gapi is hard to find, so use my good gapi hack 0 (see page 118) to mimic excellent gapi produced in Thailand. Perplexingly, some Thai products labeled "shrimp paste" are not gapi. If the list of product ingredients includes oil and other spices, that's not gapi!

[11] Hatcho miso is a dark, thick, strong- flavored paste made from pure soybeans with no other grains (unlike other types of miso), is the best substitute for dried fermented soybeans, or tua nao ถั่่่วเน่่่า, from Northern Thailand. To make a swap for tua nao, I dehydrate miso and my recipes offer fast- and slow- drying methods. Shop for quality brands of hatcho miso, like Hikari or Marukome, at Japanese markets. When unavailable, use a dark aka (red) miso. If you are lucky enough to find real Thai tua nao, use it.
