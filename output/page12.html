Cooking polenta in modern times doesn’t have much in common with the way it was prepared for centuries in the regions that now make up Northern Italy. Polenta was traditionally cooked on a grand scale in copper cauldrons over open fire. There was a lot of stirring, ladling, and creative, resourceful uses of leftovers. Historically it was considered one of the humblest foods and when I came across this line about polenta in Honey From a Weed by Patience Gray (1986), it stuck with me. “The idea of polenta, planted firmly in the north Italian imagination, is the antithesis of modern aspirations. It therefore intrudes on modern life like a vague emotional regret.”

But, polenta is wonderful. And similar to other foods that were cast off in exchange for industrialized, refined counterparts – brown rice, naturally leavened breads, etc. — many people find their way back to the “old ways” eventually.

What is Polenta?

Think of polenta as a cornmeal porridge of sorts. In The Classic Italian Cook Book (1973), Marcella Hazan asserts the importance of polenta by saying, “…to call polenta a cornmeal mush is a most indelicate use of language. In country kitchens, polenta was more than food, it was a rite. It was made daily in an unlined copper kettle, the paiolo, which was always kept hanging at the ready on a hook in the center of the fireplace.” She goes on to describe a wonderful scene where, “…there was a moment of joy as it [polenta] was poured out in a steaming golden circle on the beechwood top of the madia…”

About This Polenta Recipe

Polenta is a regular preparation in my kitchen, and it can be cooked a number of ways. Not just in a cauldron lol.  I jump back and forth between stovetop polenta and oven-baked polenta depending on a number of factors. Today I’m going to talk through my technique for making beautifully creamy polenta on the stovetop. We’ll get to the oven-baked version in a future post. That one is great if you just want to combine ingredients in a baking dish, push it into the oven, and forget about it until the timer goes off. But it’s not as creamy. In either case, the ingredient list is short, and most of your attention should be focused on sourcing some great cornmeal / polenta. Beyond that, a bit of patience, and technique is all you need. I love it served topped generously with this mushroom ragù as you’ll see down below. 

Which Polenta (a.k.a. cornmeal or grits) Should I Use?

Look for stone-ground, whole grain, whole kernel polenta without any additives – preferably organic. Store-bought, quick-cooking, refined polenta is the starchy endosperm of the corn kernel, and much of the flavor from the bran and germ is stripped out in the milling process. These are a few favorites to look for.

  • Hayden Flour Mills Yellow Corn Polenta:  My local market carries Hayden and this is the polenta I purchase most often. It is coarse grind, so it takes a bit longer to cook, but the flavor and texture is wonderful. (Pictured above right)
  • Bob’s Red Mill Organic Medium Grind Cornmeal: Stone-ground, whole grain. Readily available and a solid choice. If I’m looking for a polenta that will cook a bit faster, this is the one to grab because the corn is medium coarse and a bit finer. (Pictured above left)
  • Rancho Gordo Fine Yellow Polenta:  If you like a finer grind, this is your choice. Organic, heirloom corn grown & ground by Anson Mills for RG. IYKYK.

Whole polenta is quite perishable. Use it quickly or store it in the freezer.

Do I Have to Stir Polenta with a Wooden Spoon?

If you read through old polenta recipes, many will mandate stirring with a wooden spoon. It’s a good suggestion, but your polenta will be just fine if you use a stainless steel spoon instead. That said, wood spoons are kinder to your pots, less likely to scratch (especially if your pots have any sort of coating). And, some metal spoons (especially very old ones) can react to acidic ingredients resulting in a metallic taste. If you’re making tomato sauce or sourdough, for example.

Polenta Variations

Knowing how to make a great pot of polenta sets you up with a foundation to keep it simple and straight-forward (like the creamy polenta recipe below), or to add your own twists and flavors. Here are a few ways you can switch things up.

  • Use broth: Instead of water as the base liquid in your polenta experiment with broths, teas, or other infusions. I often make mine with a favorite mushroom tea (topping the polenta with these baked mushrooms.
  • Make it herby: Polenta has a real affinity for certain herbs. My favorite is chives. Double down with a couple handfuls of snipped chives folded in along with some chive compound butter, near the end of cooking so the chives retain their brightness.

What to Do with Leftover Polenta

There are many things to do with leftover polenta. Patience Gray includes this wonderful paragraph (Honey From A Weed, 1986) that exemplifies the cycle of a pot of polenta, “…made one day to serve hot, what is left is sliced and fried in hot oil till golden (polenta fritta), or brushed with oil and grilled (polenta ai ferri), to eat the next day. If chopped salame, parmesan cheese, butter and egg yolks are added to the polenta once it is stiff, and, when it has cooled, the beaten egg whites are folded in, and the preparation put into an oiled oven dish, what emerges after 45 minutes in a hot oven is a torta di polenta.”

Here are a couple other ideas:

  • Baked Polenta:  Slice into strips, brush with olive oil and pan-fry or bake at 350°F into golden, tasty slabs. Serve topped with mushroom ragù.
  • Polenta in Sourdough: This is a favorite polenta-centric baking tip. Crumble cooked (and cooled) polenta into sourdough prior to baking. I tend to do this after my second set of folds. Alternately, you can fold into quick-bread batters.

Crisp warm tortillas filled with chicken, bell peppers, and plenty of melting cheese provide a terrific dinner, or an excellent way to use up just a bit of leftovers. Who would turn down piping hot Chicken Fajita Quesadillas for lunch or dinner? (Not me!)

These quesadillas are something else, my friend. And, the best part is that they make use of my slow cooker chicken fajitas. Those fajitas are a “fast crockpot meal,” meaning you can start them when you have a spare moment in the afternoon and they’ll be ready by the time everyone is hungry in the evening. Tuck those fajitas in a tortilla with melted cheese, and I’m one happy camper.

Chicken Fajita Quesadillas

My sister taught me how to make absolutely irresistible quesadillas many years ago and ever since then, they’ve been a regular breakfast, lunch, and dinner option in our home.

Quesadilla fillings are endlessly fun to play with, and they’re a terrific way to use up leftover ingredients that might be in the fridge. I like to make chicken fajitas in bulk (which is why I have a slow cooker recipe), so that I can throw together lunches like these chicken fajita quesadillas, where it feels a little more planned out than just plain cheese.

Ingredients and Substitutions

Tortillas – For quesadillas, I prefer flour tortillas, for their flavor. They also tend to hold up better under heat. But, corn tortillas will work if that is your preference.

The Cheese – Any melty cheese with mild flavor will work. My go-tos are cheddar, Monterey jack, or Pepper Jack.

Chicken & Vegetables – If you’re not using leftover fajitas, you can still make this quesadilla. Just use pre-cooked chicken, and quick saute some yellow onion and bell pepper.

Butter – Butter helps keep the tortillas from sticking to the pan while crisping the edges, and adding just a hint of rich flavor too.

How to Make Chicken Fajita Quesadillas

Warming the Skillet: You’ll need to set a large non-stick skillet over medium heat on the stove for a couple of minutes to heat it up fully.

Buttering the Tortilla: I lightly butter one side of the tortilla with about ½ teaspoon of butter. Then I place it in the hot pan butter side down.

Layering the Ingredients: I sprinkle about half of the cheese across one side of the tortilla. Then, I top with the chicken fajitas, the remaining cheese, and a sprinkling of cilantro.

Folding the Tortilla: Once all the filling ingredients have been added to the quesadilla, you’ll want to fold the tortilla over on itself and let cook for 2-3 minutes, until the cheese has begun to melt and the bottom tortilla is golden brown.

Flipping the Quesadilla: I carefully flip the folded quesadilla over to cook the other side for an additional 2-3 minutes.

Serving: After the quesadilla is golden brown on both sides, you can use a spatula to transfer it to a cutting board. Slice it into wedges and serve warm.

Expert Tip

For the past few years, I’ve had quesadillas for lunch more often than anything else. Over those years, I’ve found that the most important part of the process is temperature control and patience. To make a great quesadilla, the cheese has to be fully melted in the same time it takes for the tortilla to crisp. So, if the tortilla is browning too fast, I reduce (or even remove the pan from) the heat and cover the pan with a lid to help melt the cheese.

No Fajitas, No Problem – If you don’t happen to have leftover fajitas on hand, a bit of cooked chicken and sauteed peppers and onions will do the trick nicely. Toss them together with a sprinkling of taco seasoning and you’ll have a tasty improvised chicken fajita mix for your quesadillas.

Serving Suggestions

These quesadillas turn into a real feast with a few sides on the table. I set out southwestern po’dunk sauce for dipping and pile a scoop of sweet and spicy coleslaw alongside for crunch. To make it a full plate, round it out with a helping of cajun rice. Keep it as simple or as fancy as you like!

Make Ahead & Storage

How to Store: Leftovers can be refrigerated in an airtight container for 1-3 days.

How to Reheat: To maximize crispiness, I like to reheat leftover quesadilla wedges in a skillet on the stove, or in the air fryer.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I make these fajitas with what I have on hand?

You’ll want to warm a large non-stick skillet on the stove over medium-high heat. While the pan warms, chop the onion and bell pepper. Saute with 1 tbsp of olive oil until softened and lightly charred. Then add in some pre-cooked chicken until warm.

Can I use another protein source, instead of chicken?

I’ve found that beef is a great replacement, as is leftover pulled pork. If you want to try a vegetarian fajita option, try this with some black beans in the mix.

Why is my cheese not melting fast enough?

It may seem odd, but usually the heat is too high. You need to lower the heat so that the tortilla will crisp more slowly, giving the cheese time to melt. You can also cover the pan with a lid to help the cheese steam and melt. There are more notes about managing temperature control and melting the cheese in the tips section.

These Chicken Fajita Quesadillas are crispy and delicious, loaded with bell peppers, onions, and chicken. Ready in minutes, perfect for busy nights. Course Appetizer, Main Course Cuisine American Prep Time 2 minutes minutes Cook Time 5 minutes minutes Total Time 7 minutes minutes Servings 1 Calories 387kcal Author Mary Younkin

Ingredients

  • 1 8-10" flour tortilla
  • 1 teaspoon butter
  • ½ cup shredded pepper jack, Monterey Jack, or Mexican blend cheese
  • ¼ cup cooked chicken chopped or thinly sliced
  • 2 tablespoons sauteed bell peppers thinly sliced
  • 1 tablespoon sauteed yellow onion thinly sliced
  • 1 teaspoon fresh cilantro chopped

Instructions

  • Preheat a large non-stick skillet to medium heat. Lightly butter one side of the tortilla with ½ teaspoon of butter and place the tortilla butter side down in the hot pan.
  • Sprinkle about half the cheese over the tortilla and top with the cooked chicken, bell peppers, and onions. Top with the remaining cheese and sprinkle with cilantro.
  • Fold the tortilla over on itself and let cook for 2-3 minutes, until the cheese has begun to melt and the bottom tortilla is golden brown.
  • Carefully flip the folded quesadilla over to cook the other side for an additional 2-3 minutes. After the quesadilla is golden brown on both sides, use a spatula to transfer it to a cutting board.
  • Slice it into wedges and serve warm. Repeat as desired for additional servings.

The Eggs Scene in &#8216;You Can See Everything&#8217; Scrambles Reality

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This review contains spoilers for the film You Can See Everything.

A version of this post originally appeared in Eater Today, which spotlights the freshest news and stories from across the food world every day. Subscribe now.

I saw You Can See Everything — the new documentary about disgraced Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes, co-directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim — on Thursday, and I still feel like my brain is trying to punch its way out of a bag, or like I’ve just awakened from an Inception-esque dream within a dream. It’s difficult to describe the winding, twisting, nearly three-hour film to someone unfamiliar with its many layers of context, from Fielder’s Dadaist, boundaryless approach to his subjects to Holmes’s hypnotic affect, which is either highly effective gaslighting or blinding, self-deluded earnestness. (Which is it? I still don’t know.)

What happens when you take two unreliable narrators — make that three, if you include Holmes’s partner, Billy Evans, whose on-camera persona morphs from protective, naive tech bro to unscrupulous grifter — put them in a jar, and shake them? The result is that the more questions are asked, the further the truth drifts out of reach. It’s like staring at a Magic Eye image, slightly crossing your eyes, desperate to make out the hidden 3D subject but never quite managing to see it. Eventually, you start worrying your eyes might get stuck that way.

The discourse surrounding the film is already abundant, but we’re here to talk about the eggs.

If you’re familiar with the hallmarks of Fielderian programming, you know that Fielder is prone to hyperfixating on seemingly mundane details that later return to completely upend viewers’ expectations. In You Can See Everything, one such detail is observed in an exchange between Holmes and Evans as they make eggs. How much can be gleaned from watching someone make breakfast? A lot, apparently.

In the first half of the film, Fielder is fully embedded in the rental home of Holmes and Evans, who have invited the filmmakers to document the final weeks before Holmes reports to federal prison. (How and why they agreed to this arrangement is incomprehensible if they knew anything about Fielder’s work.) During this time, the narrative takes a surprisingly sympathetic turn for Holmes, who seems intent on convincing the filmmakers — and Fielder specifically — that she is actually a sweet, misunderstood victim of circumstance, not a Machiavellian con woman who defrauded investors. Frankly, aspects of this claim feel convincing at times. Viewers are invited to witness her ostensibly less guarded moments, including a scene in which she makes breakfast with Evans. As Fielder interviews her, Holmes somewhat unenthusiastically and limply beats a bowl of eggs with a fork. The camera lingers on her feeble whisking technique. Then, after a few seconds, another set of hands enters the frame: those of Evans. Without a word, he takes the bowl and fork from Holmes and begins whipping the eggs with considerably greater fervor.

Now, backseat driving in the kitchen is hardly uncommon among couples, though it feels like a fast track to bickering. (Evans tells Fielder that he and Holmes “never fight”; Holmes is also emphatic that she’s never lied in her life. Again, unreliable narrators.) What’s less common — or at least generally considered pretty rude and aggressive — is grabbing something out of your partner’s hands mid-task to take over. Fielder points this out, asking Evans why he felt the need to grab the eggs; Holmes explains that Evans doesn’t like the way she mixes them and thinks he can do it better. 

It’s a tiny interaction, but a remarkably revealing one. Evans feels comfortable taking control of something Holmes is doing, without so much as asking, and seems largely unaware of how his behavior might come across on camera. Holmes, meanwhile, appears accustomed to the intrusion, offering an explanation rather than an objection. (Granted, unlike sustained and unblinking eye contact, beating eggs does not appear to be one of Ms. Holmes’s strong suits. Typically, one does so vigorously to aerate them and make them fluffier for an omelet or scramble.)

But with this being Elizabeth Holmes, we must also ask: Could it be a case of weaponized incompetence? Is it possible that, not wanting the task of beating the eggs, she intentionally does so insufficiently to make Evans take over? Is she being controlled, or is she getting someone else to do her work for her? Or is this evidence of her “different” way of thinking, that she truly doesn’t know the “right” way to beat them? 

In interviews in the film, Holmes portrays the Theranos years as a period during which she was manipulated by her former boyfriend and business partner, Sunny Balwani, and maintains that her intentions were ultimately good for humanity, regardless of the consequences for investors and patients who received unreliable test results. Whether she actually believes this account of events, or simply knows how to sell it, remains one of the film’s central mysteries — as does the extent to which her romantic relationships lead her to take the fall for the actions of bad worse men.

These questions reverberate in the second half of the film, when — with Holmes now in prison — we learn some unsettling things about Evans. He has power of attorney over Holmes, and has been using her X account to post odd, profanity-laced remarks that seem entirely incongruent with her own manner of speaking. Suddenly, the eggs scene looks like an early indication that Evans is far more domineering than the protective-partner persona he’s been presenting. His ostensible vigilance toward Holmes may be genuine on some level, but it’s difficult to reconcile with his willingness to license her image for billboards promoting another (bizarre-seeming) entrepreneur’s Theranos obsession, or his apparent acceptance of the possibility that her AI-generated likeness could be used to create pornography or other content antithetical to her interests. 

And yet, even as Evans emerges as an increasingly unsavory character, Holmes remains inscrutable. Does she know what he’s doing? Does she approve? Is he exploiting her circumstances, or are they working toward a shared objective — reputational, monetary, or otherwise? The film offers kaleidoscopic evidence for competing interpretations, including that even if Holmes is being dominated, she may be content with that dynamic. 

What’s extraordinary about Fielder and Oppenheim’s documentary is its optical-illusion-like layers of “truth.” Despite the film’s many harrowing close-ups of microexpressions, there is what Holmes and Evans say, then there is what they do, or have done, or plan to do (launch a Theranos 2.0 called Haemanthus, apparently). There is also Fielder’s own history as a provocateur, a faux-earnest magician who will go to virtually any lengths to actualize a concept, and who seems remarkably capable of keeping a stone-cold straight face through any scenario. 

We know enough about Fielder to question his sincerity, too. There are several moments in the film where I — and the several friends I went to see the film with — wondered if there was palpable sexual chemistry between him and Holmes, and whether Fielder would act on it. If he did, we couldn’t agree on whether it would be genuine or an act of performance art, or whether he has any barriers between those things. 

The film’s most revealing moments are the ones that feel off-script for everyone involved: a fleeting facial expression, an odd word choice in an otherwise emphatic statement, a series of gestures exchanged over a bowl of scrambled eggs. The subjects, who have invited themselves to be watched, have few moments when they seem to forget that; those instances are when we ostensibly have the clearest insight into their reality. Frankly, it’s still murky, and that seems to be both the point and the irony in the film’s title.

You Can See Everything elevates Nathan Fielder’s reputation far beyond simply a master of cringe humor; there is, here, a glimmer of genius. This story feels almost fateful in its ability to showcase his gifts, illuminating the bottomless strangeness, the infinite complexities, of how humans interact with one another. Even when an idea is big, it’s the little things that matter the most. And in a documentary that promises you can see everything, we learn that it’s up to us to look closely — and even then, what we see might look different in another light.

👩🏼‍🌾 Ingredients

Below, I share ingredient notes from both a nutrition expert's and trained chef's perspective.

  • Hulled split yellow mung beans: Also called moong dal or yellow mung dal. These blend into a smooth batter while contributing protein, fiber, and iron. Use these rather than whole green mung beans.
  • Unsweetened soy milk: Adds protein and creaminess. Choose plain, unflavored milk.
  • Low-sodium salt (optional): Saltiness varies by brand. The miso, kala namak, and sun-dried tomatoes also contribute salt.
  • Nutritional yeast: Adds a savory, slightly cheesy flavor.

  • Turmeric: Gives the batter a golden color. A small amount is enough without overpowering the flavor.
  • Miso paste: Adds savory depth. Choose gluten-free miso if needed.
  • Baking powder: My simple hack for a little lift in the unstrained batter. Keeping all the bean pulp retains its fiber and other nutrients.
  • Onion and bell peppers: Add sweetness, texture, and color. Chop finely so they distribute evenly.
  • Spinach or tender greens: An easy way to add more vegetables. Wash and dry thoroughly before chopping.
  • Sun-dried tomatoes: Add concentrated, tangy flavor. Drain well if packed in oil.
  • Fresh parsley or chives: Add freshness to balance the rich, savory batter.

🌱 Substitutions

This recipe is versatile and easy to adapt. Below are some simple ingredient substitutions.

  • Nondairy milk: Use plain, unsweetened plant milk or your milk of choice. Soy and pea milk typically provide more protein, while hemp milk can also add creaminess. Protein content varies by brand.
  • Spinach: Substitute finely chopped tender kale or Swiss chard, removing tough stems.
  • Bell peppers: Replace some with finely chopped broccoli or cooked, well-drained mushrooms.
  • Fresh herbs: Try parsley, chives, dill, or a combination.
  • Kala namak: Optional for a more pronounced eggy flavor. Miso and nutritional yeast add savory depth. Regular salt provides seasoning but won't give the same eggy flavor.
  • Lower-sodium option: Omit the kala namak, omit the salt or choose reduced-sodium salt, and use unsalted sun-dried tomatoes.
  • Miso: Chickpea miso is an option if avoiding soy. Also avoid soy milk to make the recipe soy-free.

One Crust Is All You Need

The filling is fully cooked on the stovetop before it ever hits the oven. That means the crust is only there to bake up flaky and golden, and a single top crust does that job perfectly. Skipping the bottom crust keeps every bite crisp on top and creamy underneath, without any doughy layer at the bottom of the dish.

How to Make Chicken Pot Pie Casserole Step by Step

Gather the ingredients: Gather all the ingredients together. Preheat the oven to 375°F and spray a 2-quart casserole dish with nonstick spray. Take your 9-inch pie crust out of the fridge and let it sit at room temperature for 15-20 minutes. Cold dough cracks when you roll or bend it, and a little time on the counter makes it soft enough to work with.

Melt the butter and oil: Heat 4 tbsp of unsalted butter and 2 tbsp of olive oil in a large skillet over medium-high heat until the butter melts and the fat starts to shimmer. The oil keeps the butter from browning too fast, so you get all that buttery flavor without any burnt bits.

Build the veggie base: Add 2 diced carrots and 2 diced ribs of celery and cook for 3 minutes. They go in first because they’re the firmest and need a head start. Add 1 minced sweet yellow onion and cook until it turns soft and translucent. Then stir in 2 cloves of minced garlic for 30 seconds, just until fragrant. Garlic burns quickly, which is why it goes in last. This carrot, celery, and onion trio is the classic base of any pot pie, and it gives the gravy a sweet, savory backbone.

Add the chicken, mushrooms, and peas: Stir in 3 cups of cubed cooked chicken, 4 oz. diced button mushrooms, and 1 cup thawed frozen peas, and cook for 1 minute. The chicken is already cooked, so it only needs to warm up. The mushrooms add a rich, meaty umami flavor that makes the filling taste deeper, and the peas bring little pops of sweetness and color.

Cook the flour: Sprinkle ¼ cup of all-purpose flour over everything and cook for 3 minutes, stirring the whole time. The flour soaks up the butter and oil and turns into a paste that coats the chicken and veggies. Cooking it the full 3 minutes gets rid of the raw flour taste and gets it ready to thicken the gravy. Everything should look evenly coated, with no dry white streaks of flour left.

Deglaze with broth: Pour in 2 cups of low-sodium chicken broth and scrape the bottom of the pan with a wooden spoon. The browned bits stuck to the skillet are packed with flavor, and the broth lifts them right into the gravy. Low-sodium broth lets you control the salt since the filling reduces as it simmers. It will look thin at this point, and that’s normal.

Add the wine, cream, and seasonings: Stir in ¼ cup of white wine and 1 cup of heavy cream. The cream makes the filling rich and velvety, and the wine adds a little acidity so all that richness doesn’t feel heavy. Season with ½ tsp of kosher salt, 1 tsp of freshly ground black pepper, ½ tsp of dried thyme, and ¼ tsp of ground sweet basil. The thyme is what gives it that classic pot pie flavor.

Simmer until thick: Keep cooking and stirring until the filling thickens to a thick-stew consistency. It should coat the back of a spoon and slowly fill back in when you drag a spoon through the pan. Don’t rush this part, because the filling won’t thicken much more in the oven. Taste it and add more salt if needed.

Fill the casserole dish: Pour the filling into the prepared casserole dish and spread it into an even layer so the crust sits flat on top.

Top with the crust: Roll the pie crust to about ¼-inch thickness. Cut it into 1-inch strips and weave a lattice over the filling, or drape the whole crust over the top and trim or crimp the edges. If you use a full crust, cut a few small slits in the center so steam can escape. Keep the crust as taut as you can over the dish so it bakes up crisp instead of sinking into the filling.

Brush on the egg wash: Beat 1 large egg with 1 tsp water and brush it over the crust. The egg wash is what gives the top that shiny, deep golden-brown finish.

Bake until golden: Bake on the center rack for 35-45 minutes, until the crust is golden brown and the filling is bubbling around the edges. If the crust browns too fast, loosely tent it with foil. 

Rest and serve: Let the casserole rest for about 10 minutes before serving so the filling can set.

How to Store, Freeze, and Reheat

Store leftover chicken pot pie casserole in an airtight container (or tightly covered in its baking dish) in the refrigerator for up to 3 days. The crust will soften a bit overnight, but the oven brings back some of its crispness.

Freezer

You can freeze chicken pot pie casserole both before and after baking. Just be sure to do so in a non-glass baking dish (a metal or foil pan works great) to prevent it from shattering. Tightly wrap the casserole in 2 layers of plastic wrap and 1 layer of aluminum foil and freeze for up to 2 months. The cream sauce may look slightly separated after thawing, but it comes back together as it heats.

Reheating

  • Oven (best for leftovers). Reheat refrigerated leftovers in a 375°F oven for 25 to 30 minutes, or until warmed through. Cover loosely with foil if the crust starts getting too dark.
  • Microwave (best for single servings). Heat a scoop in 30-second bursts, stirring the filling between rounds. The crust won’t be crisp, but it’s the fastest option for lunch.
  • Already-baked frozen casserole. Place it in a 375°F oven for 30 to 35 minutes.
  • Unbaked frozen casserole. Bake in a preheated oven with the foil on for 30 minutes, then remove the foil and continue to bake for 45 to 50 minutes, until golden brown and 165°F in the center.

Cook the filling as directed, then let it cool completely. Store it covered in the fridge for up to 2 days. When you’re ready to bake, spread it in the dish, top with the crust, and bake as usual.

Assemble the Night Before

Let the filling cool completely before you add the crust. Hot filling steams the dough from underneath and can leave it gummy. Once it’s assembled, cover the dish and refrigerate until you’re ready to bake. Brush on the egg wash right before it goes in the oven, and add an extra 5 to 10 minutes to the bake time since it’s starting cold.

Freeze It Unbaked

Assemble the casserole in a freezer-safe pan with cooled filling, skip the egg wash, and wrap it as described in the freezer section. Either thaw it overnight in the fridge and bake as usual, or bake it straight from frozen using the reheating directions above. This is a great meal to drop off for a new parent or a friend who needs a hand, because they can bake it whenever they’re ready.

Recipe Success Tips

  1. Keep those pork chops juicy! I use boneless pork chops in this recipe, which are fairly lean, so they can dry out quickly. I recommend using an instant-read thermometer to check the thickest part of each chop, especially if yours are thicker than the ones I’ve used. Pork is safely cooked at 145°F, followed by a 3-minute rest. Set the chops aside while you make the gravy, then return them to the skillet briefly to warm through so they don’t overcook.
  2. Give the pork chops room to brown. Let the chops sit at room temperature for 10-15 minutes to help take the chill off. Then, pat them dry with paper towels before seasoning and dredging, and make sure the skillet is hot before adding them. If your chops don’t fit comfortably in one layer, cook them in batches! Crowding the pan traps moisture, making it harder to develop that lovely golden crust. Watch the heat, too, as the flour coating can burn if the pan gets too hot.
  3. Get the gravy consistency right. The butter and reserved flour form a roux, a mixture of roughly equal parts fat and flour that thickens the gravy. Storage & Reheating

Store leftover smothered pork chops in an airtight container in the fridge for up to 3 days. I recommend keeping the chops covered with plenty of gravy to help prevent them from drying out. Refrigerate within 2 hours of cooking.

Reheating

I prefer reheating these pork chops in a covered skillet over medium-low heat to help keep them tender. The gravy will thicken as it cools, so add a splash of chicken broth or milk to loosen it up. Heat the gravy thoroughly and warm the pork chops until they reach an internal temperature of 165°F. Try not to heat them any longer than necessary, as boneless pork chops can become tough when reheated.

Freezing

I wouldn’t freeze these smothered pork chops. The heavy cream in the gravy can separate or turn grainy after thawing, and the pork chops may lose some tenderness.

The ‘Tavern-Style’ Pizza Takeover

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →
The thin-crust pizzas popular throughout the Midwest have taken off as the “tavern-style” pizza trend | Brad Bobrow/La Merna

In the suburbs of Chicago where they both grew up, it was just pizza, according to Brittany and Brennan Grosvenor, the couple behind Proof in Portland, Oregon. Ordering came down to the question of “thin-crust, deep-dish, or double-dough,” Brittany recalls of her family’s Friday night tradition. The Grosvernors sell the former at Proof, though the language of “thin crust” has given way to the more nationally familiar phrasing of “tavern-style,” the pizza trend that has swept from coast to coast in recent years. 

Outsiders might associate Chicago with its famous, tourist-beckoning deep-dish pizza, but Brittany says that’s just not reflective of reality, citing deep dish as a much less frequent treat than the Midwestern, thin-crust style that’s gaining popularity. “The pizza you get every week, or if you go to a kid’s birthday party, it’s always tavern-style,” she says.

“Tavern-style” pizza — which is prevalent  across the Midwest, with Chicago, Milwaukee, Minnesota, and St. Louis touting similar pies — is known for its thin, cracker-like crust, cut into a lattice instead of triangular slices. Depending on the region and the pizza shop where one’s nostalgia lies, the sauce might lean sweet or spicy. Lightness and crispness, though, is the style’s unique sell. Writing for Esquire in 2023, Omar Mamoon called the variant, which was then still on the rise, “a foil” to famously thick Detroit-style pizza, which had its big moment in the late 2010s. 

The beauty of the tavern-style pie is its shareability. A pizza yields more slices when cut into a grid than into triangles, explains Bradley Shorten, who’s behind both Kim’s Uncle Pizza in his hometown Chicago suburb of Westmont, Illinois, and the recently opened La Merna in Washington, D.C. Pizza cut into little squares makes for great drinking food, especially since it fits on a cocktail napkin. What makes a good pie, according to Shorten, is that every square has a little of every ingredient; the toppings go to the edge. And while the four, small corner slices might not be the “best” ones, Shorten says, they’re the ones “everybody goes for,” especially if they’re from Chicago.

Over the past few years, as “taverns” have become growing fixations for a cadre of restaurateurs, the “party cut” pizza and tavern-style pizza spots have also become ubiquitous, with openings like Keepers in New York city; One Trick Pony in Forth Worth, Texas; Tillie’s Tavern Pizza in Wheat Ridge, Colorado; and Zarella and Dimmi Dimmi Corner Italian in Chicago, where the style has always been abundant. For some Chicagoans, these openings across the country are an opportunity to correct the record about their city’s pizza culture, and for other Midwesterners to claim their stake in the region’s pizza, too.

When Mike Fadem opened the second location of his popular NYC pizzeria Ops — known for its wood-fired Neapolitan-esque pies with leopard-spotted crusts and pliant dough — he wanted to offer something new. Fadem is from St. Louis, where the city’s namesake pizza is super-thin. Despite his love for it, “it’s maybe not the best version of tavern pizza,” Fadem admits, noting the iteration’s many critics, some of whom take issue with its use of the gooey processed cheese called provel. The trajectory of tavern-style pizza has been like a game of telephone, he explains: St. Louis pizza is derived from thin-crust Chicago pizza, which itself came from Neapolitan pizza. Divergence has accompanied each jump.

One of Fadem’s employees at Ops, meanwhile, is from New Jersey, which claims its own thin-crust, tavern- or bar-style pizza tradition. (The bar pies of the Boston area also have their own devoted following.) Accordingly, “I had this interest in ‘can we make an Ops version of this?’” Fadem says. With Ops known for sourdough, Fadem also wanted to highlight the rich flavor of whole grains. And he wanted it to be really crispy, a quality he says draws in New Yorkers. Fadem’s take is on the menu as the regionally agnostic “tavernetta.” Though St. Louis and New Jersey served as initial inspiration, the best pizza from his research was actually in Milwaukee. The Tavernetta hasn’t displaced the other pies at the restaurant; it’s ordered in addition to them, with most groups getting at least one.

In June, Los Angeles cocktail spot Bar Next Door celebrated its third anniversary by adding food to its menu. Previously, the bar had allowed customers to bring in pies from Prince Street Pizza next door since Prince Street co-owner Lawrence Longo is a partner in it. “But, you know, after having a slice, sometimes you get a little full and you’re like, I want to go to bed,” says owner and bar director Brynn Smith. “That’s the whole goal for us: for people to stay a little longer, drink more. We thought the tavern-style pizza would be perfect.” The fact that the pizzas — they’re technically listed on the menu as “bar pies,” to better go with the establishment’s name — are made exclusively for the bar by Prince Street just adds another reason for people to go out, Smith says.

The idea of the tavern as a social and convivial space is inseparable from the tavern-style pizza. Shorten’s goal with La Merna — which he runs with his wife Meg Schmitt, who grew up in St. Louis — was the feel of a Chicago dive bar or a Wisconsin supper club: a place with wood-paneled walls and checkered floors, where people could slide into a red leather booth for pitchers of beer and pizza and hang out for a while. As my colleague Francky Knapp wrote last year, the rising appeal of the “tavern” vibe across restaurants nationwide is rooted in the sense of providing somewhere homey and lived-in. At taverns, diners are beckoned to sprawl out and stay awhile; to free themselves of pretense and to tap into something welcoming in its familiarity. This, for so many people, is exemplified by the archetype of hometown pizza shop, which is forever tethered in our cultural imaginations to the trappings of a previous, simpler era.

It’s for this reason that, in Oregon, the Grosvenors have started serving their take on Bosco sticks, the stuffed breadsticks of Midwestern cafeterias, and Italian beef with giardiniera. While they could find certain Midwestern hits around Portland, it sometimes frustrated them that they couldn’t find everything in one place. According to Brennan Grosvenor, “We’re just trying to embody the feeling: If you went into a tavern in Chicago, what could you order there?”
