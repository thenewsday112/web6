NBA Champion Josh Hart Is the Winner of Our Wine & Culture Award

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For a few nights last June, it was normal to see groups of people—friends, complete strangers—crying tears of joy together in the streets of New York. First, it was over the Knicks’ miraculous 29-point comeback against the Spurs in Game 4 of the NBA Finals. Then it was when the team won the title for the first time in 53 years. For many New Yorkers, it was the biggest collective outpouring of happiness in their lives.

Josh Hart, the Knicks’ small forward and shooting guard, celebrated with a family trip to Bordeaux, where he stopped at the famed châteaux Mouton Rothschild, Latour, and Petrus, among others. At Château Lynch-Bages, he was treated to a six-bottle semi-blind test, which he aced. He also tried a 1995 Château Margaux, which he said was the best wine of the trip.

It was a dream vacation for Hart, who’s been serious about wine for the past six years and posts the exceptional wines he drinks on his Instagram page, Jhart Cellars. At a time when the wine industry is struggling to connect with large segments of the population, his passion is educating new audiences and bringing more drinkers into the fold. He’s one of the most enthusiastic wine ambassadors in sports today, and with his ambitions, what we see on social media is only the beginning. That’s why Wine Enthusiast is honoring him this year with a special Wine & Culture Award.

But Hart wasn’t always a wine lover. He grew up in Silver Springs, Maryland, in a family that never had wine at the table. His breakthrough came in December 2018, when he was playing for the Lakers. He bought a bottle of 2009 Harlan Estate for a secret Santa gift exchange with the team. They beat the Golden State Warriors on Christmas Day, and to celebrate after the game, they uncorked the Harlan on the team’s airplane. Even though Hart hated wine at the time, he took a sip. “I loved it,” he said. It was the first time he realized wine’s super powers.

“It was powerful but still elegant. It was also when we beat Golden State on Christmas. So, I think it was the notes of the wine, but also the memories and the experience,” he said. “That’s what got me into doing all this.”

Obviously, I would love to come out with a wine of my own. The huge goal would be to own some land in Napa.

Josh Hart

Cut to 2026, and he’s a serious collector with a basement cellar stocked with bottles from the world’s most sought-after producers. While his collection is heavy on famous Burgundy and Bordeaux estates, he’s also been getting into Montefalco Sagrantino, specifically the wines of Paolo Bea. “He has probably four different wines, and I love them. It’s a really small region, very small production, and they’re amazing,” Hart said.

All told, he’s amassed over 1,000 bottles, and his collecting strategy has changed over the years. “I think for anybody in their wine journey at the beginning, you’re just buying everything, trying different varietals, different producers, seeing what you like,” he said. “And then as you start drinking more and more, you start to define your palate. So now I’ve gotten more into Amarone. Dal Forno Amarone is one of my favorite wines ever.”

When asked if there was a bottle that he’s never had but would love to try, he said Château Angélus. It was a delightful coincidence: When the Wine Enthusiast team went to his house to do the interview and shoot photos, we brought a 2023 Château Angélus as a gift, along with a 2023 Château Cheval Blanc. (Judging from his Instagram posts, we figured Bordeaux was a safe bet.)

Bucking the chillable, gluggable red trend, he’s also a fan of Rustenberg Wines’ Peter Barlow Cabernet Sauvignon from South Africa’s Stellenbosch region. “It was one of the best wines I had this summer,” he said. 

In August, Hart teamed up with the SuperVinoBros, media personalities known for their blind tastings, to shoot Instagram videos. Hart stumped them with the Peter Barlow. They returned the favor and blinded Hart on a red, and he guessed Cab Sauv from Napa. While it was actually Loire Valley Cabernet Franc, he was reassured it was a reasonable guess because the latter is the father grape variety of the former.

While Hart is clearly having fun these days as a collector and all-around wine guy, he has greater ambitions in the space. “I don’t know what my plans in the wine world are. I’m optimistic that I’m going to do something,” he said.

Three years ago, he launched a Napa Valley Cabernet Sauvignon called Bank Shot in collaboration with Maayan Koschitzky, but the long-term dream is a project that’s entirely his. “Obviously, I would love to come out with a wine of my own,” he said. “The huge goal would be to own some land in Napa. Seeing the prices now, though, that’s pretty tough.”

But in the immediate future, Hart is focused on his day job and winning another chip. Napa will be waiting for him.

South Africa’s Fine Wine Scene Has Grown Up with the Help of These Families

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

South Africa has been making wine for nearly four centuries. Yet the wine industry we know today is surprisingly young. How can both be true? For much of that history, winemaking was dominated by merchants and the cooperative system. Only in the last 50 years or so have estate bottlings and own-label wines given rise to a culture of fine wine.

The Love Story That Started It All

The country’s first crush took place in 1659. Another sort of crush blossomed not long afterward, between an enslaved woman and a German immigrant. Ansela van de Caab and Laurens Campher’s love story is intertwined with the genesis of the South African wine industry, helping create the mythology surrounding the historic Stellenbosch estate, Muratie.

The Muratie estate traces its origins to the late 17th century. Courtesy of Rijk Melck

In the early Cape, such relationships were not unusual; a European settler marrying an enslaved person, however, was virtually unheard of. Van de Caab (“of the Cape”) lived in a Dutch East India Company slave lodge and likely worked in the Company’s garden, where she is thought to have met Laurens, a soldier. They had three kids out of wedlock between 1686 and 1692. Laurens lived about 30 miles east of the Cape, in the Stellenbosch region, and would travel by foot or ox-drawn wagon to visit Ansela, who remained enslaved. After she was emancipated in 1695, Laurens brought her and the children to live with him at De Driesprong farm, which would later become the Muratie estate.

Courtesy of Muratie

Visit Muratie today, and it’s like the clocks stopped in 1699, the year Laurens officially took over the property. The wooded farmyard is enclosed by whitewashed Cape Dutch buildings. Sprawling across the cellar entrance is an ancient oak tree that’s said to have been planted by Ansela to bless their marriage. Standing beneath this tree in the dappled shade, it’s easy to imagine Ansela and Laurens in conversation, as they are in the play inspired by their lives, Ansela van de Caab. In one scene, Laurens declares, “One day, I’ll come and fetch you. I want to marry you and have you always beside me, next to me, and you must be mine.”

Ansela replies, “How can I belong to you when I don’t even belong to myself?”

The Camphers planted 8,000 grapevines on the property and started making wine. The farm passed through a series of owners before Martin Melck bought it in 1763. It remained within the Melck-Beyers family for 134 years and was sold off again; in 1987, Ronnie Melck bought it back.

His son, Rijk Melck, the current owner, carries the soulful air of a poet. We meet in the tasting room beside the cellar, which is draped in thick cobwebs. Portraits of previous inhabitants hang on the crumbling stone walls, including nude studies of someone’s mistress, Amber. She, like Ansela, has a wine named after her; in fact, all the wines bear the names of characters from the farm’s past.

Current Muratie custodians Rijk Melck and his wife, Kim, continue the family dynasty. Courtesy of Rijk Melck

We head outside, and Rijk leads me to the original homestead, built by Laurens in 1685. Inside are thick, rough walls of clay, mud brick, and stone, crossed overhead by heavy timber beams. Rijk tells me that while the Campher bloodline is extensive, no one in the family is currently working in wine. Remarkably, though, his nephew married a direct descendant of Ansela and Laurens.

Muratie is one of the few Stellenbosch wineries that remains in family hands. “If you think about what’s around us, who’s left?” says Rijk, referring to the recent spate of historic estates being sold to foreign entities or divided up for property development.

Bringing Bordeaux to the Cape

Head south from Muratie toward the Eerste River, with the glittering Atlantic in the distance, and you’ll find another Stellenbosch family that’s still holding fast. Here lies South Africa’s oldest, and one of its most respected, family-owned estates. Meerlust, Dutch for “pleasure of the sea,” has been in the Myburgh dynasty since 1756.

At the end of the long, oak- and palm-lined drive, the white-gabled manor house glows gold through latticed windows. I arrive as they are throwing a party to celebrate the 50th anniversary of the first Meerlust-labeled wine, a 1975 Cabernet Sauvignon. The tinkle of glassware and laughter floats on the soft night air, as it has for eight generations of Myburgh family gatherings here.

Children have chased one another through these rooms for centuries. In a way, nothing changes. It stays the same.

Hannes Myburgh, Meerlust Wines

The Meerlust farm was first established in 1693 and acquired by Johannes Albertus Myburgh in 1756. For the next six generations, the family sold wine in bulk, until a 1967 visit to France convinced Nico Myburgh that the estate’s maritime terroir was not dissimilar to Bordeaux’s. This revelation inspired Rubicon, a Bordeaux-style blend first made in 1980 with Italian winemaker Giorgio Dalla Cia. They named it for Julius Caesar’s river crossing, the “point of no return;” for them, it was a poignant coming of age. Released in 1980, Rubicon positioned the estate at the forefront of the country’s fine-wine movement.

Nico and Jean Myburgh of Meerlust Estate in 1967. Jean spearheaded the meticulous restoration of Meerlust’s derelict manor house and historic buildings, which culminated in their being declared national monuments in 1987. Courtesy of Meerlust Estate

Nico’s son, Hannes Myburgh, the current owner, is at the party. We’re in an old house, so naturally the topic of ghosts comes up. He hasn’t seen any himself but says a female specter has been spotted descending the staircase and rearranging the flowers in the vestibule. Hannes feels that the past generations are very alive here. “Children have chased one another through these rooms for centuries,” he says. “In a way, nothing changes. It stays the same.”

Hannes was, as he puts it, “promised to the land.” He says he had a carefree childhood, roaming the farm barefoot, catching fish in the dam. During school holidays, he was put in charge of irrigation in the vineyard. “It was damn hard work, but I loved it.”

After growing up in the family business, Hannes needed “free rein to become myself.” In 1976, he set out for Germany’s Rheingau region. Walking amongst vineyards that plunge toward the Rhine, something clicked: Suddenly he felt part of wine’s 8,000-year history.

He came home briefly in 1977 under sad circumstances: His mother was ill, and his parents were divorcing. The family stress prompted him to take a gap year in Paris, where he worked as a professional claqueur, someone planted in fashion-show audiences to start the applause. More consequential for his career was meeting members of the Rothschild family.

Hannes returned to Stellenbosch in 1982 and took over the vineyard, but went back to France the following year to work the harvest at Château Lafite Rothschild. This opportunity led to a watershed moment for both him and South African fine wine: He was invited to a dinner where Lafite 1961 was poured.

That bottle was his aha moment. “I experienced just how great wine could be,” he says.

His father died five years later, when Hannes was 31. “There I was, with the full responsibility of Meerlust,” he says.

Back at the party, in another room, someone drops a tray of wineglasses, snapping us back into the present. “Such a boring family, still living in the same old house!” Hannes jokes. “I’m 70 now, and having lived here all my life, this place is under my skin.”

Meerlust owner Hannes Myburgh with his constant companions. His pack has included as many as eight dogs at times. Courtesy of Meerlust Estate

Hannes has no children of his own. When asked the question of who takes over next, he smiles evasively. He reveals that there are some family members he is “really fond of.” In the cellar, succession has been rock-solid: Wim Truter is only the third winemaker since the first estate bottling in the 1970s. Hannes knows the wine is in safe hands.

‘You Can’t Make Good Wine in a Place That Looks Like a Hospital’

We move northeast now into the Simonsberg foothills, where Kanonkop, like Meerlust and Muratie, helped establish the modern Stellenbosch estate model half a century ago. Often compared to a Bordeaux First Growth, the winery has become a benchmark for age-worthy South African reds.

Kanonkop means “cannon hill,” and fittingly, a black cannon greets you at the top of the driveway. Centuries ago, the mountain peaks rang out with cannon booms to alert farmers that trading ships had entered Table Bay.

Unruly bushvines give way to old oaks on the approach to the cellar, which sits behind whitewashed walls. My visit coincides with the release of their 2023 Paul Sauer, arguably South Africa’s best Bordeaux-style red blend. After the tasting is a traditional snoek braai (fish barbecue), thrown by the estate’s owners, brothers Johann and Paul Krige, grandsons of former owner Paul Sauer.

A young Johann (left) and Paul Krige (center) of Kanonkop at the family table with their dad, Jannie Krige (right) in 1972. Courtesy of Kanonkop Estate

Like many people who grow up on a vineyard, Johann once thought about leaving the family business. “Two years after I qualified as a lawyer, I realized I was too honest for the profession,” he tells me as he works the grill.

Kanonkop was once part of a larger farm called Uitkyk. In 1903, politician J.W. Sauer bought the property and lived there with his wife, Mary Cloete Sauer, and their children, including their only son, Paul. Mary, already of famous wine stock, was the daughter of Hendrik Cloete, owner of Groot Constantia, the Cape’s oldest wine estate, established in 1685.

After J.W. died in 1913, Mary remained at Uitkyk, where Paul began farming fruit and wine grapes; he also got into politics like his father. In 1929, Mary sold Uitkyk to a German nobleman, but excluded 85 hectares of undeveloped land from the sale. She gave this portion to Paul, who named it Kanonkop.

Paul is credited with saying, “You can’t make good wine in a place that looks like a hospital.” In other words, a cellar should have character. While he vastly improved the Kanonkop visitor experience, the vineyard still operated on the coop model and didn’t produce estate bottlings.

Two years after I qualified as a lawyer, I realized I was too honest for the profession.

Johann Krige, Kanonkop Wine Estate

His daughter, Mary, inherited Kanonkop with her husband, Jannie Krige. In 1973, Jannie and winemaker Jan “Boland” Coetzee persuaded Paul to bottle a portion of the year’s yield under the Kanonkop name; a decade later, their son, Johann Krige, broke with co-ops entirely.

Kanonkop’s commitment to Cabernet Sauvignon and Bordeaux-style wines made a strong case for Stellenbosch’s fine-wine potential. The estate has also championed Pinotage, proving to the world that the South African native grape can produce wines of complexity and ageability. Their Black Label Pinotage, made from a single block, is a sought-after, small-production wine with many devotees. Johann, in his typically unsentimental way, says, “One does not set about making a cult wine; it is a status bestowed upon you.”

As Kanonkop grew in prominence, Jan “Boland” Coetzee (second from right) became a leading figure in Stellenbosch wine circles. Courtesy of Kanonkop Estate

Continuity in the cellar has been crucial: The reins passed from Coetzee to Beyers Truter to Abrie Beeslaar, whose two decades at the helm further cemented Kanonkop’s international reputation. Earlier this year, Dirk van Zyl was appointed head winemaker, bringing experience from the esteemed Glenelly Estate; he’s poised to make his mark.

“Nothing is promised,” says Johann of succession. “You have to work, prove yourself, be consistent.” Paul’s daughter, assistant winemaker Suzaan Krige, is doing just that. She is the first family member to ever join the Kanonkop winemaking team.

The fifth generation has arrived, and just in time. Urbanization is one of the greatest threats to South African wine, with housing developments eating away at prime viticultural land, taking pieces of these histories with them. Low grape prices, rising replanting costs, and the struggle to turn a profit are driving families to sell off their sprawling estates.

But with the likes of the Melcks, Myburghs, and Kriges championing both family and fine wine, you get the feeling the next act in this ongoing saga is a new beginning.

Before the first kitchen timer goes off, international trade duties have already driven up the cost of your holiday dinner. Between a historic shortage in American cattle herds raising beef prices sky-high, tariffs on tinplate steel inflating the price of canned goods, and foreign trade duties hitting European cheeses, wines, and sweets, hosting a holiday meal in 2026 can feel like walking a tightrope. I’ve been seeing these price hikes firsthand while shopping, and it’s definitely made me rethink a few of my usual grocery picks.

And while we can’t control international trade policies, we can outsmart them at the grocery store!

Your Guide to a Budget-Friendly Holiday Feast

Hosting family shouldn’t mean taking on a mountain of stress or spending your whole paycheck on dinner. With a few smart, budget-savvy pivots, I’m here to help you put together a celebratory feast that tastes incredible, looks festive, and sidesteps the latest markups on the shelves. Alongside my favorite budget-saving swaps and shopping tips, we’ve also put together a complete holiday menu that feeds eight people for around $45!

TL;DR: The 4 Smartest Swaps for a Tariff-Proof Holiday Table

Instead of buying the exact same grocery list you’ve used over the holidays for years, we found making these four simple adjustments will help keep your receipt total down:

  1. Ditch the canned goods: Tariffs on tinplate steel and imported aluminum have turned these former budget heroes into surprise budget busters. Buying canned pumpkin puree, canned green beans, canned yams, and canned cranberry sauce means you’re paying a hefty markup just for the packaging. Stick to fresh, bagged, or frozen U.S. produce instead. It’s cheaper, avoids the packaging surcharge, and often tastes noticeably better.
  2. Rethink the centerpiece protein: Avoid expensive beef roasts and foreign seafood this holiday season, both of which are carrying markups right now. Focus on domestic whole turkeys, bone-in turkey breasts, pork roasts, or glazed holiday hams. These proteins rely on local agricultural supply chains and are largely insulated from foreign trade taxes. We found whole turkey for as little as $0.85 per pound at Walmart, making it a fantastic budget-friendly centerpiece!
  3. Keep your dairy and drinks domestic: European cheeses like French Brie and Italian Parmigiano-Reggiano, along with wine and imported European butters, are carrying heavy import taxes. The U.S. produces both dairy and wine, so if you’re avoiding tariffs, they make a great swap. Switch to Wisconsin aged cheddar, Vermont goat cheese, domestic sweet cream butter, and West Coast wines.
  4. Focus on seasonal fruit, grain, and spice desserts: High tariffs have sent the price of specialty chocolates soaring, but we’re not giving up dessert! Lean into fruit-forward desserts like a pear galette or an apple crumble. You could also try an egg-rich chess pie, or any number of bread-based desserts. Other great options include sweet potato pie or pecan pie using pantry grains and domestic sweeteners.

“Kicking the Can” at Thanksgiving

A stress-free Thanksgiving dinner often relies on convenience canned products like jellied cranberry sauce, condensed cream-of-mushroom soup bases, and pumpkin puree. But in 2026, paying a premium for steel packaging is the quickest way to run up your total at the register.

Aside from straight facts about how tinplate steel and aluminum tariffs are impacting our grocery carts this holiday season, let’s talk about yield, which applies to pre (and post–let’s hope!) tariff times, too. For example, if you want to give 8 people an honest half-cup portion of carrots, you cannot rely on the canned carrot’s label touting that 3.5 servings are ready to be enjoyed inside. You’d need at least 4 cans, yielding roughly 5 to 6 cups total; at $0.96 per can, that’s $3.84, and over a third of what you bought was salt water and packaging.

On the flip side, a 4 lb. bag of raw carrots yields a solid 14 to 16 generous side servings. Even after peeling and trimming the tips (about a 10% prep loss), you are left with roughly 57 to 58 oz of pure edible veg. At $4.77 for the 4 lb bag, you feed twice as many guests for roughly 40% less out of pocket than buying the 6 to 7 cans needed to match that yield, paying around 8.3 cents per edible ounce compared to over 13 cents for canned solids, while skipping the canned brine (and tinplate steel and aluminum tariffs) completely!

Here is how to ditch the cans and build a fresher table for less money with just a little bit of extra elbow grease:

Make a 15-Minute Fresh Cranberry Sauce with 3 Ingredients

A can of jellied cranberry sauce seems cheap until you compare its price per ounce to a simple bag of fresh, American-grown cranberries. Simmering whole cranberries on the stove with a little sugar, a splash of water, and fresh orange zest takes less than twenty minutes. It thickens naturally as it cools, costs less, and delivers a vibrant flavor that makes the canned jelly completely obsolete. Our easy three-ingredient cranberry sauce recipe (shown above) has become my go-to.

Only need a small batch of cranberry sauce? Put the rest of those fresh cranberries to good use and make my sugared cranberries. They’re a delicious, festive, budget-friendly treat that doubles as a gorgeous garnish for your turkey platter and holiday desserts.

A little update from the grocery aisle! When we photographed our holiday menu on 10/22/26 for this blog post, Great Value fresh cranberries weren’t available at Walmart yet, so we picked up a 12 oz. bag from Kroger instead. But when we checked Walmart again on 10/26/26, they were finally in stock for just $0.76 a bag! That’s cheaper than a can of cranberry sauce AND a whopping $2.23 less than we paid at Kroger for the same size bag. Proof that a little comparison shopping can make a big difference to your holiday grocery bill.

This photo was taken on 10/26/26 at our local Walmart in Nashville, TN.

Swap Out the Green Bean Casserole

Traditional green bean casserole often relies on canned french-cut green beans and canned cream soups…a double hit of packaging markups! Instead, grab two pounds of fresh green beans from the produce section or a couple bags of frozen from the freezer section. Sauté them in a skillet with a drizzle of olive oil or domestic butter, garlic, and a pinch of other ingredients you probably already have on hand. It’s crisp, colorful, and feels like something you’d order at a nice restaurant.

Elevate the Bread Basket

Instead of spending $8 to $12 on a small wedge of imported cheese to snack on before dinner, make the bread basket your showstopper instead. Bake a quick batch of warm homemade dinner rolls or a skillet of golden cornbread, and serve it alongside whipped domestic sweet cream butter drizzled with local honey and sea salt. They cost pennies per serving, actually fill people up, and feel deeply comforting. Short on time? Don’t feel bad about grabbing a bag of ready-made dinner rolls at the grocery store like we did this time. At only $1.52 for 12 dinner rolls, I have no regrets…just extra time to spend with my family!

Prep Ahead to Keep Your Sanity

Prepped carrots, celery, and potatoes in deli containers with cold water.

Admittedly, scratch-cooking fresh foods takes more prep than opening a row of cans with a can opener, but that doesn’t mean you need to be glued to the stove all day. Make your cranberry sauce two days in advance. Peel and chop your carrots, celery, and potatoes the day before and keep them refrigerated in water. Then on the holiday morning, you’re just assembling, roasting, and mashing, not stressing!

Christmas & Holiday Entertaining: Luxury Without the Luxury Tax

Christmas dinner and New Year’s Eve often lean toward luxury imports like Champagne, ornate cheese boards, and expensive cuts of beef and roasts. You can still create an indulgent spread without absorbing tariffs:

1. Build an American Charcuterie Board

Spot the savings! The imported Swiss Gruyère on the right costs $7.97 for 6 oz., while the Wisconsin-made Gruyère right next to it is just $4.87 for 7 oz. That’s $3.10 less for an EXTRA ounce!

The days of carefree grazing boards might be behind us (for now…fingers crossed!), but you don’t need imported French cheeses or Italian prosciutto to put together an impressive board. The U.S. produces excellent artisanal cheeses! While we navigate this timeline with import taxes kicking us while we’re down, try a sharp, aged Wisconsin white cheddar, a log of creamy Vermont goat cheese, or an Oregon blue cheese instead. Pair your American-made cheeses with domestic cured salami, crisp sliced apples, local honey, and candied Georgia pecans. It looks gorgeous, takes no time at all, supports local dairies, and dodges hefty markups.

2. Pour West Coast Bubbles or Switch to a Festive Mocktail

If your holiday tradition calls for a celebratory toast, you might need to leave the French Champagne and Italian Prosecco behind. Luckily, California, Oregon, and Washington state produce exceptional sparkling wines at a fraction of the cost of imported bottles, giving you festive bubbles without paying international shipping and tariff penalties! If alcohol isn’t in your budget, period, swap it out and save yourself a bunch of money by making a festive mocktail instead!

3. The Real Math: Why We Retired Our Holiday Pot Roast

At Budget Bytes, we don’t typically build holiday recipes around $70 rib roasts, but a cozy beef chuck roast used to be one of our favorite reasonably-priced celebratory centerpieces. Historically, our absolute max to keep a recipe accessible on the site is staying under $3.50 per serving.

So, when we ran the numbers on our classic pot roast between 2024 and 2026 at Walmart, the math was eye-opening:

  • 2024 Beef Pot Roast (8 Servings): A 2.61 lb chuck roast ran $17.33. Combined with basic vegetables, bouillon, and seasonings, the whole pot cost $25.86, or $3.23 per serving.

Why This Strategy Works

This menu works because it centers on staple proteins and produce, featuring domestically raised turkey alongside hearty crops like potatoes, carrots, onions, garlic, and cranberries. I recently had to defend my Cosmopolitan at a dive-adjacent Manhattan bar. 

“It’s a classic cocktail, actually,” I told my friend, who smirked while sipping a whiskey on the rocks. I blathered on about Carrie Bradshaw and the Cosmo’s historic significance as an emblem of New York City, admittedly striving to downplay its pink, fruity hallmarks and prove it qualifies as a “serious” drink, whatever that means. 

Perhaps linking a “girly” cocktail to a “girly” television show (albeit a prestige HBO hero series) wasn’t doing me any favors. But as new micro-labels hit the streaming era zeitgeist, the latest being the female-targeted “white wine thriller,” it’s evident that both entertainment and the beverage world are still plagued by limiting gender stereotypes.

The Era of ‘White Wine Thrillers’

In August, The Hollywood Reporter published a deep dive on the trending subgenre, linking the origin of the term “white wine thriller” to an unnamed Peacock executive, who used it during a conversation about female-led thrillers.

Anyone who has seen All Her Fault, Big Little Lies, or The Perfect Couple—the latter two series graced by Nicole Kidman in a wig—have experienced the mildly hair-raising buzz of a white wine thriller.

“A white wine thriller can get dark but not too dark,” wrote reporter Mikey O’Connell. “The body count is kept low. … There must be aspirational elements, typically wealthy characters wearing Brunello Cucinelli while emotionally unraveling in Nancy Meyers-worthy kitchens.” 

ALL HER FAULT – Episode 108 Sarah Snook as Marissa Irvine, Dakota Fanning as Jenny Kaminski. Photo by: Sarah Enticknap/PEACOCK

Often book adaptations that center female protagonists and target female audiences, these series are alluring and sexy yet fun and palatable … just like the stereotype of white wine. “White wine feels lighter, refreshing, enjoyable,” Lisa Katz, president of scripted content at NBC and Peacock, told THR. “It goes down easy.”

Its edgier sister, the “red wine thriller,” is as dark and complex as an aged Napa Cab. She leaves a gripping sensation in the mouth, a bleak feeling that sticks with you long after the credits roll. With more kills and more blood, “a red wine thriller stains your teeth, it’s tannic,” a producer told THR, which cited Sharp Objects and Hulu’s 2026 hit Furious as prime examples of red wine thrillers with mile-long finishes. 

The phrase “white wine thriller” is lighthearted and whimsical, but it’s hard to ignore the Chardonnay misogyny of it all. Entertainment centering women often acquires labels that are rarely given to media geared toward men. Think about it, what’s the male equivalent of a “chick flick?”

Beyond the world of entertainment, people have long wielded white wine as a rhetorical tool to minimize the significance and complexity of women, all while disparaging their taste. From satire articles depicting a request for ice cubes in a glass of “Chardy” as a womanhood milestone to Fox News deeming the middle-aged women who protest ICE “Chardonnay Antifa,” white wine-related nicknames for women are seldom kind, let alone accurate. 

Some of the most complex, age-worthy, and collectible wines are white. I think it teeters into misogyny when the type of wine a woman drinks becomes a proxy for her sophistication, emotional stability, or seriousness.

Michelle Chen, sommelier

The idea that all women prefer beverages and entertainment that lean light and refreshing over dark and complex is a notion that women wine professionals would like to dispel. Behind its playful veil, “white wine thriller” is just another infantilizing trope. 

White Wine and Stereotypes

Despite the gendered associations, the language we use to talk about red wine vs. white wine is often rooted in biochemical reality. 

“In general, white wine is meant to be consumed earlier and is overall less complex,” Rustic Canyon Family Wine Director Kathryn Coker tells Wine Enthusiast. “Red wine is generally more ‘serious’—they are considered more chemically and structurally complex than white wines because they ferment with the grape skins, seeds, and stems.”

Red wine is commonly described as bold, tannic, heavy, and spicy, while white wine is associated with crispness, lightness, and fruitiness. These are vital wine descriptors with true meaning, but other wine language perpetuates gendered stereotypes: A strong red equals masculine, while a sweet white equals feminine. 

“Whites have often been described in tasting notes using more feminine-coded language, while reds are described as ‘muscular’ or ‘masculine,’” says sommelier Michelle Chen. 

To Chen, the misconception that white wine is “easy, unserious, or entry-level,” perhaps a novice choice, is absurd.

“Some of the most complex, age-worthy, and collectible wines are white,” Chen says, calling Riesling a god-tier food-pairing option. “I think it teeters into misogyny when the type of wine a woman drinks becomes a proxy for her sophistication, emotional stability, or seriousness.” 

In Coker’s 15-plus years as a sommelier and wine bar owner, she can attest that women drinkers contain multitudes. A woman may enjoy a floral Riesling as deeply as a powerful red blend, and neither choice is indicative of her character or level of sophistication. “There is absolutely no ‘norm’ for women’s go-to in terms of wine,” she says. 

While Charlotte Marshall, sommelier and beverage director of Nic and Junior’s Tavern in Chicago, has observed women wine drinkers gravitating towards Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc, she emphasizes that those happen to be “among the most approachable and widely available wines in the American market.” 

“Stereotypes have a place, and there’s data behind them, but staying too within them does a disservice to the guest and the wines,” Marshall continues. Indeed, there is some research to support the blanket statement that women prefer white wine, and it involves taste buds. 

Studies have shown that supertasting abilities appear in women more often than men. As for how that impacts wine preference, wine educator Dr. Liz Thach, MW, has noted that these “hyper-sensitive” tasters tend to avoid tannic and acidic wine, opting for whites and rosés. While research seems to indicate that many women gravitate toward white wine due to biological factors (a.k.a. superior fungiform papillae density), it hardly excuses any derogatory generalizations.

Natalie Dial, co-owner and wine director of Los Angeles’ Tomat, feels that the derogatory association between white wine or rosé and women is a distinctly American construct. 

Betty Friedan, women’s liberation movement pioneer and author of The Feminine Mystique, raises her hands in the air to make a point. October 23, 1981. Getty Images

“My guess is that the association between women and white wine is the modern-day equivalent of ‘suburban tranquilizers’ described by Betty Friedan in The Feminine Mystique,” Dial says. 

The 1963 book highlights the emptiness and discontent felt by many post-World War II American women, who were expected to find fulfillment in homemaking, modesty, motherhood, and marriage. Popping pill-form tranquilizers like Valium (a k a “Mother’s Little Helper”) and drinking alcohol offered an escape from domestic hell. 

“Once those prescriptions became more difficult to come by, of course we were reaching in the fridge for a cooling white wine to take the edge off of a long day jam-packed with patriarchy and repression,” Dial adds.

Chardonnay Girl Syndrome

Popular culture and entertainment certainly influence gender stereotypes in wine, and vice versa. “I think it’s a feedback loop between media and the cultural narratives that media then reinforces,” Chen says.

This interplay is painfully present in early-aughts film and literature. Renée Zellweger’s 32-year-old chain-smoking single gal Bridget Jones is the poster child for Chardonnay girl syndrome.

Bridget Jones’s Diary film still. Courtesy of Working Title Films

“I’ve failed again. I’ve poured an enormous glass of Chardonnay and I’m going to put my head in the oven,” reads one of her diary entries in the original 1996 novel, Bridget Jones’s Diary. The 2001 film adaptation of the same name is a complex piece of media when revisited; it was directed by a woman, Sharon Maguire, and the titular character herself is the exaggerated alter ego of her creator. Though endearingly pathetic and eternally relatable, a 136-pound, 30-something single woman who wishes to lose weight and “find a nice, sensible man” is a female character profile that has me needing something stronger than a glass of Chardonnay in 2026. 

The ABC (Anything But Chardonnay) movement of the mid-’90s spilled into the early-2000s, making it cool to turn one’s nose up at the buttery Napa Chardonnay that had oversaturated the market. By the time it became loveless Bridget Jones’ vice, the horn effect had already interfered and British sales sank.

“Chardonnay became associated with the single woman who is struggling and coping with her love life. Once that association was established, it became self-perpetuating,” Chen says of the romcom’s impact. This shallow association hasn’t entirely evaporated from mainstream culture, which is why terms like “white wine thriller” exude a similar level of sexism as “chick flick.”

To associate the seriousness of women to the seriousness of white wine, in this case Chardonnay, is already ridiculous, but it’s especially absurd given that some of the world’s most prestigious wines are Chardonnay-based.

“An oaky California Chardonnay might get stereotyped as ‘mom wine’ or saddled with more offensive terms like ‘cougar juice,’ while Grand Cru white Burgundy is treated as the domain of serious collectors, who are still culturally imagined as male,” she says. “In both cases, they’re drinking Chardonnay.”

The Horrors of Womanhood

The reality is, women are mocked no matter what they drink—Jones drowns her romantic sorrows in red wine, as well—or watch. Women are derided for being the predominant consumers of true crime, a gruesome genre that doesn’t exactly “go down easy.” If a woman can gulp a full-bodied Malbec while hitting “Next Episode” on a disturbing Netflix true-crime docuseries, then a white wine thriller can contradict the nickname it never asked for: they don’t always feel so fun and refreshing. 

Liz Meriwether’s red wine thriller Furious is a post-Epstein crime series that deals with sociopolitical issues like sex trafficking and domestic violence, with an overarching theme of female rage. Created by a woman, female-fronted, and undeniably feminist, the series’ red wine thriller label suggests its grisly elements somehow give it a masculine edge, as opposed to “lighter” white wine thrillers like All Her Fault and Big Little Lies, both of which centralize the nuances of motherhood and female friendship. 

A heartwarming allyship between Sarah Snook and Dakota Fanning’s All Her Fault characters may soften the blow of a child kidnapping mommy witch hunt story, and Big Little Lies’ central murder is tonally alleviated by its ensemble cast of A-list Hollywood divas portraying the Monterey Five, a group of suburban wine-guzzling moms. A cardigan-wrapped Kidman gripping a glass of wine had become a TikTok meme, despite her character, Celeste Wright, being a battered wife. Vulture even released a BLL drinking game in 2017, leaning into the obvious wine mom coping phenomenon weaved into the show. “Mommy juice” and “rosé all day” vibes seem to cancel out what would otherwise be a tannic domestic violence plotline applicable to the red wine thriller label. 

Media depictions of women and wine are always evolving. Chen suggests Scandal’s powerful Washington D.C. “fixer” protagonist Olivia Pope and her oversized glasses of Shiraz have broadened associations between women and wine.

In the end, I will happily watch Queen of AMC Theatres Nicole Kidman in a wig on HBO alongside a bold Nebbiolo. Another night may call for a chilled glass of Pinot Grigio in my feminine little hand as Lola Petticrew (also in a wig) murders New York City’s most vile men. There are no rules.

The FDA still has not named Trader Joe’s as the source of chicken wraps that sickened 50 people in an E. coli outbreak, but the agency has declared that the outbreak is over.

Neither the Food and Drug Administration nor the USDA’s Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) have named the grocery chain in their outbreak investigation reports. However, the FSIS confirmed for USA Today that Trader Joe’s was the source of the chicken wraps.

Both agencies are involved in the investigation because the wraps contain chicken, which is under the jurisdiction of FSIS, and the rest of the ingredients are under FDA jurisdiction. Both agencies say the reason no source of the E. coli has been revealed is that the wraps are past their expiration date and no longer available for sale.

“The implicated wraps were proactively removed from the market before the link to the outbreak was established. At the time of this action, illnesses occurred during one week in August. Since these products were removed from sale and the wraps people ate were out of shelf life, more detailed product information would not have provided consumers with an action they could take to prevent illness. FDA’s outbreak investigation remains active,” the agency reported in its outbreak update on Oct. 28.

The FDA reported that it was tracing ingredients, doing onsite inspections and testing samples, but it still has not revealed what is being traced, what locations are being inspected or what is being tested.

The USDA reports that it is continuing to investigate the E. coli outbreak, but has not revealed any details about that investigation.

There were likely many more sick people in the outbreak than the official count shows. This is because some people do not seek medical attention and others are not specifically tested for E. coli. The CDC estimates that for every confirmed patient in an E. coli outbreak, 26 go undetected.
