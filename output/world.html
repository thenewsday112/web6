We’re in the Era of the $200 Cup of Coffee

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →
Sey | Sey

These days, Bushwick is perhaps best known for its graffiti art, ecuavolley, and queer bars; the Brooklyn neighborhood is famous for its family-owned taquerias, American-Italian pastry shops, and the hallowed grounds of the Girls episode where Shoshanna accidentally does meth and runs around pantsless at a warehouse rave. Situate yourself at the heart of its largest park, Maria Hernandez, and you’ll see a kaleidoscopic blend of ravers, Latino families, and the baklava guy.

Starting this week, it’s also home to a $200 cup of coffee. 

Bushwick’s Sey Coffee recently announced that it would be touring a rare Panamanian geisha from Finca Sophia, a coffee farm in Panama’s Chiriquí highlands. Sey scooped up the geisha at auction for $44,275, which comes to $6,325 per kilogram, or roughly $200 per cup. The geisha is billed as extraordinarily aromatic, floral, and juicy; Doug Cohen, Sey’s director of brand strategy, advises enjoying it with a clean palate, completely black, and taking your time with it. “As the coffee cools, different aspects of its character become apparent, and that evolution is part of the experience,” he says. 

Even among fourth-wave coffee shops where prices continue to climb higher, Sey’s $200 cup is an outlier. “Coffee as a premium product is relatively new,” says Cohen. “For so long, coffee has been treated as a commodity, and its value has been constrained by that perception.” He says that both before and right after COVID, folks were becoming more curious about coffee, and educating themselves on platforms like Youtube, Discord, and online forums about how to appreciate it and “understand just how high the ceiling of quality can be.” The arrival of the $200 cup of coffee from a cafe in a neighborhood at the epicenter of conversations about both culinary innovation and gentrification feels ripe for closer examination.

So, how did this coffee end up at this price? “We have been purchasing coffee from Finca Sophia for several years,” Cohen says. “This release really grew out of that existing relationship.” When Finca’s general manager told Cohen that the farm’s yields were down by approximately 95 percent, Cohen says a bigger question was raised: “What does it mean to be a good partner when the people you have built relationships with are facing a difficult year?” Buying up the geisha beans felt like a way to both support Sey’s producer, and further develop what Cohen calls “elevated coffee experiences.” 

In the 2010s, the artisanal coffeehouse wave washed over the country, and especially Brooklyn, with hipster gusto. Sey Coffee was an integral part of that boom. Initially called Lofted Coffee, it operated out of its founders’ Bushwick loft until it rebranded to Sey in 2017, and expanded operations to its present Bushwick location on Grattan Street. That space has become something of a local icon since; Sprudge called its aesthetic “shoebox crossed with an airplane hangar”; the minimal-industrial, cream-hued interiors hit a very Axel Vervoordt-with-a-cyber-sigil-tattoo note (positive). The shop’s cachet has drawn coffee lovers, artists, yuppies, and artist-yuppies; customers have snacked on the gluten-free pastries from L’imprimerie (and now, post-L’imprimerie closure, pastries from Smør), and often post up with their dogs thanks to the semi-indoor-outdoor layout. 

Sey is hoping to elevate the coffee shop model to something more premium, with a stated focus on ethics and sustainability. Thus far, it’s earned acclaim for those efforts. Food & Wine named it the best coffee shop in New York and received positive media attention for opting out of tipping, choosing instead to pay its staff up to more than 80 percent above the NYC hourly minimum wage; full-time baristas can make up to $31 per hour in addition to receiving benefits and a share of the cafe’s revenue. 

Increasingly, coffee is getting the same treatment as high-end dining and tasting experiences. There’s the omakase-style menu at NYC’s Driftaway Coffee, or San Francisco’s Komakase coffee tasting room, both of which steer coffee away from quick consumption and toward a world of degustation that appears to have more in common with pricier wine and spirit tastings. The expansion of “espresso house” chain Watch House stateside is also an indicator: The London-based specialty roaster expanded to a fourth location in New York City this summer, raising eyebrows for its $32 cup of coffee while also reportedly scouting for more locations in Austin, Chicago, and Miami. 

The emerging demand may be just much about luxury as it is about craft. I can’t help but wonder if the $200 cup of coffee is happening in Bushwick because the Girls-era Bushwickian has grown up and wants more exclusive experiences. Is this what happens when, as culture writer Raven Smith said on his new BBC podcast last week, the indie outgrows its sleaze? “For us, this is not about pushing prices higher for the sake of it,” Cohen says. “It is about challenging how we assign value to coffee in the first place. What is an extraordinary coffee actually worth?” 

As much as we may lament climbing prices for specialty coffee and espresso, why wouldn’t this happen to the category when so many other aspects of food and beverage — from cocktails to sushi to bread — are following the same trajectory? Higher consumer costs notwithstanding, the marketing of specialty beans does steer U.S. coffee culture away from its productivity-focused consumption and the work-optimized “coffee break.”

“Why do we accept that certain wines can command thousands of dollars, yet question whether coffee can have comparable value?” Cohen asks. It’s a fair question, albeit one that I’d answer with another: Once we acknowledge that coffee should command a higher price, who still gets to drink it?

After a handful of lackluster releases, BY&I 2026 is a masterful return to form. I loved just about everything about this whisky, finding it hard to put down from the jump.

The nose enchants with a gentle layer of orange blossom, evident immediately, more blossom than orange and quite delicate in structure. What does whisky taste like before it touches oak? Technically, we shouldn’t even be calling this a whisky. Japan’s Nagahama offers an opportunity to explore the character of their lightly peated new make spirit, stripping away the influence of maturation and puts the distillate itself front and center.

What is New Make?

New make (or white dog in the American whisky world) is essentially whisky post-distillation, pre-maturation. The majority of the flavors you find in your glass are a result of maturation and its many variables, however, there are several production choices made before the spirit enters the cask that have an enormous effect on flavor, mouthfeel, texture, and weight.

Nagahama Lightly Peated New Make Review

The nose demonstrates a pronounced malty character, with fresh cereal sweetness and a faint smoky edge. This expression is lightly peated, but does not carry any aggressively medicinal notes. There really isn’t much more to share here, and I’d be lying if I told you I’m picking up loads of flavor here – but that’s exactly the point of this exercise.

At 59% abv, this spirit is to be approached with caution. The absence of maturation brings additional heat to an already-aggressive abv. In terms of flavor, the malt-forward profile is joined by the light smoke we discovered on the nose. On my second taste after dilution, some orchard fruit reveals itself, but without oak to contribute vanilla, tannin, or dried fruit richness, the focus remains firmly on the underlying spirit.

The finish echoes the malty and gently smoky base, with fresh fruit becoming more apparent as the alcohol is brought into balance.

New make isn’t for everyone, but it certainly can be a fascinating to whisky enthusiasts interested in production processes. Without years in a cask to soften the spirit, integrate flavor, extract from the oak, and develop complexity, the distillate has nowhere to hide. This bottle makes me wish it were easier to get hold of new make from well-known distilleries to achieve a deeper understanding of their house style.

This expression also offers a useful opportunity to experience peated spirit before oak reduces some of the impact.

Overall, this is an educational bottle rather than a conventional sipping whisky.

Brown Butter Financiers

Of all the pastries in France, one of my favorites is the financier. Enriched with nuts, and moistened with butter, every bakery in France offers their own version of them. They come in different sizes, shapes, and even flavors; almond is the most popular, but you’ll sometimes come across financiers made with ground hazelnuts or pistachios. I like them all. There are a few theories how this mini-gâteau got its curious name.

One is that, traditionally, they’re baked in small, rectangular molds. Once baked and unmolded, the little cakes resemble bars of gold, which appeals to people in the financial world. Another is that people in France are known to indulge in an afternoon sweet stop at their local bakery, called a goûter. Because people who work in the financial industry normally wear nice outfits or suits, something that’s neat to eat is appreciated, so they can stay presentable when heading back to the office.

I don’t have to worry about that, nor has anything come out of my oven ever turned to gold. (Quelle dommage!) But when I found myself with some leftover brown butter from infusing it in bourbon for Brown Butter Old Fashioneds, since I treat butter like gold, I repurposed it as a base for a batch of financiers.

Using browned butter keeps the butter flavor even more in focus. Some people get a little anxious when they see dark specks in pastries and desserts; I once had a waiter ask me what the dark flecks in the vanilla ice cream were. When I told him they were vanilla beans, and asked him (incredulously) what he thought they were, he replied, “I thought they were dirt.”

So after you brown the butter, when pouring it out of the pan, you don’t want to scrape up too much of the dark bits at the bottom of the pan, but I don’t mind a few in my cakes (in fact, I prefer them). So no need to fret about having a few in your batter. These cakes are also very forgiving, which is why so many bakeries in France offer them. They’re easy to make, keep well, and are the perfect afternoon snack – or as the French say, un snack.

For financiers, I use mini-muffin tins, which are easily available. I’m not a fan of silicone bakeware but know that some people like it. This batter is pretty forgiving so can be baked in madeleine molds or even in larger muffin tins, filling them only about halfway. If you use another size mold, you’ll likely need to adjust the baking time; bake them until browned on top, and the feel just set in the center when you touch them. As mentioned, I made these with leftover brown butter from the Brown Butter Old Fashioned recipe. I started with 4 ounces (8 tablespoons/115g) of butter, which yielded the amount called for in the recipe. If starting from scratch, and making your own brown butter, start with that amount of brown butter, then you can measure it out when it’s browned and cooled. Crispy rice treats are an iconic American sweet. When my family came to the United States, we had no clue that they were. We took them for an American version of a Vietnames puff rice bars called cơm nổ (exploded rice). The puff rice makers popped rice from scratch over a live fire in a drum that rotates constantly. After cooling the puffed rice, the cooks mix it in caramelized palm sugar then press the hot mixture into a wood mold. By comparison, the American version, Rice Krispies Treats, was so easy to prepare with breakfast cereal, butter, and marshmallows! Funny thing is that we never thought of tweaking the American recipe to give it an Asian twist.

That was about fifty years ago. Nowadays, people stir all kinds of add-ins to tweak rice crispy treats. One stand out recipe is chef Joyce Tang's Fancy Crispy Rice Treats. They are green from matcha and contain salty pretzels. Sweet-salty treats are a natural for Asian kitchen.

Putting an Asian Spin on Classics

Joyce's recipe, published in her debut cookbook, Bake Sum was born of nostalgia and perhaps even some boredom. Instead of making the same old Rice Krispies Treats, she boldy inserted her creativity to channel her preferences. In turn, she created a nod to her Asian American, adult palate. As Joyce writes in the Fancy Crispy Rice Treats recipe introduction:

During the global pandemic of 2020, I was one of many people who was moved to take stock and look at life differently. Stuck at home, I joined the masses in baking obsessively and finding comfort in nostalgia. In this era of bulk-buying dried beans and cereal, I made and devoured countless Rice Krispies Treats, which really brought me back to my childhood in a big way. This recipe is versatile; I've found that most types of cereal work well, but using smaller, similarly sized pieces yields the best results. In this "fancy" version, I love how matcha powder gives these treats both a unique green color and a slightly more grown-up touch of bitterness. Salty pretzels add nuance and texture too.

Joyce's cooking and book weaves together cultures and melts borders. That's how most of us exist nowadays so why shouldn't we cook and eat that way too? Fancy Crispy Rice Treats

An Asian American take on crispy rice treats, this recipe has been slightly tweaked from the original one published in Bake Sum, a cookbook by Joyce Tang (co-authored with Soleil Ho, published by Hardie Grant, 2026). Check the main post for extra tips from Joyce. I suggested swaps for a vegan version! Course Dessert Cuisine Asian American Keyword crispy rice ceral, crispy rice treats, matcha, not-too-sweet Servings 12

Ingredients

  • Cooking spray for the pan
  • 6 tablespoons | 85g unsalted butter (cow or plant-based), at room temperature
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 2 tablespoons milk (cow, oat, or otherwise), at room temperature
  • 7 cups | 420g mini marshmallows (standard or vegan)
  • 1 tablespoon matcha powder
  • 10 cups | 255g crispy rice cereal
  • 1 cup | 50g mini pretzels
  • 1 ½ teaspoons vanilla extract

Instructions

  • Prep the pan: Grease a 9-inch (23 cm) square pan with cooking spray and line it with parchment paper, allowing the parchment to extend slightly beyond the edges. This will create handles for lifting the treats out of the pan later.
  • Cook the crispy rice mixture: In a large shallow saucepan over high heat, melt the butter, salt, and milk together. Once melted, lower the heat to medium and add 5 ¼ cups (320g) of the marshmallows and cook, stirring occasionally, until they fully melt. (You can do this step in the microwave: Just use a medium bowl and blast it for 30 seconds at a time.)
  • Sift the matcha into the melted marshmallows and mix until combined. Allow the matcha/marshmallow mixture to cool slightly, about 1 minute, before stirring in the rice cereal, pretzels, and vanilla. (This brief cooling period helps prevent the treats from becoming soggy by allowing some steam and moisture to escape.) Stir in the remaining 1 ¾ cups (100g) marshmallows using a stiff spatula, wooden spoon, or, my personal favorite, a fiberglass spatula while the mixture is still warm.
  • Mold, cut and serve: Transfer the mixture to the prepared pan and press it down flat with an oiled hand or spatula. Let it cool for several hours before cutting. For best results, use an oiled serrated knife and a ruler to cut the treats into even shapes. They're delicious no matter how you slice them. Store airtight at room temperature for up to 3 days.

An authentic wheel of Grana Padano carries a signature brand burned into its rind—so you know that if you see a diamond pattern and the cheese’s name, it must have been produced in the Valle Padana (Po River Valley) of Northern Italy according to strict rules with roots in a 12th-century Cistercian monastery. At the time, the region surrounding the city of Milan was primarily soggy marshland. The monks who built the monastery—Chiaravalle Abbey—were followers of the Rule of Saint Benedict, which, along with devotion to prayer, mandated that they practice agriculture with a focus on responsible, sustainable use of land. They first figured out how to turn the marshy landscape into arable fields so they could grow high-quality feed for dairy cattle. Since milk (and fresh cheese) didn’t last long, the next challenge for the monks was how to preserve this nutritious, valuable food source to nourish themselves and their community.

The method they developed—heating the milk in copper cauldrons, adding rennet (a coagulating enzyme), cutting the curds into tiny pieces, and molding, salting, and aging the cheese—remains largely unchanged today. By producing the first iteration of what is now known around the world as Grana Padano, the monks of Chiaravalle Abbey earned the distinction of operating the world’s first cheese dairy.

Easy to like, portable, and a good source of protein, the cheese became an emblematic food, enjoyed by Italians at all levels of society through the Renaissance and into the modern era. Its iconic name, meanwhile, developed over time. The monks called it “caseus vetus”—Latin for “old cheese”—but since the peasants didn’t know Latin, they referred to it as “grana” for its distinctive grainy texture. As production spread well beyond the monastery, each province had its own grana: Cheese made in Milan was “grana Milanese,” and “grana Mantovano” was from Mantua.

It wasn’t until 1954 that the Italian government codified the name Grana Padano to unite the different varieties of grana cheese under a single wider geographical indication and that the Grana Padano Protection Consortium was established. In 1996, Grana Padano was awarded the European Union’s Protected Designation of Origin (PDO) designation. Today the consortium incorporates 134 member dairies, who together produced over 6 million wheels of cheese in 2025. In addition to safeguarding the name, the organization is also responsible for the global promotion of Grana Padano PDO and governs every detail of production to ensure that each wheel meets the strict standards established under the PDO. 

The specifications start with the cows’ diet: All fresh forage and at least 75 percent of dry fodder (such as hay) must be grown within the cheese production area. Raw milk must be collected within 24 hours of milking and be partially skimmed, allowing for natural separation of the cream. After a natural whey starter is introduced to the copper (or copper-lined) milk vat, calves’ rennet is added for coagulation. The cheese is then broken into rice-size curds using a spino, which resembles a large whisk, and packed into round molds, where it sits for at least 36 hours. The wheels are immersed in brine for anywhere from 14 days to a month and aged for at least nine months and up to 24 months or longer. Wheels aged over 20 months can be labeled Riserva.

Bite into a chunk of Grana Padano PDO, and you taste the sweetness of fresh milk coupled with a savory hint of toasted nuts. Riservas may be flakier and more crystalline in texture than younger versions of the cheese but never dry or crumbly, and they must still have a delicate flavor. Grana Padano PDO is an exceptionally versatile cheese, equally delicious on a cheese board, wrapped with a slice of Prosciutto di Parma PDO and drizzled with aged balsamic for a sophisticated appetizer, as a filling for ravioli, or grated over soup or pasta.

It’s good for you, too. Lactose- and gluten-free, Grana Padano PDO is low in fat and an excellent source of protein, calcium, and vitamin B12. A 30-gram piece (just over one ounce) has as much nutrition as a pint of milk, making it a beneficial as well as tasty solution to the “afternoon slump” for both kids and adults. Whoever you share Grana Padano PDO with, be sure to tell them what they have in common with Italians from more than 1,000 years ago—that they’re savoring a taste of European culinary history.

Want to capture the true heart of a Thai person? Make pad gaprao, a street-food stir-fry loaded with gaprao, sultry holy basil. It is fast, savory, and deeply satisfying-the ultimate any-time-of-day dish that locals crave.

I first had the bold, simple stir-fry on New Year's Eve during my first trip to Bangkok in the early 1990s. It was at a restaurant owned by Mechai Viravaidya, a Bangkok-based politician and activist known as the "Condom King" who used his humor and vast network to distribute condoms to combat the HIV/AIDS epidemic. He also owned a popular restaurant quirkily named Cabbages and Condoms. (The Thais know how to get your attention.)

What is pad gaprao? How does it compare to pad Thai as Thailand's comfort food?

Cooking Thai presents recipes, memories, and techniques that reflect the traditions and evolution of Thailand's cuisine. Here's and excerpt from "Sacred Wok Magic" which opens the stir-fry recipe chapter:

When you ask a non-Thai person what the quintessential Thai stir-fry dish is, their answer is probably pad Thai. But if you ask a Thai person the same question, their answer would likely be pad gaprao, the spicy, herbaceous, ever-so-adaptable stir-fry dish redolent with citrusy, pungent bai gaprao (holy basil, sacred basil, or tulsi). The stir-fry is commonly enjoyed with rice and is often topped with a crispy fried egg so diners can mix the runny yolk into the rice and stir-fry.

I once read in a Thai newspaper that the average Thai person eats pad gaprao at least once a week. That's believable because you can find pad gaprao set out in large trays at rad khao (over-rice) canteens, ready to be spooned over rice for a quick lunch. You can pretty much order pad gaprao with any common protein: chicken, pork, beef, and sometimes even seafood. The meat can be minced or cut into larger pieces. The heat level can be adjusted by request, sometimes by specifying the precise number of chilies you want in the wok. Your order of pad gaprao typically arrives with a little bowl of fish sauce with chopped chili (nampla prik). Sometimes the sauce also contains sliced garlic or shallot and a bit of lime juice. Diners add the sauce to pad gaprao to spike flavors as they please.

Personalization is key in how the pad gaprao is prepared, served and enjoyed. Given that, what to do about your herb choices?

Holy basil vs. Thai basil: Making the right swap

Most likely, you've seen pad gaprao at Thai restaurants but if that restaurant isn't in Thailand, chances are the stir-fry was made with Thai basil. It's actually Pad Horapa, prepared with bai horapa (Thai basil leaves). It works fine for this dish when I prepared it out of season. But when the weather is warm, I have a nice patch of holy basil growing in my garden so I can load up on this stir-fry. Regardless of the basil used, you need a lot. Pat and/or spin the herb dry to ensure their flavor blooms well in the stir-fry. Use the leaves and flowers.

The holy basil vs Thai basil flavor? Holy basil has a deeper earthy flavor that's very special. It's less sweet than anisey Thai basil. I've made this stir-fry with lemon basil too. Learn more about holy, Thai, and lemon basil in my Asian basil guide.

The "bougie" upgrade: Choosing the best beef steak for pad gaprao

Beef is expensive but you do not need much to feed four people with this stir-fry. Bougie Pad Gaprao with Beef | Pad Gaprao Neua

In Thai script, this recipe is written as: ผััดกะเพราเนื้. It as been very slightly tweaked from the recipe published in Cooking Thai, written by Pim Techamuanvivit with Andrea Nguyen (2006, Ten Speed Press). Be sure to see the above tips for using other kinds of Asian basil. Italian basil is not strong enough! Course Main Course Cuisine Thai Keyword asian basil, beef chow fun, holy basil, stir fry, thai basil oil Servings 4

Ingredients

  • Seasoning sauce made by mixing 2 tablespoons fish sauce 1 tablespoon Thai oyster sauce, 1 ½ teaspoon Thai black soy sauce, and 1 Tablespoon water (see Notes)
  • 450 g | 1 pound trimmed bavette New York, or top sirloin steak, cut into 1-inch (2.5cm) chunks (see Notes)
  • ¼ teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 big pinch of granulated sugar optional
  • 20 g | 3 tablespoons thinly sliced shallots
  • 10 to 15 g | 6 to 8 fresh Thai chilies to taste
  • 2 g | 2 teaspoons coarsely chopped cilantro root optional; see Notes
  • 40 g | ¼ cup garlic cloves
  • 90 g | 3 mini sweet peppers or other mild pepper
  • 30 g | 1 ½ cups fresh holy or Thai basil leaves plus tender tips for garnish
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil such as rice bran, divided
  • 1 batch Nampla Prik optional, see Notes below

Instructions

  • Prep the meat: Mix together the seasoning sauce ingredients in a small measuring cup. Spoon 1 tablespoon of the seasoning sauce into a medium bowl. Add the beef, baking soda, and the sugar if you want a touch of sweetness. Massage the seasonings into the beef.
  • Pound the aromatics: Use a mortar and pestle to pound the shallots, chilies, and cilantro root to a coarse texture (recognizable pieces of chili skin and shallot pieces are normal). Drop in the garlic, then pound to flatten. Scrape the aromatics into a small bowl.
  • Halve the sweet peppers lengthwise, then seed and cut on the diagonal into pieces ½ inch (1.25cm) wide. Set the aromatics, peppers, beef, remaining seasoning sauce, basil, and oil by the stovetop.
  • Stir-fry and serve: Set a large wok over medium-low heat on the biggest burner. When the pan is hot (to test, flick in a couple of drops of water; they should sizzle and evaporate within seconds), add 1 tablespoon of the oil, swirl the pan to coat with the oil, and add the garlic mixture. Let it gently sizzle, stirring continuously, to tease the garlic and mellow its harsh raw edge, 30 to 60 seconds. Take care to avoid burning. Return the garlic mixture to its bowl. Rinse and dry the wok.
  • Set the wok over high heat. When it is hot, add the remaining 1 tablespoon oil and swirl the pan to coat with the oil. Add the beef cubes, spreading them out in a single layer. Let sear until browned on the underside, 1 minute.
  • Drizzle about 2 tablespoons of the seasoning sauce around the perimeter of the wok, letting it sizzle and caramelize as it slides down the pan wall to flavor the meat. Flip the meat, dump in the garlic mixture, and stir-fry briefly to further cook the meat, 30 to 60 seconds.
  • Add the sweet peppers and the remaining seasoning sauce and cook, stirring, until the peppers begin to soften, about 45 seconds. Dump in the basil leaves and stir to incorporate and wilt, about 20 seconds.
  • Turn off the heat, transfer to a serving dish, garnish with the basil tips, and serve immediately.

Notes

Make Nampla Prik before you stir-fry and set it at the table. Let the shallot mellow and flavors bloom in the sauce. The Cooking Thai recipe for Nampla Prik is here.

This five minute Peppermint Bark is smooth, sweet chocolate layered with cool peppermint, and my boys can’t get enough of it. Make a batch for a party, or package it up as a gift for a friend.

Similar to the Christmas batches of peppermint marshmallow fudge that I like gifting to friends, peppermint bark is a cinch to make and requires just a few ingredients you may already have in your pantry. It’s perfect for packing into pretty tins or bags, or for serving on dessert trays at holiday parties. Once you see how easy this is to make this peppermint bark recipe, you’ll likely never be tempted to buy it again.

Peppermint Bark

I’ve been making this recipe as a holiday treat for friends and family for many, many years now. It’s even been a requested stocking stuffer for my boys over the years, and I’m looking forward to making it again this fall.

Sometimes, you’ll see a recipe for peppermint bark call for a full layer of white chocolate on top of milk or dark chocolate. But, I choose to swirl the chocolates together for a couple of reasons.

First, the two different colored chocolates all swirled and spiraled together simply looks prettier. And more practically, that swirl helps hold the dark and white chocolate together. There’s less chance of the two chocolates separating when you slice the chocolate into individual pieces. This way you get all three main flavors–white chocolate, dark chocolate, and peppermint–in each and every bite of peppermint bark.

Ingredients and Substitutions

The Chocolate – I use both white chocolate and semi-sweet or dark chocolate.

Peppermint – You’ll need real peppermint extract to add that unmistakeable sharp, minty flavor.

Candy Canes – Candy canes provide that iconic, seasonal topping.

How to Make Peppermint Bark

Lining the Baking Sheet: To start, I line a baking sheet with parchment paper.

Cutting the Chocolate: If you’re not using chocolate chips, you’ll want to chop the chocolate into small, easily meltable pieces,

Portioning the Chocolate: Place the white chocolate and semi-sweet chocolate into two separate microwave-safe glass bowls.

Adding the Extract : I add 1/4 teaspoon of peppermint extract to each bowl.

Heating: First up, we’re going to heat the dark chocolate in the microwave at 50% power, stirring every 30 seconds or so. Do NOT heat at full power. See my Expert Tip for instruction on melting chocolate in the microwave without scorching.

Pouring & Spreading: I pour the chocolate onto the lined baking sheet and use a spatula to spread it evenly across the paper.

Heating the White Chocolate: Repeat the heating, stirring, and pouring steps for the white chocolate. I use a spoon to drizzle the white chocolate on top.

Swirling: You can use a knife or the edge of the spatula to lightly swirl the chocolates together.

Crushing the Candy Canes: Place the candy canes in a sturdy zipclose bag. Then, use the back of a heavy measuring cup or a rolling pin to crush the candy.

Sprinkling with Candy Canes: I sprinkle the candy canes on top of the melted chocolate, while it’s still warm.

Chilling: You’ll want to let the bark sit in the refrigerator until it firms up. This will take at least 1-2 hours.

Serving: I like to slice the bark into bite-sized pieces, and then set it out for people to snack on.

Expert Tip

A microwave is the fastest way to melt chocolate with as little hassle as possible. But, that does come with the need to keep a close eye on the chocolate and use short intervals. I typically allow for 30 second increments and stir between each.

The most important thing to remember is never to heat chocolate at full power. I set my microwave at 50% power when melting chocolate. This method of melting results in smooth, velvety liquid chocolate.

Measuring Peppermint Extract – I never measure peppermint extract over the mixing bowl, always off to the side. Because just a drop or two more than needed and that lovely mint flavor can turn ”toothpaste” real quick.

Fully Melting Chocolate – You’ll know the chocolate has melted properly when it’s smooth enough to pour, but running a spoon through the middle of the pan still leaves a trail behind. There should be a glossy sheen across the surface. The chocolate should never become hard, grainy, or resistant to stirring: that is called seizing.

Swirling the Chocolates – Swirling the chocolates together not only looks pretty, but also provides structural support. While you’re cutting the bark into pieces, swirling will help hold together the white and dark chocolate together.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I use chocolate bars, or chips?

Since the chocolate will be melted, it won’t make a difference. If I’m using chocolate bars, I chop them into small, bite-sized pieces first, to make melting easier.

How thick should the chocolate layers be?

For a light, crunchy bite, I usually aim for layers about 1/8″ thick. You can redistribute the chocolate with a spatula if it looks uneven in the pan.

Layers up to 1/4″ will still set, but thinner layers are much easier to bite and break apart. Thicker pieces of chocolate bark can be difficult to comfortably chew.

How firm should the bark be after chilling?

Once the bark has had time to set in the refrigerator, it should feel firm and snappable. You’re looking for a satisfying crunch in each bite.

There are precious few food products that have been enjoyed for as much of human history as Prosciutto di Parma. The signature silky texture and buttery, sweet-savory flavor of the “King of Hams” is recognized around the world as the result of carefully controlled production methods, dedication to quality, and traditions continued for centuries.

The history of Prosciutto di Parma reaches back more than 2,000 years, to Roman times, when the art of preserving pork through salting and natural drying was already well established in the Po Valley. As early as the 3rd century BC, Cato the Elder described methods for curing pork legs with salt and allowing them to dry and mature. Today, Prosciutto di Parma is produced exclusively in a strictly defined area of northern Italy, in the province of Parma in the Emilia-Romagna region, where local know-how combines with distinctive climatic conditions. Humid breezes from the Tyrrhenian Sea become drier as they flow over the Apennines and down onto the hills. Prosciutto producers discovered that this created an ideal environment for dry aging, and they built storerooms with tall windows that could be opened and closed as necessary, allowing the air to circulate through the racks of hams for gradual drying.

The specifics of aging, like every step in the process, are governed by a consortium—the Consorzio del Prosciutto di Parma—that was founded in 1963 to protect the authenticity of the product. In 1996, Prosciutto di Parma obtained the European Union’s Protected Designation of Origin (PDO). These have a red and yellow seal to show that a product is made in a certain geographical area using time-honored, traditional methods. Grana Padano, a nutty, aged hard cheese also from Northern Italy that’s often paired with Prosciutto di Parma on a charcuterie plate, was awarded a PDO the same year.

Grana Padano PDO and Prosciutto di Parma PDO also share the distinction of being fire-branded—a sign for consumers that you are buying the real thing. Only Parma hams that meet the PDO’s specifications can receive the five-pronged Ducal Crown that signifies a true Prosciutto di Parma. 

Well before that brand is applied, production starts with the pigs. Which breeds, where they can be bred and raised (in 11 Italian regions), and what they are fed (a list that also includes whey from local cheesemakers) are all precisely detailed. Within the 28th day from birth, the piglets receive a breeder tattoo that will also appear on the final product along with the crown brand. The fresh pork (hind legs only) sent to prosciutto producers must weigh between 26 pounds and 40 pounds, with a set amount of fat coverage. After they arrive at the production facility, the legs are trimmed to form the characteristic rounded shape, chilled to a precise temperature, and salted by hand with sea salt, which joins the pork as the only two ingredients that go into the making of Prosciutto di Parma PDO.

Following a resting period of at least 90 days, the hams are washed then hung in windowed storerooms to dry slowly. The next step is called sugnatura: The parts of the ham not covered by rind are rubbed with sugna—a mixture of pork fat, salt, and pepper—to keep the meat from drying too quickly. For final aging, the hams hang in cool, dimly lit cellars. From start to finish, the process cannot last less than 14 months (many producers continue aging for 24, 30, or even 36 months and beyond). The last step before inspection may be the most fascinating: The hams are tested using a needle made from horse bone, selected because the bone is able to absorb the aroma and quickly shed it. By inserting the needle into different parts of the cured meat and smelling it, experts can determine if a whole ham leg has the qualifications to be named Prosciutto di Parma PDO. 

The Consorzio, which started with 23 producers, now numbers 125, and in 2025 they turned out 6.75 million Prosciutto di Parma PDO hams. Beyond its economic significance to the region, the “King of Hams” is a cultural icon and a tourism magnet. The annual Parma Ham Festival is held in September, and there’s even an entire museum dedicated to the region’s signature product—with a tasting room, of course. 

However you experience the exceptional flavor and texture of Prosciutto di Parma—on a visit to the museum or from your local specialty store—look for the crown and PDO seal, and take a moment to remember that you are tasting a distinctly delicious slice of history.

Making fluffy whipped cream without dairy seems like a foolish project but I've spent a total of about three (3!) weeks of my life trying to do it. The first two weeks were for a Vietnamese mocha cake recipe in my 2023 cookbook, Ever-Green Vietnamese. Many online sources pointed to using well chilled coconut cream but that method never worked for me. Nothing ever got fluffy, as you expect. I stuck with regular whipped cream for that favorite recipe in the book.

So, what was I looking for in the ideal whipped cream topping? It should be billowy and ethereal in texture, with a delicate creamy, rich flavor. That is easy to achieve with cow whipping cream (heavy cream) but using plant-based whipping creams can be tricky. I've tried a few and they did not work or had an off taste. Recently, I came across a method in Miyoko Schinner's super-useful book, The Homemade Vegan Pantry, that used coconut oil and soy milk. She's the vegan dairy expert behind Miyoko's plant-based butter and other products!

I tried Miyoko's recipe and the whipped toping was wondrously light, albeit a bit unstable after whipping. So, I manipulated it for a sturdy frosting to decorate a vegan strawberry and cream cake. Developing the vegan whipped cream frosting taught me so much that I returned to Miyoko's original recipe to refine and tweak it so you get this vegan whipped topping recipe -- which is lighter than frosting but sturdy enough to keep in the fridge for days! My husband, who loves regular whipped cream, adores this vegan version. He deemed it just as good as the conventional one. I hope you concur!

A vegan whipped topping for everyone

If you're a vegan, this recipe is for your dessert pleasure! Dip fresh fruit in for a simple treat. Spoon it onto a cake or pie for a rich garnish. If you're not a vegan -- this recipe is great for when you need a whipped topping but don't have regular whipping cream handy. It's a remarkable substitute.

What if you're allergic to soy milk? Miyoko suggests using water instead of soy milk and adding 1 teaspoon of guar or xanthan gum to the mixture. The result is a Cool Whip-style of topping. Below are my recipe development and testing notes for you to go at it yourself.

Why this vegan whipped cream topping works

Many recipes called for aerating chilled coconut cream but it's too dense to get fluffy enough. Here, the key is combining coconut oil with soy milk and cashews! Emulsifying them allows you to make a thick enough vegan whipping cream that you can successfully aerate into a whipped cream topping. Soy milk has enough fat and protein to support the coconut oil. Similarly, raw cashews add body and creaminess.

  • Use unsweetened soy milk made from soybeans and water. Keep it simple.
  • Select a soy milk that has a decent amount of protein -- about 9 grams per cup. I tested with West Life and Trader Joe's soy milk. Edensoy should work well too.
  • Select refined coconut oil because it is flavor neutral. When I made this with virgin, unrefined coconut oil, the whipped cream tasted like tanning lotion. GROSS.
  • Miyoko's ratio is 1:2 coconut oil with soy milk in terms of volume. I weighed the coconut oil to be consistent. If you weigh the soymilk, it should be about 240g for 1 cup.
  • To save money, use cashew halves and pieces.

Add-ins for vegan whipped cream topping

Aside from the coconut oil and soy milk, here's what the other ingredients do for this vegan whipped topping:

  • Sugar lends body and adds sweetness to the whipping cream. Do not cut back on the sugar because the cream will not whip up and thicken well without a certain amount of sugar.
  • Vanilla and salt add aroma and flavor. Salt amplifies the mildly sweet vegan whipped cream. I used vanilla extract. When I tried vanilla paste, the mixture barely whipped up.
  • Instant vanilla pudding mix or Dr. Oetker's Whip It stabilize the whipped cream so it will sit well in the fridge for days. They contain powerful modified cornstarch and other ingredients to absorb moisture and set the liquid mixture. Dr. Oetker's will yield a firmer, more stable whipped topping than the pudding mix. The pudding mix adds a pale yellow cast to the whipped topping. Buy Dr. Oetker's Whip It online or at Cost Plus (like I did!).

Watch me make vegan whipped cream topping

It's a miracle that these everyday ingredients come together for form a fluffy, light vegan whipped cream topping.

One final note . . .

Regular cow's whipping cream increases in volume as you whip it and aerate it. Vegan whipped cream does not increase in volume much. It does thicken and get billowy. It's rather magical. I hope you enjoy this recipe. Share your insights if you tweak it and how you serve it!

Vegan Whipped Cream Topping

One batch makes 2 cups. Use as much as you like per serving but for dipping or spooning, about ¼ cup (60ml) seems like a nice amount of richness. Weigh ingredients, whenever possible, for consistent results. Servings 8

Ingredients

  • ½ cup | 110g refined coconut oil
  • 1 cup | 240ml unsweetened soy milk, at room temperature
  • ¼ cup | 30g raw cashews (whole or pieces)
  • 2 ½ oz | 75g granulated sugar (about ⅓ cup organic or 6 tablespoons white)
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
  • ⅛ teaspoon fine sea salt
  • 2 tablespoons instant vanilla pudding mix or Dr. Oetker Whip It

Instructions

  • Make vegan whipping cream: Measure the coconut oil into a microwave safe measuring cup. Heat in the microwave on high power until just clear and melted, 30 to 45 seconds. Let cool to when it feels warm (about 100F). Pour the coconut oil into a blender jar. Add the soy milk, cashews, sugar, and vanilla. Whirl on high speed until pureed and creamy white, 30 to 60 seconds.
  • To ensure it is very cold and will whip up nicely, the cream should be 35 to 40F. Pour into a bowl, cover well, and at least 4 hours or better yet, overnight. (When in a hurry, transfer to a metal bowl and partially freeze for 2 hours, stirring 1 or 2 times.)
  • Whip it well: Use an electric mixer on medium-low speed to whip the vegan whipping cream. After a few minutes, when it starts to thicken (the beater tracks are a little deeper), sprinkle in the pudding mix (or Whip It). Continue beating, increasing the speed to medium or medium high. Beat until until soft peaks form, 5 to 8 minutes total. (If the cream doesn't start thickening during this time period, it's not cold enough! Freeze it for an hour or so then resume whipping.) Use immediately or refrigerate until serving time. Briefly rewhip if the cream seems a bit flat.

Ingredients

  • 1 ½ tablespoons lime juice
  • 1 ½ tablespoons fish sauce or Thai thin soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon chopped fresh Thai chilies
  • 1 tablespoon very thinly sliced shallot

Instructions

  • In a small bowl, mix together the lime juice, fish sauce (or Thai thin soy sauce), and Thai chilies. If using, stir in the shallot too. Let sit for a few minutes before serving with stir-fries, fried rice and fried foods. Dispense it with a small spoon because a little goes a long way.

Cooling and refreshing noodles are the Asian antidote for hot days. I have amassed a small collection of recipes for them and am adding this one from Japanese Comfort Cooking, a cookbook by Tadashi Ono and Harris Salat. They are two New Yorkers with good tastes and a realistic, cheffy approach to presenting doable Japanese recipes. I loved their past books, such as Japanese Soul Cooking, because I always learn from their approachable recipes. Moreover, they do not sugarcoat cultural truths.

This cold soba dish caught my eye because it's more substantial than the classic cold soba dish -- zaru soba. Whenever I eat zaru soba, I need to make other dishes as sides because the noodles and clear broth are not enough. Goma dare soba is loaded with veggies and seasoned with a protein-rich, salty-sweet broth. The broth is called "goma dare" and you may have had something similar with shabu shabu. Here it's plays a major role to define the flavor and texture of the noodle dish.

Cold sesame soba is easy to put together and delicious when you have homemade dashi in the fridge; make it vegan with shiitake dashi or use regular dashi. You can prep the broth, noodles and cucumber a couple days ahead, and then return them to room temperature and finish dish. Lunch or dinner is made. Below are some pointers for you to be extra successful with this comforting Japanese noodle dish.

Cold sesame soba broth done your way

Ono and Salat's original recipe is wonderful but in preparing the noodles, I made some minor tweaks. First, I halved the broth recipe. The authors say you'll have leftovers but so why over commit, in case you do not like the noodles? You can halve my halved recipe to serve two (or serve 4, if you're not brothy).

The bold broth for this cold noodle acts as a dressing of sorts. Like with many brothy Japanese noodles, you're not expected to drink all the broth. It is purposefully salty. Add as much or as little as you like to flavor your bowl. Any leftover broth will keep for days in the fridge so you can make more cold sesame soba! The adapted recipe below offers a range so you can have guardrails. The broth ingredients below are not exotic. Whenever you're in a pinch, make your own mirin with this recipe, because it will be leagues better than the sugary, bleh stuff at supermarkets.


Ono and Salat call for Japanese sesame paste which is not as readily available. Tahini is milder tasting and they permit it as sub. I used tahini and the flavor was very good. I opted for agave syrup for a rounder mouthfeel than sugar. When the vinegar and soy sauce hit the other broth ingredients, the broth curdles and separates a bit . That's the soy milk reacting to the acid and salt. Do not worry! Once the broth hits the soba, the starch in the noodles cancel out the separated soy milk and your noodles become wonderfully cloaked in silkiness. I used my homemade dashi for excellent flavor.

Noodle and veggie swaps

Aside from soba, Ono and Salat suggest featuring substituting ramen, somen, or udon. I imagine if you have capellini pasta, that would work too! The beauty of soba is that they are packaged as pre-portioned 3-ounce quantities. The Japanese are consistent!

Asparagus season is fleeting so when it's unavailable use green beans, snap peas, or carrots. I cut my green beans on a steep bias so they blend in with the noodles and be chopstickable. Blanch the veggie in the pot of water before cooking the soba. You'll save dishwashing time.

How to thinly slice avocado

The avocado lends richness to these noodles. If you want it to look good and not be mushy or clumpy. Here are my tips:

  • Make sure to use a firm-ripe avocado.
  • Halve, seed, then peel the avocado.
  • With the cut side facing down, quickly drag your knife through the avocado, spacing the cuts a good ⅛ inch (3mm) apart.
  • Use your hand to gently press, tilt and fan out the avocado slices to one side.
  • Transfer the slices to the bowls.
  • Do the avocado slicing before serving to avoid browned avocado.

Cold Sesame Soba | Goma Dare Soba

This recipe for 胡麻だれ蕎麦 has been adapted from the original published in Japanese Comfort Cooking: An Opinionated Guide toModern, Homey, Classic Japanese Recipes by Tadashi Ono and Harris Salat (© 2026, Ten Speed Press, an imprint of Penguin Random House). Course Main Course, noodles Cuisine Japanese Keyword cold noodles, soba, vegan Servings 4

Ingredients

Sesame broth

  • ¾ cup plus 2 tablespoon | 210ml Dashi, regular or shiitake (see Notes)
  • ¼ cup | 60ml Japanese sesame paste or tahini
  • ¼ cup | 60ml unseasoned rice vinegar
  • ¼ cup | 60ml mirin (see Notes)
  • 1 cup soy sauce
  • ¼ cup sugar or agave syrup
  • 2 cups unsweetened soy milk

Soba and toppings

  • 12 ounces | 360g dried soba noodles (4 portions)
  • 2 Persian or 1 small English cucumber, cut into thin strips
  • 4 ounces | 120g asparagus or green beans, blanched, cooled, and
  • 1 firm-ripe avocado, sliced
  • ½ small | 60g red onion, thinly sliced and soaked in cold water for 10 minutes
  • 2 teaspoons wasabi
  • 1 or 2 full sheets nori, snipped into narrow, short strips (see Note)

Instructions

  • Make the sesame broth: In a large bowl, whisk together the dashi, sesame paste, rice vinegar, mirin, soy sauce, sugar, and soy milk until smooth. (Depending on the package, you might need to knead the sesame paste a bit to get it all out.) Keep the broth in the refrigerator until you're ready to serve.
  • Cook the soba: To cook the soba, first prepare an ice water bath in a large bowl and set it in the sink. Bring a large pot of water to a boil over high heat. Add the soba and cook for about 3 minutes, or until it is cooked through but still retains some bite. Drain and dunk the noodles in the ice water bath. When the noodles are cool, drain them and set aside.
  • Assemble and serve: Divide the soba among four serving bowls. Pour ½ to 1 cup (120ml to 240ml) of the sesame broth over the noodles in each bowl. Top each bowl with equal portions of cucumber, asparagus, avocado, and drained red onion, arranging them in separate mounds. Add a small dab (about ½ teaspoon) of wasabi to each bowl. Sprinkle the nori over the top for garnish.
  • Serve immediately, encouraging everyone to mix everything together before diving in. Refrigerate leftover broth for up to a week to use again.

Notes

  • For the best flavor, make dashi yourself. Use my recipes for regular or shiitake dashi here. 
  • No mirin? Use this recipe to make some ASAP!
  • Instead of red onion, use a medium shallot. 
  • Instead of Japanese nori, use Korean gim, toasted seaweed sheets.
