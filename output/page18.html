Bougie Beef Pad Gaprao: A Premium Twist on Thailand's Favorite Stir-Fry

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Want to capture the true heart of a Thai person? Make pad gaprao, a street-food stir-fry loaded with gaprao, sultry holy basil. It is fast, savory, and deeply satisfying-the ultimate any-time-of-day dish that locals crave.

I first had the bold, simple stir-fry on New Year's Eve during my first trip to Bangkok in the early 1990s. It was at a restaurant owned by Mechai Viravaidya, a Bangkok-based politician and activist known as the "Condom King" who used his humor and vast network to distribute condoms to combat the HIV/AIDS epidemic. He also owned a popular restaurant quirkily named Cabbages and Condoms. (The Thais know how to get your attention.)

I've chased the same exciting flavors since then and have made Thai-style holy basil stir-fries many times at home. But I didn't know how to get excellent results until my friend, chef Pim Techamuanvivit, showed me how for our book, Cooking Thai: Heritage and Modern Recipes from My Kitchen Notebooks. Pim is a Bangkok native with three Michelin star restaurants in the United States and Thailand. Her outstanding balanced recipe is punched up with basil, garlic, chilies, and savory notes. It perfectly captures the exciting experience I had with my first pad gaprao in Bangkok.

Thailand has changed a lot since my first trip there, and Pim's version of pad gaprao is a premium rendition featuring beef steak. She cheekily named the recipe Bougie Pad Graprao. It is made with beef steak whereas the typical version employs ground meat. In this post, I'll share Pim's Bougie Pad Gaprao recipe from Cooking Thai along with our pro-chef tips for making smart holy basil swaps and buying good beef. If you're unfamiliar with pad gaprao, the explainer below, excerpted from Cooking Thai, fills in the blanks.

What is pad gaprao? How does it compare to pad Thai as Thailand's comfort food?

Cooking Thai presents recipes, memories, and techniques that reflect the traditions and evolution of Thailand's cuisine. Here's and excerpt from "Sacred Wok Magic" which opens the stir-fry recipe chapter:

When you ask a non-Thai person what the quintessential Thai stir-fry dish is, their answer is probably pad Thai. But if you ask a Thai person the same question, their answer would likely be pad gaprao, the spicy, herbaceous, ever-so-adaptable stir-fry dish redolent with citrusy, pungent bai gaprao (holy basil, sacred basil, or tulsi). The stir-fry is commonly enjoyed with rice and is often topped with a crispy fried egg so diners can mix the runny yolk into the rice and stir-fry.

I once read in a Thai newspaper that the average Thai person eats pad gaprao at least once a week. That's believable because you can find pad gaprao set out in large trays at rad khao (over-rice) canteens, ready to be spooned over rice for a quick lunch. Additionally, you can order pad gaprao at any tam-sung (as-requested) shop, the type of neighborhood corner restaurant that will basically cook you anything you want as long as the ingredients are on hand. And, yes, these spots will always have holy basil.

You can pretty much order pad gaprao with any common protein: chicken, pork, beef, and sometimes even seafood. The meat can be minced or cut into larger pieces. The heat level can be adjusted by request, sometimes by specifying the precise number of chilies you want in the wok. Your order of pad gaprao typically arrives with a little bowl of fish sauce with chopped chili (nampla prik). Sometimes the sauce also contains sliced garlic or shallot and a bit of lime juice. Diners add the sauce to pad gaprao to spike flavors as they please.

Personalization is key in how the pad gaprao is prepared, served and enjoyed. Given that, what to do about your herb choices?

Holy basil vs. Thai basil: Making the right swap

Most likely, you've seen pad gaprao at Thai restaurants but if that restaurant isn't in Thailand, chances are the stir-fry was made with Thai basil. It's actually Pad Horapa, prepared with bai horapa (Thai basil leaves). It works fine for this dish when I prepared it out of season. But when the weather is warm, I have a nice patch of holy basil growing in my garden so I can load up on this stir-fry. Regardless of the basil used, you need a lot. Pat and/or spin the herb dry to ensure their flavor blooms well in the stir-fry. Use the leaves and flowers.

The holy basil vs Thai basil flavor? Holy basil has a deeper earthy flavor that's very special. It's less sweet than anisey Thai basil. I've made this stir-fry with lemon basil too. Learn more about holy, Thai, and lemon basil in my Asian basil guide.

The "bougie" upgrade: Choosing the best beef steak for pad gaprao

Beef is expensive but you do not need much to feed four people with this stir-fry. Because the beef steak is cut as cubes and cooked very fast like Vietnamese shaking beef, select Wagyu or well-marbled steak for the most tender results. Pim and my choices include New York, top sirloin, and bavette. Note that bavette may be sold as "flap" at markets. Affordable top sirloin cap (also called picanha and coulotte) is also excellent. One pound (450 grams) goes a long way when cut into cubes. The garlicky, fiery seasonings will coat the beef super well to ensure good searing and flavor in each bite. In the recipe, a little baking soda helps the meat caramelize while tenderizing it a bit too.

Bonus tips for a sensational pad gaprao and menu

Some extra pointers that chef Pim and I offer in the book to help you succeed:

  • Don't have cilantro root? Just skip it. There's just a small amount needed and if you happen to have stubby roots attached to your cilantro, use it for a bit of extra complexity. Otherwise, there's no need to bother with subbing cilantro stems.
  • Use Thai condiments for the best outcomes. Get pointers on basic ones here.
  • To add a vegetable side, use the wok to cook it before stir-frying the pad gaprao. Heat the wok over high heat until hot, then add 1 tablespoon oil and swirl the pan to coat with the oil. Add 2 or 3 garlic cloves, smashed, to the oil and heat briefly to infuse the oil, then dump in a bunch of greens, cut into bite-size pieces, and season with 1 or 2 big pinches of fine sea salt as you swiftly stir-fry. Double the serving sauce so there's plenty for the vegetable side and main dish.
  • For extra protein, add a fried egg for each person to enjoy with the stir-fry and rice.

Bougie Pad Gaprao with Beef | Pad Gaprao Neua

In Thai script, this recipe is written as: ผััดกะเพราเนื้. It as been very slightly tweaked from the recipe published in Cooking Thai, written by Pim Techamuanvivit with Andrea Nguyen (2006, Ten Speed Press). Be sure to see the above tips for using other kinds of Asian basil. Italian basil is not strong enough! Course Main Course Cuisine Thai Keyword asian basil, beef chow fun, holy basil, stir fry, thai basil oil Servings 4

Ingredients

  • Seasoning sauce made by mixing 2 tablespoons fish sauce 1 tablespoon Thai oyster sauce, 1 ½ teaspoon Thai black soy sauce, and 1 Tablespoon water (see Notes)
  • 450 g | 1 pound trimmed bavette New York, or top sirloin steak, cut into 1-inch (2.5cm) chunks (see Notes)
  • ¼ teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 big pinch of granulated sugar optional
  • 20 g | 3 tablespoons thinly sliced shallots
  • 10 to 15 g | 6 to 8 fresh Thai chilies to taste
  • 2 g | 2 teaspoons coarsely chopped cilantro root optional; see Notes
  • 40 g | ¼ cup garlic cloves
  • 90 g | 3 mini sweet peppers or other mild pepper
  • 30 g | 1 ½ cups fresh holy or Thai basil leaves plus tender tips for garnish
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil such as rice bran, divided
  • 1 batch Nampla Prik optional, see Notes below

Instructions

  • Prep the meat: Mix together the seasoning sauce ingredients in a small measuring cup. Spoon 1 tablespoon of the seasoning sauce into a medium bowl. Add the beef, baking soda, and the sugar if you want a touch of sweetness. Massage the seasonings into the beef.
  • Pound the aromatics: Use a mortar and pestle to pound the shallots, chilies, and cilantro root to a coarse texture (recognizable pieces of chili skin and shallot pieces are normal). Drop in the garlic, then pound to flatten. Scrape the aromatics into a small bowl.
  • Halve the sweet peppers lengthwise, then seed and cut on the diagonal into pieces ½ inch (1.25cm) wide. Set the aromatics, peppers, beef, remaining seasoning sauce, basil, and oil by the stovetop.
  • Stir-fry and serve: Set a large wok over medium-low heat on the biggest burner. When the pan is hot (to test, flick in a couple of drops of water; they should sizzle and evaporate within seconds), add 1 tablespoon of the oil, swirl the pan to coat with the oil, and add the garlic mixture. Let it gently sizzle, stirring continuously, to tease the garlic and mellow its harsh raw edge, 30 to 60 seconds. Take care to avoid burning. Return the garlic mixture to its bowl. Rinse and dry the wok.
  • Set the wok over high heat. When it is hot, add the remaining 1 tablespoon oil and swirl the pan to coat with the oil. Add the beef cubes, spreading them out in a single layer. Let sear until browned on the underside, 1 minute.
  • Drizzle about 2 tablespoons of the seasoning sauce around the perimeter of the wok, letting it sizzle and caramelize as it slides down the pan wall to flavor the meat. Flip the meat, dump in the garlic mixture, and stir-fry briefly to further cook the meat, 30 to 60 seconds.
  • Add the sweet peppers and the remaining seasoning sauce and cook, stirring, until the peppers begin to soften, about 45 seconds. Dump in the basil leaves and stir to incorporate and wilt, about 20 seconds.
  • Turn off the heat, transfer to a serving dish, garnish with the basil tips, and serve immediately.

Notes

Make Nampla Prik before you stir-fry and set it at the table. Let the shallot mellow and flavors bloom in the sauce. The Cooking Thai recipe for Nampla Prik is here.

This five minute Peppermint Bark is smooth, sweet chocolate layered with cool peppermint, and my boys can’t get enough of it. Make a batch for a party, or package it up as a gift for a friend.

Similar to the Christmas batches of peppermint marshmallow fudge that I like gifting to friends, peppermint bark is a cinch to make and requires just a few ingredients you may already have in your pantry. It’s perfect for packing into pretty tins or bags, or for serving on dessert trays at holiday parties. Once you see how easy this is to make this peppermint bark recipe, you’ll likely never be tempted to buy it again.

Peppermint Bark

I’ve been making this recipe as a holiday treat for friends and family for many, many years now. It’s even been a requested stocking stuffer for my boys over the years, and I’m looking forward to making it again this fall.

Sometimes, you’ll see a recipe for peppermint bark call for a full layer of white chocolate on top of milk or dark chocolate. But, I choose to swirl the chocolates together for a couple of reasons.

First, the two different colored chocolates all swirled and spiraled together simply looks prettier. And more practically, that swirl helps hold the dark and white chocolate together. There’s less chance of the two chocolates separating when you slice the chocolate into individual pieces. This way you get all three main flavors–white chocolate, dark chocolate, and peppermint–in each and every bite of peppermint bark.

Ingredients and Substitutions

The Chocolate – I use both white chocolate and semi-sweet or dark chocolate.

Peppermint – You’ll need real peppermint extract to add that unmistakeable sharp, minty flavor.

Candy Canes – Candy canes provide that iconic, seasonal topping.

How to Make Peppermint Bark

Lining the Baking Sheet: To start, I line a baking sheet with parchment paper.

Cutting the Chocolate: If you’re not using chocolate chips, you’ll want to chop the chocolate into small, easily meltable pieces,

Portioning the Chocolate: Place the white chocolate and semi-sweet chocolate into two separate microwave-safe glass bowls.

Adding the Extract : I add 1/4 teaspoon of peppermint extract to each bowl.

Heating: First up, we’re going to heat the dark chocolate in the microwave at 50% power, stirring every 30 seconds or so. Do NOT heat at full power. See my Expert Tip for instruction on melting chocolate in the microwave without scorching.

Pouring & Spreading: I pour the chocolate onto the lined baking sheet and use a spatula to spread it evenly across the paper.

Heating the White Chocolate: Repeat the heating, stirring, and pouring steps for the white chocolate. I use a spoon to drizzle the white chocolate on top.

Swirling: You can use a knife or the edge of the spatula to lightly swirl the chocolates together.

Crushing the Candy Canes: Place the candy canes in a sturdy zipclose bag. Then, use the back of a heavy measuring cup or a rolling pin to crush the candy.

Sprinkling with Candy Canes: I sprinkle the candy canes on top of the melted chocolate, while it’s still warm.

Chilling: You’ll want to let the bark sit in the refrigerator until it firms up. This will take at least 1-2 hours.

Serving: I like to slice the bark into bite-sized pieces, and then set it out for people to snack on.

Expert Tip

A microwave is the fastest way to melt chocolate with as little hassle as possible. But, that does come with the need to keep a close eye on the chocolate and use short intervals. I typically allow for 30 second increments and stir between each.

The most important thing to remember is never to heat chocolate at full power. I set my microwave at 50% power when melting chocolate. This method of melting results in smooth, velvety liquid chocolate.

Measuring Peppermint Extract – I never measure peppermint extract over the mixing bowl, always off to the side. Because just a drop or two more than needed and that lovely mint flavor can turn ”toothpaste” real quick.

Fully Melting Chocolate – You’ll know the chocolate has melted properly when it’s smooth enough to pour, but running a spoon through the middle of the pan still leaves a trail behind. There should be a glossy sheen across the surface. The chocolate should never become hard, grainy, or resistant to stirring: that is called seizing.

Swirling the Chocolates – Swirling the chocolates together not only looks pretty, but also provides structural support. While you’re cutting the bark into pieces, swirling will help hold together the white and dark chocolate together.

Serving Suggestions

If you are putting a plate of this out in December, then there’s really only one thing to put out next to it. More peppermint! My vote will forever go to peppermint hot cocoa with a couple of homemade marshmallows, but you can also dress up a scoop of peppermint ice cream with a piece of two of peppermint bark. Make Ahead & Storage

Make Ahead: Yes! This bark will keep in the refrigerator for a good while. For that reason, I find that this is an excellent treat to make in advance for the holiday baking season.

How to Store: I store it in an airtight container in the refrigerator.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I use chocolate bars, or chips?

Since the chocolate will be melted, it won’t make a difference. If I’m using chocolate bars, I chop them into small, bite-sized pieces first, to make melting easier.

How thick should the chocolate layers be?

For a light, crunchy bite, I usually aim for layers about 1/8″ thick. You can redistribute the chocolate with a spatula if it looks uneven in the pan.

Layers up to 1/4″ will still set, but thinner layers are much easier to bite and break apart. Thicker pieces of chocolate bark can be difficult to comfortably chew.

How firm should the bark be after chilling?

Once the bark has had time to set in the refrigerator, it should feel firm and snappable. You’re looking for a satisfying crunch in each bite.

{5 Minute} Peppermint Bark

Peppermint Bark made with swirled white and dark chocolate and crushed candy canes. Five minutes of work and an hour in the fridge. Course Dessert Cuisine American Prep Time 5 minutes minutes Chill Time 1 hour hour Total Time 1 hour hour 5 minutes minutes Servings 12 servings Calories 372kcal Author Mary Younkin

Ingredients

  • 12 ounces white chocolate chopped or chips
  • 16 ounces semi sweet or dark chocolate chopped or chips
  • ½ teaspoon peppermint extract divided
  • 10 mini candy canes crushed, about 2-3 tablespoons worth

Instructions

  • Line a large baking sheet with parchment paper. Place the white chocolate and the semi-sweet chocolate into two separate glass bowls. Add ¼ teaspoon of peppermint extract to each bowl.
  • Place the dark chocolate in the microwave and heat 90 seconds, as 50% power, do NOT heat at full power. Stir and heat an additional 30 seconds at 50% power. Stir again until smooth. Pour the chocolate onto the parchment lined baking sheet and use a spatula to spread it across the paper.
  • Repeat the melting process with the white chocolate. Spoon the melted white chocolate over the dark chocolate. Use a knife or the edge of the spatula to lightly swirl the chocolates together.
  • Sprinkle with the crushed candy canes. Refrigerate until firm. Use a knife to cut the bark into pieces. Store in an airtight container for up to two weeks.

Pastina Soup (Italian Penicillin)

5 Stars 4 Stars 3 Stars 2 Stars 1 Star

4.9 from 10 reviews

  • Author: Lindsay
  • Total Time: 45 minutes
  • Yield: 6–8 servings 1x
Print Pin

Description

This soup has become my go-to recipe when my family is sick. The broth is amazing – simple, light, flavorful, and nourishing! It’s absolute comfort food. 

Ingredients

Units USM

For the Broth:

  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 3 large carrots, peeled and cut into large chunks
  • 2 stalks of celery, cut into large chunks
  • half of 1 yellow onion, peeled and cut into large chunks
  • 4 cloves garlic, peeled and smashed
  • 10 cups chicken or vegetable broth (I use 10 cups water plus 2 tablespoons Better Than Bouillon chicken base)
  • 1 teaspoon salt

For Serving: 

  • 1 1/2 cups pastina (I use Stelline from the Good and Gather brand at Target)
  • freshly grated Parmesan cheese for topping
Cook Mode Prevent your screen from going dark

Instructions

  1. Sauté Veggies: Heat oil in a large Dutch oven over medium-high heat. Add the chunky carrots, celery, onion, and garlic to the pot. Sauté for 5 minutes. 
  2. Simmer: Add broth and salt. Simmer for 30 minutes, until the vegetables are very tender.
  3. Remove Veggies: Use a slotted spoon to transfer the vegetable chunks to a bowl. Set aside to cool for a few minutes (you don’t want to add scorching hot veggies to your plastic blender). 
  4. Blend: When the veggies have cooled a bit, blend them with 1-2 cups of broth until very smooth. Add the blended mixture back to the pot; stir to incorporate.
  5. Pastina: Cook pasta according to package directions. Drain and toss with olive oil and a pinch of salt. 
  6. Serve: Put a scoop of pastina in a shallow bowl. Ladle the steaming-hot broth over the top, then top each bowl with freshly grated Parmesan.

Commonly Asked Questions

What is toasted sesame paste?

I love using toasted sesame paste for a deeper flavor. It’s made from sesame seeds and it’s just like tahini but it’s slowly roasted for a rich, delicious, nutty flavor. While you can substitute it with tahini which is similar as it’s also sesame-based, I’d suggest peanut butter which has a similar rich and nutty flavor profile.

Can I make sesame noodles for meal prep?

Yes, you can easily meal prep cold sesame noodle salad as meal prep. I’d suggest storing the julienned cucumber on the side and adding it to the dish right before eating to avoid any sogginess!

Are sweet potato noodles gluten-free?

Sweet potato noodles, also known as glass noodles, are naturally gluten-free as they’re made from 100% sweet potato starch. Another healthier noodle swap I love is Roasted Spaghetti Squash!

Can I add more vegetables to the BEST Creamy Sesame Noodles?

Sure! Feel free to add chopped carrot, thinly sliced bell peppers, spiralized zucchini, or any other veggies you have on hand. Try my Rainbow Peanut Noodles or Sweet Potato Noodle Japchae next!

How can I make sesame noodles higher in protein?

Make this dish protein packed by adding air-fried tofu on top or pairing it with another protein of your choice! I love adding edamame on top or on the side.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can this recipe be made gluten-free?

Yes! Substitute gluten-free tamari for the soy sauce.

Can I use regular (non-silken) tofu?

You can! Many restaurant versions of agedashi tofu are made with silken tofu, but regular tofu works too. It will be a little firmer and less custardy inside, but it’s also easier to handle if you’re new to frying tofu.

Can I bake my agedashi tofu?

I don't recommend it. The cornstarch coating needs a heavy, even distribution of oil in order to crisp up properly, and silken tofu is too delicate to toss or rub with oil after coating. In the oven, you're likely to end up with dry, powdery patches of cornstarch instead of the thin, crisp coating you get from frying.

How should I store my leftovers and how long will they keep?

Store your broth, garnishes, and tofu in separate airtight containers in the fridge for up to 3 days. To reheat the tofu, place the pieces on a baking sheet and in a 400°F oven until they start to crisp back up and sizzle.

Yat gaw mein, also known as Baltimore yak or dirty yak, is Baltimore carryout comfort food at its finest. A Chinese American remix of Beijing’s zha jiang mian, the dish dates back to the late 19th century, when Chinese immigrants started settling in the city. Setting up shop in traditionally Black and working-class neighborhoods, Chinese cooks adapted their recipes to locally available ingredients, swapping fermented pastes for a simpler, soy-based gravy. Over time, something new emerged: savory wheat noodles, stir-fried with shrimp, beef, and vegetables and punctuated with a hard-boiled egg. Today you can find the beloved dish at counter spots around town like Jimmy’s Carryout, Danny’s, and Pimlico Chinese Carry Out.

In this at-home version from Steve Chu, the chef behind Baltimore’s Ekiben, smoky wok-charred noodles are tossed with umami-packed soy sauce and oyster sauce, rich fat, and sweet caramelized onions. What makes the dish “dirty” is the flavor that develops when soy sauce, sugar, and aromatics scorch against the hot metal of the wok, creating deep savory notes balanced by subtle sweetness. Be sure to keep the wok on high heat and stir frequently, since this allows the wok hei—which translates to “the breath of a wok” and refers to that elusive smokiness—to work its magic. The finishing squeeze of ketchup might sound unusual, but it delivers the acidic punch that makes the dish complete.

Makes: 2–4 Time: 25 minutes

Ingredients

  • Kosher salt
  • 1 lb. fresh wheat noodles, such as lo mein or udon
  • ¼ cup duck fat, chicken fat, vegetable oil, or other cooking fat
  • 1½ cups bean sprouts
  • 1 medium onion, thinly sliced
  • 1 medium scallion, thinly sliced
  • ¼ cup dark soy sauce, plus more if needed
  • 2 Tbsp. light soy sauce
  • 2 Tbsp. oyster sauce
  • 1 Tbsp. MSG or chicken bouillon powder
  • 1 Tbsp. sugar
  • 1–2 tsp. Tianjin chile flakes or crushed red chile flakes
  • Steamed or poached shrimp or medium-rare seared beef slices, for topping
  • 1 hard-boiled egg, peeled, for topping
  • Ketchup and hot sauce, for serving

Instructions

  1. Bring a large pot of salted water to a boil. Add the noodles and cook for 1 minute less than the package instructions so they remain slightly undercooked. Drain, then rinse briefly under cold water. Set aside to cool. 
  2. Heat a wok over high until lightly smoking. Add the duck fat, followed by the bean sprouts, onion, and scallion. Stir-fry until the mixture begins to brown around the edges, about 1 minute. Add the noodles, both soy sauces, the oyster sauce, MSG, sugar, and chile flakes. When cooks talk about basil, they often mention the varieties used in the Mediterranean but basil is popular in many parts of the world. In fact, basil was domesticated first in India and Southeast Asia circa 3000 BCE. It took thousands of years and trade routes to bring basil to ancient Egypt around 350 BCE, and then to the Mediterranean. Basil's modern association with pesto and the like wouldn't have existed if basil didn't first arrive on the scene in Asia. In this post, I'll share some fun facts about basil and delve into three kinds of Asian basil: Thai basil, holy basil, and lemon basil.

From royal embalming to pesto: the journey of the "King of Herbs"

Basil got its start in India and through trade routes going eastward across the Indian Ocean to the South China Sea, variations of Ocimum basilicum (sweet basil) traveled to the areas we now know as Southeast Asia -- Thailand, Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia, plus Taiwan. Plant selection by local farmers resulted in specialized varieties. There are three main kinds of basils used in Asia today.

Thousands of years later, around 350 BCE, trade brought basil from Asia to ancient Egypt. Basil's preservative quality made it great for embalming bodies in ancient Egypt. Alexander the Great likely brought basil from Egypt to Greece, and from there migrated further into the Mediterranean. Basil's modern name comes from basilikon (Greek for "royal" or "king"). So it's the King of Herbs. During the 16th and 17th centuries, basil went westward in Europe and to the Americas with European colonists in the early 1600s. But, you're here for Asian basil insights to let's get to it!

Thai basil (O. basilicum var. thyrsiflora), the most common Asian basil

What's widely known as Thai basil (Ocimum basilicum var. thyrsiflora) was bred to withstand Southeast Asia's hot rainy, humid weather, have favorable flavors of anise, licorice, and mint, and have a sturdy stem that won't easily wilt! It became a cornerstone for Thai cuisine and southern Vietnamese cooking. Thai speakers know Thai basil as horapa and Viet speakers know it as rau húng quế. Most other people know it as the go-to Asian basil. 

Thai basil literally means cinnamon basil in Vietnamese but know that there is a cinnamon basil variety that is a different cultivar. There are over 40 cultivars of basil so it's easy to be confused! If you cannot find Thai basil, use dark opal basil, which has similar flavor.  Thai basil has a stronger flavor than Italian/Mediterranean basil so if you sub one for the other remember to use more (or use less!). 

  • Thai basil cultivars: What's sold as Thai basil at Asian markets has a thicker, sturdier stems and fleshier leaves than what is sold as starter plants at typical nurseries. The Asian market's Thai basil often has a stronger flavor. 
  • Thai basil uses: Add to Thai curry for aroma and flavor at the last minute. Serve as a garnish for southern Vietnamese-style beef pho. Stuff into a sandwich! 
  • Thai basil sourcing: Thai basil is sold year-round at Asian markets. Grow your own for the freshest. Seeds are available here on Etsy.  I buy starter plants from Southeast Asian markets like Battambang or Thien Thanh in San Jose.

Holy basil or Tulsi (Ocimum tenuiflorum), a mighty and precious herb

Holy basil has softly serrated leaves with a tiny bit of fuzz. It's one of the oldest varieties of basil, having originated in India. It was revered not as a mere plant, but as the earthly manifestation of the goddess Lakshmi. Its holiness was not just spiritual. Early Sanskrit medical texts identified tulsi as a major adaptogen which can help the body deal with stressful situations. Nicknamed "The Mother Medicine of Nature", was valued for treating respiratory conditions, fevers, and snakebites.

In Thai, holy basil is known as กะเพรา (gaprao) and in South Asia, it's called tulsi. Thai holy basil comes as green or reddish purple cultivars. Compared to familiar Thai basil, holy basil has a spicier, more peppery flavor than Thai basil. Some describe holy basil as have a punch clove-like scent. It's unique and distinct.

  • Holy basil and tulsi cultivars: Indian tulsi has slightly thicker leaves than Thai gaprao. You can use tulsi in the Thai stir-fry and vice versa for Indian tea.
  • Holy basil and tulsi uses: Steep in hot water for a calming, soothing tea that's great for sleep. Pluck off the leaves for the Thai classic - Pad Gaprao stir-fry. You'll need about 1.5 oz (45g) of holy basil leaves plus lots of garlic and chilies for one pound (450g) of protein (chicken, pork, beef, or tofu). Holy basil seeds are steeped for a thickish pudding like dessert (think chia seed like). Vietnamese chè hột é (chè hat é) features holy basil seeds in a dessert sweet soup with cooling grass jelly and other ingredients.
  • Holy basil and tulsi sourcing: Buy during warm weather season from Cambodian markets or Southeast Asian farmers who will bring them to farmer's markets. Grow tulsi and gaprao from seeds or starter plants (I buy mine from Battambang, a Cambodian market in San Jose). Grow it or buy it for this sensational Bougie Pad Graprao stir-fry.

Lemon basil (Ocimum × citriodorum), delicate looking yet flavor packed

Lemon basil has a lovely citrusy scent that may evoke lemongrass. The leaves are a lighter green than Thai basil and smaller, with delicate features. It's classified as Ocimum × africanum but most people know it as lemon basil, hoary basil, Thai lemon basil, or Lao basil.

Interestingly, lemon basil is a hybrid basil (Ocinum basilicum) and American basil (Ocinum americanum). The primarily grows in northeastern Africa and Southeast Asia ior its lemony perfume and flavor. Many cuisines employ lemon basil, from Middle Eastern, Indonesian, and Filipino to Lao, Malaysian, Indian, and Thai.

  • Lemon basil cultivars: Mrs. Burn's lemon basil is easy to grow but is not as aromatic as the cultivar grown by Southeast Asian farmers. Use extra if you've got Mrs. Burn's.
  • Lemon basil uses: Enjoy raw or stir leaves into a curry at the last moment. Lemon basil is delicate when the sprig is long. Use it soon if it's leggy.
  • Lemon basil sourcing: During summer, Cambodian markets seem to have a good supply of lemon basil, sold in bags or as starter plants. Hmong and Lao farmers grow it during the warmer months too. Buy "Thai lemon basil" seeds if you want the Southeast Asian variety. Persian (Iranian) markets often sell lemon basil too.

Asian basil uses and substitution in recipes

These three Asian basils have certain "proper" uses but the certainly can be used as substitutes. For instance, true Thai pad gaprao should feature holy basil because the herb defines the flavor and name of the dish. I've shared a premium recipe called Bougie Pad Gaprao from Cooking Thai, a book I wrote with Michelin-starred chef Pim Techamuanvivit.

But, I've cooked off-road to use lemon and/or Thai basil in this chicken and mushroom rendition of Thai holy basil stir-fry. If you use other basils, change the name to Pad Horapa (Thai Basil Stir-Fry), for instance!

Beyond the Thai repertoire, hearty Thai basil is fabulous with beef pho, but for chicken pho, I prefer lemon basil's lighter flavor. Viet people also eat Thai basil raw along with certain foods like hột vịt lộn (half-hatched fertilized duck eggs, aka balut).

For nasi ulam, a Malasian rice salad, you can use whatever you like! When making my grilled lemon basil chicken, you can swap out Thai basil for wonderful results too. There's so much more to basil than Genovese and pesto. Asian basils unlock more flavor opportunities for your culinary rotation.

If you’ve ever been starstruck by a perfect Cinderella pumpkin or a stately stalk of Brussels sprouts, you likely understand the appeal of a book focused on a single fruit or vegetable. While books like this aren’t always long, they typically go deep, with the fervor of an author who is fully obsessed.

One charming example, The Core of an Onion: Peeling the Rarest Common Food by Mark Kurlansky, melds authority with a dose of irreverence. First published in 2023, it’s a slim 200 pages, but the writer (who’s also written nonfiction books dedicated to oysters, salt, and cod) packs a lot in. “Onions have a way of taking on unexpected significance,” he posits. The first section covers the allium’s history and cultural impact. (Did you know that ancient Egyptians tied them to mummies’ feet as a preservative?) Kurlansky then offers loosely structured recipes for breads, sauces, and more, all centering the humble bulb.

Elevating a perhaps overlooked ingredient is a core tenet of single-­subject cookbookery. In The Compleat Squash: A Passionate Grower’s Guide to Pumpkins, Squash, and Gourds, a canonical 2004 ode to members of the Cucurbitaceae ­family, master ­gardener Amy Goldman Fowler’s passion is abundantly evident. “Sometimes I don’t know where pumpkins begin and I end,” she writes, before segueing into a series of squash and gourd portraits, each showing an individual specimen more striking than the last. Fowler shares recipes too, in addition to breed spotlights, resources for seed ­buying, and advice on growing your own.

Some such tomes aim to introduce the reader to the joys of less ubiquitous ingredients. Lane Selman and Margarett Waterbury’s Radicchio: A Celebration of the Beloved Italian Vegetable tells the “human and natural history” of the bitter chicory, along with instructions for how to grow it and cook with it. Selman is the founder of the Culinary Breeding Network, an initiative that rallies scientists, farmers, and chefs to geek out over new produce varieties. The book gets weird in the best way possible, with profiles of Italian growers and recipes including gnocchi with radicchio pesto, radicchio with prosciutto ­gratin, and chocolate–radicchio cake (why not?).

Radicchio reads almost like a community cookbook, and that’s intentional. Selman’s inspiration to pursue the topic was the sheer lack of writing on this oddball vegetable: “All of these people that we’ve met on our way down the rabbit hole needed to be included,” she says. “They’re the ones who have given us all this information. Sometimes it takes a village.”

Other single-subject cookbooks showcase pantry ­staples that are rarely in the spotlight. Anna Hezel’s Little Book of Olives: A Guide to Cooking, Eating, and Drinking for the Brine-Inclined presents recipes including cantaloupe and crushed-olive panzanella, Castelvetrano and pistachio pesto, and a whole chapter of olive desserts, plus shopping tips and a breakdown of varieties. The book’s retro aesthetic lends ­gravitas to an ingredient often relegated to garnish status.

Finally, some books exist more to dazzle than to inform, as is the case with pop artist Ed Ruscha and River Café chef Ruthie Rogers’s 2025 release Squeeze Me: Lemon Recipes & Art. In the pair’s high-gloss love letter to the lemon, pop art–coded photos with cheeky captions and a suite of minimalist recipes—such as lemon–­mascarpone tart and limoncello—make this ode to the simple citrus equally at home on a coffee table or the kitchen shelf.

While styles and forms vary, these books serve a ­common goal: to illuminate the sometimes surprising pleasures of a particular fruit or vegetable. That they manage to do so in such delightful fashion is proof that, once in a while, ­working with less really is more.

The hot is for veterans, and the extra hot is something the CIA should use to extract information. The crust is thin and it has the perfect amount of crunch, and the seasoning goes all the way through. But most importantly, every week we get the same order, and never once in 17 years have they messed up that order. I feel like a highly underrated aspect of a great restaurant is doing exactly the same thing 52 weeks out of the year, and I have mad respect for Rocky’s.

A Note on the Squash

I note this in a few places on this page, but it deserves emphasis. You can make this soup using butternut squash as well as a wide range of other winter squash, or even sweet potato. I love using red kuri squash with its dense sweet flesh and deep color. Kabocha squash is another regular go-to, along with acorn squash and adorable honeynut squash. I’m seeing honeynuts more and more alongside traditional butternut squash these days, they look like pint-sized versions.

For the Silkiest Soup

When I’m feeling lazy, I often blend soups like this using a simple hand blender. It makes for easy cleanup. And for this exact soup, the hand blender does a pretty good job – really smooth, but not hyper-silky. All of which is a long way of saying that this soup goes next-level when you use a high-speed blender, and the result is arguably worth the extra effort. But don’t be deterred if a hand blender is what you use! It can still do a good job, the photos here are of a version I made using an old-school hand blender.
