Publisher’s Platform: Radical Transparency? What I Am Asking the FDA to Do Is Not Radical at All

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has rightly made “radical transparency” the motto of his Department of Health and Human Services. HHS even has a page by that name. I think it is a good motto, and I want to help Secretary Kennedy and Acting FDA Commissioner Kyle Diamantas deliver on it in food safety, with two citizen petitions that ask for nothing the federal government has not already done, has not already said it believes in, and, as of October 26, has not been told by the Department of Justice that it has the authority to do. These petitions are meant as a hand up.

Before getting to the petitions, it is worth looking at where the phrase came from and how it found its way into food safety, because HHS and FDA put it there themselves.

Where “radical transparency” came from

The phrase did not start in Washington. It is a management idea popularized by Bridgewater Associates founder Ray Dalio, who built his hedge fund around what he called radical truth and radical transparency and pitched it in a widely watched 2017 TED talk. Dalio’s own summary of the business case is worth keeping in mind for what follows: if you are handling things well, radical transparency will make that clear, and if you are handling things badly, it will make that clear too.

Kennedy made it his own at his confirmation hearings. Before the Senate Finance Committee on January 29, 2025, he promised “radical transparency” within the department. Answering Senator Grassley, he said that if Congress wanted information, the doors are open. And he added a line every outbreak investigator should tape to the wall: “We should tell Americans when we don’t know.”

Three weeks later, in his first address to HHS staff on February 18, 2025, he gave the idea its philosophical core: “Only through radical transparency can we provide Americans with genuine informed consent, which is the bedrock and the foundation stone of democracy.”

At the start, the phrase was mostly about Congress, conflicts of interest and vaccines. It did not stay there.

How it moved into food, then into outbreaks and recalls

On March 10, 2025, HHS directed FDA to explore closing the self-affirmed GRAS pathway, and the press release said HHS and FDA “are committed to promoting radical transparency to make sure all Americans know what is in their food.” That is the first time I can find the phrase attached to food.

Marty Makary, then FDA’s incoming commissioner, brought it with him. In his confirmation testimony he described a generational opportunity to usher in radical transparency. Then, on July 9, 2025, FDA sent a letter to food industry leaders about recall communications that said this: “At the FDA, we believe that radical transparency in food safety and nutrition actions is key to reducing foodborne illness and chronic disease.” Read that again. Foodborne illness. Recalls. Radical transparency. FDA’s words, not mine.

The next day, FDA published more than 200 drug rejection letters under the headline “FDA Embraces Radical Transparency”, and in September it announced it would release them in real time, calling radical transparency “one of the guiding principles of this administration.” Those letters are redacted for confidential commercial information, but, in FDA’s words, they “will contain company names.” Hold that thought.

On September 24, 2025, FDA’s food program launched Executive Incident Summary abstracts for closed outbreak investigations. The constituent update opened: “The FDA is reinforcing our commitment to radical transparency to ensure Americans know what is in their food and that it is safe to consume.” Both the recall letter and the abstracts came out of FDA’s food program, led at the time by Deputy Commissioner for Food Kyle Diamantas. By then the phrase had become the administration’s brand more broadly; that same month the Senate HELP Committee held a hearing titled “Restoring Trust Through Radical Transparency.”

The people have changed since then; the commitments have not. Makary resigned in May 2026, and Diamantas, the same official whose food program wrote radical transparency into the recall letter and the outbreak abstracts, has been acting commissioner ever since. In August, President Trump nominated Dr. Heidi Overton to be the next commissioner. The commitments the agency put in writing are still there for its leaders to build on, and that continuity is an asset.

For my part, the place to start is food safety, where HHS and FDA have already laid a strong foundation in their own words.

The unfinished part

The outbreak abstracts were a real step forward, and FDA deserves credit for them. The one piece still missing is the piece the public most needs: who and where. Food Safety News reported that FDA has withheld company names in its mango outbreak summary, and Food Safety Magazine described a summary of a fatal Listeria outbreak in which FDA redacted all key details, down to the states where the patients lived. When Stop Foodborne Illness petitioned FDA in February to stop treating company names as confidential, invoking radical transparency, FDA answered that the names are protected by the Trade Secrets Act. That was an honest statement of the legal question the agency believed it faced, and, as I explain below, that question now has an answer.

The cost of that gap shows up in real outbreaks. In September FDA closed a romaine E. coli investigation and named no grower, no processor and no brand. In October, in a 50-person E. coli O157:H7 outbreak linked to chicken wraps, the public learned the brand from a lawsuit and a newspaper, not from FDA or FSIS – although FSIS later confirmed the source to a reporter (I represent plaintiffs in that outbreak). However, when FDA declared that outbreak over on October 28, its entry still did not name the brand, the maker or the states, and it explained that more detailed product information would not have given consumers an action they could take to prevent illness. I understand the reasoning, but it assumes the only reason to name a food is so people can throw it out. As I explain below, that is not the only consumer who needs to know.

Not who might have been the source, but who was

I want to be clear about one thing, because it is the heart of both petitions. I am not asking FDA to name every firm that might have been the source of an outbreak, or to publish working hypotheses while an investigation is still sorting out the facts. Everyone remembers the 2008 tomato warning that turned out to be peppers. I am asking FDA to name the firm its own investigation found actually was the source.

The 2024 romaine outbreak is the clearest example I know. That fall, an E. coli O157:H7 outbreak linked to romaine sickened 89 people in 15 states, hospitalized 36, caused seven cases of hemolytic uremic syndrome and killed one person. FDA closed the investigation with the word romaine and nothing more. When the traceback summary was first released, the processor, the grower and the ranch were blacked out. When it was later released without redactions, only after firms in the supply chain signed authorizations consenting to the release of their own information, the same document showed that the traceback had converged on one processor and one grower. There was no uncertainty to protect. The government had the answer the whole time, and the public learned it through records requests more than a year later, after the season, the product and any chance to act had passed.

That is the line the petitions draw: disclosure of what FDA has confirmed, through the same traceback and laboratory evidence it relies on to close an investigation, and not of what it merely suspects.

The two petitions

That is why I am filing two citizen petitions together.

The first, on outbreak firm disclosure, asks FDA to amend its regulations to say that the name and location of a firm is not confidential commercial information when epidemiologic evidence implicates a food in an outbreak and traceback or laboratory evidence, including whole genome sequencing, identifies the firm as the source of the contaminated food. That is the same evidence FDA already uses to close an investigation, and it is not a suspect list. It asks FDA to disclose implicated growers, processors and distributors whether or not there is a recall, and to finish the transparency initiative it started by publishing those abstracts without redacting firm names.

The second, on recall consignee lists, asks FDA to publish the list of retail stores that received a recalled food in every Class I recall (the kind that can cause serious illness or death) and to classify every food recall, and publish the classification, within a set number of days.

That is it. Neither petition asks FDA to reveal a recipe, a process, a customer price or anything else that is a genuine trade secret. They ask for who and where.

Why none of this is radical

Start with USDA. For meat and poultry, FSIS has published retail consignee lists in Class I recalls under a final rule adopted in July 2008, during the Bush administration. A consumer who bought recalled ground beef can find out whether their store got it. A consumer who bought recalled cheese, sprouts or bagged salad usually cannot. Eighteen years on, the beef and poultry industries are still standing.

Congress noticed the gap. In the Food Safety Modernization Act of 2011, it told FDA, in its new mandatory recall provision, to consider USDA’s consignee policy. FDA did and wrote a guidance document saying it would publish consignee lists in some cases.

FDA itself has named farms, processors and brands in the same circumstances it faces today, at least eight times since 2006. States do it routinely.

And then there are those drug rejection letters. FDA has already decided, under the banner of radical transparency, that a drug company’s name is not the confidential part of a letter rejecting its application. Applying that same principle to food would bring the agency’s two halves into line: if a pharmaceutical company’s name can be published with a failed application, the name of a farm that whole genome sequencing has tied to children on dialysis can be too.

None of this is radical. It is a proven practice waiting to be adopted.

Good for consumers

Kennedy said radical transparency is how Americans get “genuine informed consent.” You cannot consent to anything about a food when the government knows who made it and will not say. A named firm and a published store list let a family check their refrigerator, let a doctor connect a patient’s illness to a source, and let a health department find cases it would otherwise miss. Over time, names build a track record, so consumers can tell which companies keep turning up in outbreak after outbreak.

Gross deception of the consumer, and who the consumer is

Section 705(b) of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act lets FDA share information about food in situations involving, in the Commissioner’s opinion, imminent danger to health or gross deception of the consumer. Most of the conversation about outbreak disclosure has focused on the first of those, imminent danger. The second deserves just as much attention, and I think it is the more natural fit for what these petitions ask.

Think about who the consumer is. It is the mom and dad in the grocery aisle trying to buy what is safe for their kids. It is also the buyer for Walmart or Taco Bell, or for a school district or a hospital, deciding which supplier to keep and which to drop. Every one of them makes that decision on the information available. When FDA has confirmed which company’s food made people sick and that fact stays in the file, each of those decisions is made on a record the government knows is materially incomplete. The parents cannot steer around a brand, and the buyer cannot weigh a supplier’s record, that FDA has already identified.

Deception does not have to be a false statement; an omission can mislead just as surely. A commodity-only advisory that leaves shoppers blaming every grower of a crop, a firm publicly denying it was the source while the agency’s traceback converged on it, or an outbreak table that still lists a food as “suspect” after a federal agency has confirmed it to the press each leaves consumers with a false picture that FDA holds the record to correct. And unlike imminent danger, deception does not end when people stop getting sick. The parent and the buyer are still making choices next season.

The limiting principle is FDA’s own knowledge. This ground reaches what the agency has confirmed, not its working hypotheses, which is exactly the line the petitions draw. I wrote about this in more detail when FDA closed the chicken wrap outbreak, and I have revised both petitions to rest on it.

Good for business

This is the part the industry should hear most clearly. When the government names a commodity and stops, consumers avoid the whole commodity. During this summer’s Cyclospora outbreak, Numerator data reported by Grocery Dive showed that 6.5 million households stopped buying salad mixes and kits in a single month. That cost fell on every grower and processor in the category, including the ones who did everything right. In 2008, tomato growers lost a season to a Salmonella outbreak that turned out to be peppers. Anonymity does not prevent harm; it spreads one firm’s harm across an entire industry.

A name is the only thing that separates the firm that caused an outbreak from the competitors who did not. It lets a buyer at a national chain weigh a supplier’s history the way a parent weighs a brand, lets insurers price risk and gives every firm a reason to find its problem before next season. Ray Dalio would recognize the logic: if you are running a safe operation, transparency makes that clear.

Good for HHS and FDA

The agencies promised to rebuild public trust. Few things would build trust faster than the public hearing the name of an implicated firm from FDA first, rather than from a court filing or a reporter. A clear rule also gets FDA out of the business of making ad hoc, case-by-case judgments that look inconsistent from the outside, and it makes the food program consistent with what FDA is already doing for drugs. It lets the agency finish an initiative it launched itself. And it is a concrete, announceable win: no new statute, no new appropriation, a rule FDA can write on its own.

And now DOJ says FDA has the authority

The sticking point for FDA has been legal, and it has been a fair one: the agency has said the Trade Secrets Act ties its hands. On October 26, 2026, the Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel answered that argument in an opinion addressed to HHS.

OLC agreed that FDA’s current regulations generally do not authorize disclosure of supply chain information, so the Trade Secrets Act largely forbids it, and that the existing route through 21 CFR 20.91 is exceedingly narrow in practice. But it concluded that section 705(b) of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, together with FDA’s rulemaking authority in section 701(a), permits the agency to amend its regulations to authorize disclosure of the names and locations of firms in a supply chain in situations involving imminent danger to health or gross deception of the consumer. Disclosure under such a regulation, OLC said, would be authorized by law and would not violate the Trade Secrets Act.

OLC also drew a line: a section 705(b) regulation authorizing disclosure after an outbreak has already ended would likely be inconsistent with the statute. That is imminent-danger logic, since an outbreak’s danger ends. But OLC treated the two statutory conditions as independent, allowing a regulation that permits disclosure when either one is met, and deception of the consumer does not end with the last illness. OLC did not decide whether its timing limit reaches the deception ground, and I have said so in the petition. The petitions also rest on a separate rule that a firm’s name and location simply is not confidential commercial information in the first place, so FDA has more than one sound path.

In other words, the obstacle has never been the statute. It has been FDA’s own regulations, and the Department’s lawyers have now told HHS that FDA can change them. The petitions ask it to.

Secretary Kennedy, Acting Commissioner Diamantas, Dr. Overton

HHS and FDA have already done the hard part: they made the commitment, and they put it in writing for food, in the GRAS announcement, in the recall letter to industry and in the launch of the outbreak abstracts. Mr. Diamantas, those last two came from the food program you ran. These petitions offer a way to complete that work and put your name on the result. You are also a lawyer, the first to lead the agency, and you will recognize that the OLC opinion clears the legal path the agency has long said it needed. Dr. Overton, if you are confirmed, this would be a meaningful early win to carry forward. And none of it requires anything radical. It is ordinary transparency, the kind USDA has practiced since 2008, that FDA has used at least eight times, that Congress asked you to consider in 2011, that you already apply to drug companies, and that the Justice Department says you have the authority to adopt.

Secretary Kennedy said we should tell Americans when we don’t know. These petitions ask for the other half of that promise, telling them when we do. I hope HHS and FDA will take it, and I am glad to help however I can.

New community fundraising initiative launches at 18 restaurants, connecting neighborhood schools, nonprofits and local organizations with opportunities to raise funds while bringing people together over great food

Dallas, TX  (RestaurantNews.com)  Rusty Taco the fast-casual restaurant brand known for its fresh, street-style tacos, welcoming atmosphere and neighborhood roots, is strengthening its commitment to community engagement through a new partnership with Force4Good, a technology platform designed to make local fundraising easier, more accessible and more effective for restaurants and the organizations they support.

Launched in October 2026 across 18 participating Rusty Taco restaurants, the initiative creates new opportunities for local schools, youth sports teams, nonprofits and community organizations to connect with Rusty Taco franchisees and host fundraising events that bring people together for a shared purpose.

The partnership represents an important step in Rusty Taco’s ongoing commitment to supporting the neighborhoods it serves, while giving franchise operators additional resources to establish meaningful relationships with local organizations.

“Rusty Taco has always been about more than great tacos. It’s about creating a place where people feel welcome, where neighbors connect and where communities come together,” said Michael Meche, President of Rusty Taco. “Our partnership with Force4Good gives our franchisees a simple, sustainable way to turn those values into action. Whether it’s helping a local school raise funds, supporting a youth sports team or partnering with a neighborhood nonprofit, we want Rusty Taco to be a place that makes a positive difference.”

Making Community Fundraising Easier and More Accessible

Through Force4Good, participating Rusty Taco restaurants can streamline the entire fundraising process, from coordinating events and promoting them to tracking sales and managing fundraising administration.

The platform helps eliminate many of the time-consuming logistical challenges traditionally associated with restaurant fundraising events, making it easier for franchisees to support local causes while remaining focused on delivering a great guest experience. For participating organizations, the initiative offers an accessible way to connect with a neighborhood restaurant, engage supporters and raise funds through community dining events.

The partnership is designed to provide several benefits:

  • For local schools and nonprofits: Opportunities to organize restaurant-based fundraising events that bring together families, supporters and community members.
  • For Rusty Taco franchisees: A repeatable local marketing and community engagement program that simplifies planning, promotion and reporting.
  • For restaurant guests: New opportunities to support causes that matter to them while enjoying their favorite Rusty Taco menu items.
  • For local communities: A collaborative approach to strengthening relationships between neighborhood businesses and the organizations working to make a difference.

By simplifying the fundraising experience, Rusty Taco hopes to encourage more frequent and meaningful collaboration between participating restaurants and the communities they serve.

A Proven Platform for Community Engagement

Force4Good works with thousands of restaurant and retail locations to make fundraising and community partnerships easier to organize and measure. According to Force4Good’s published platform statistics, more than 9,500 retail locations use its community giving technology, while more than 45,000 nonprofit organizations are part of its fundraising network.

Force4Good also reports that its marketing programs helped secure more than 2,250 fundraising events in 2025, generating approximately $1.77 million in restaurant sales across participating businesses. These platform-wide results demonstrate the opportunity for restaurants to combine community support with customer engagement and sustainable local marketing.

For Rusty Taco, the partnership provides the infrastructure to develop those opportunities across its franchise network, creating a more consistent approach to community involvement while preserving each restaurant’s ability to build relationships with organizations in its own neighborhood.

Supporting Franchisees and Building Stronger Neighborhood Connections

As Rusty Taco continues to grow, maintaining its identity as a neighborhood gathering place remains an important part of the brand’s strategy. The Force4Good partnership reflects the belief that restaurants can play a meaningful role in their communities beyond serving food. By providing franchisees with the tools to coordinate fundraising events, promote local partnerships and track participation, Rusty Taco is creating a scalable program that supports both community involvement and restaurant-level business development.

“Community engagement shouldn’t be complicated or limited to a once-a-year event,” added Meche. “We want to make it easier for our franchisees to say yes when a local organization reaches out and to build relationships that continue long after a single fundraiser.”

The program also offers an opportunity to introduce Rusty Taco to new guests, encourage repeat visits and create shared experiences centered around food and community.

Looking Ahead to 2027

Rusty Taco launched the Force4Good partnership in October 2026 with 18 participating restaurants and plans to add three additional locations during the first quarter of 2027. As the initiative develops, participating restaurants will have opportunities to establish partnerships with local schools, sports organizations, charitable groups and other neighborhood organizations.

The company anticipates that the program will become an increasingly valuable part of its community engagement strategy, helping franchisees build stronger local relationships while creating new opportunities for organizations to raise awareness and support for their missions.

For Rusty Taco, the goal is simple: make it easier for neighborhoods to come together, support one another and enjoy great food along the way. For more information, visit RustyTaco.com.

‘The Gilded Age’ Is Back! Here’s What to Eat While You Watch the Show

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I can be persuaded to watch almost anything that promises extravagant set designs and beautiful costumes. If there’s history and a little mischievous social maneuvering involved, even better. My coworkers recently recommended The Gilded Age, and within two weeks, I’d watched all three seasons just in time for season four to drop.

There’s plenty to admire: Bertha Russell’s determination, Peggy Scott’s writing career and rooms furnished with a reassuring disregard for minimalism. I love the history, too, from the railroad fortunes and New York bankers to Lewis Latimer’s work in electric lighting and the woman who helped bring the Brooklyn Bridge to completion. Every episode gave me another reason to keep watching.

My husband is often still at the restaurant in the evenings, so once the kids are asleep, I have a few hours to settle in with a show. While I watched The Gilded Age, I usually poured a glass of wine (some of my favorites are under $25 at Walmart) and enjoyed the fire as Christine Baranski made an art of Agnes’s disapproval. But with the new season arriving on Nov. 1, I’m borrowing a few ideas from the show to make a watch party spread.

Set the Scene

I have a decidedly romantic streak when it comes to my surroundings, so the show’s interiors have my full attention. I’m particularly taken with the glassware and china that can make even a small supper feel indulgent. With a few soft lamps and the fire going, I’m looking forward to borrowing a little of that grandeur for my own evening.

  • A favorite plate and a special glass: Bannister may have reservations about Bertha’s colored glasses, but as a collector of vintage glassware, I find it rather inspiring. Thrift stores are wonderful places to look for floral china, small silver-plated trays and individual glasses to mix and match for a pretty tablescape.
  • A few flowers on the table: A handful of stems from the grocery store in a little pitcher is plenty. They’re equally charming when arranged in tiny bud vases or in one center arrangement. Finding flowers that mirror the colors in your china is a great way to make everything look put together.
  • Something soft beneath the dishes: Because so many watch parties happen in the living room rather than the kitchen, cover your coffee table with a tablecloth. It’ll look pretty, and it’ll catch any crumbs before they end up in the carpet. Add even more Gilded Age elegance with cloth napkins under silverware rather than paper napkins.
  • A little note for company: A handwritten card listing dishes like “Ada’s Clam Chowder” would be a nice addition if you have company for your watch party. Cream card stock and a nice pen are enough to give the food a thoughtful connection to the show.
  • Music before the show: A little opera while crafting vintage cocktails would be perfect for setting the scene, though I’d leave the battle over opera boxes to Mrs. Astor and Bertha.

A Glass for Agnes—or Ada

I’m more than happy to borrow the show’s habit of pouring wine into crystal glasses. Mine usually contains Champagne, but Ada’s season-three temperance campaign gives us a reason to put some thought into an alcohol-free option too.

Agnes’s feelings about wine appear to be settled, but there’s room for both sisters.

  • Champagne or sparkling wine: I’d choose a bottle I enjoy and pour it into a favorite glass. There’s simply no need to improve on a good Champagne.
  • A Champagne cocktail: Brandy and bitters give a Champagne cocktail a little extra ceremony, which is something perfectly befitting of a Gilded Age watch party.
  • An alcohol-free spritz: Sparkling water with cranberry juice and an orange twist would be my contribution to Ada’s cause, but any mocktail would make an equally good toast to the evening. I’d use the same glassware and leave the pledge unsigned.
  • Tea: Some evenings call for a pot of black tea and a little milk. I like bringing the pot into the living room on a tray.

Small Bites for High Society

The Russells may require a meticulously planned succession of courses, but I’d be content with a small selection of savory bites. Oysters, warm crab dip or little sandwiches would make a perfectly elegant dinner by the fire.

  • Oysters: Oysters make frequent appearances at the show’s lavish dinners, and a few with lemon would be enough to make the evening feel special. Our Oysters Rockefeller are baked with butter, spinach and Romano. And with John D. Rockefeller reportedly arriving this season, his namesake oysters certainly deserve a place at the table.
  • Crab: Marian’s visit to the Scotts is difficult to forget, though I’d rather take inspiration from their crab luncheon than her assumptions. A lovely crab meat au gratin would bring some of that indulgence home, served warm with toasted bread or crackers.
  • Asparagus: Few vegetables receive such careful instruction on how to eat them. After Bannister explains the difference between English and American asparagus etiquette, the Russell kitchen decided to avoid the matter altogether. I’d serve our roasted asparagus anyway, with a squeeze of lemon, and trust everyone to choose their own utensils.
  • Tea sandwiches: Mamie Fish’s doll-themed tea party is more commitment than I’m looking for, but I can appreciate the sandwiches. Cucumber and cream cheese, butter with thinly sliced radish or chicken salad would all be good on soft bread.

Dinner Across Fifth Avenue

For something more substantial, you can look to the show’s luncheons as much as its elaborate dinner parties. A bowl of soup or a sandwich can feel quite grand when the ingredients are good and someone else is responsible for the evening’s drama.

  • Clam chowder: A bowl of New England clam chowder is enough to win over Reverend Forte though it does little to impress Agnes. I’m with Ada on this one. Our New England clam chowder has tender potatoes, clams and a creamy broth that sounds perfect for a November season premiere.
  • Lobster rolls: The lobster left behind after Bertha’s disastrous first reception deserves a more appreciative audience. Our lobster rolls put that idea into buttery toasted buns, making them much more convenient for my own seating arrangements.

Review: Pienaar & Son Tiny Batch Medium Char

South African whisky has come a long way in recent years, and Pienaar and Son’s Tiny Batch is one of the bottles that has helped put local whisky on the radar, particularly in the main market with fun gifting campaigns and great social media work.

Medium Char is part of a two-part release (the other being Heavy Char) both single grain, single cask whiskies matured in virgin American oak and bottled at 43% abv. Each cask only produces around 550 bottles, so I would have personally preferred to see “Single Cask” on the label, as there isn’t much “batch” about it.

Pienaar & Son Tiny Batch Medium Char Review

The nose is sweet and fairly delicate. Vanilla, honey, and fresh oak come through first, followed by caramel and a distinct grassy note. There’s a touch of coconut and white chocolate. It’s pleasant, although the oak is definitely doing a lot of the talking.

The palate is soft, sweet, and easy-drinking. Again, vanilla, caramel, and toasted oak lead the way, later being met with baking spice and a little pepper. There’s a nice creamy quality to it, but is overall pretty straightforward. At 43% abv, there’s no heat to speak of, although I’d have liked a little more weight and concentration, however I understand the decision to keep the abv on the more accessible side for this one.

The finish is medium-short, with more vanilla and spice. It’s clean and pleasant, but doesn’t exactly hang around.

Overall, I like what Pienaar & Son are doing here, and I think Tiny Batch is an important whisky for South Africa. I can’t say it’s a bottle I’ll show off in my collection, but I don’t think that’s it’s purpose. Rather, it is a very approachable young whisky with a clear identity. The virgin oak gives it plenty of vanilla and sweetness, while the medium char keeps things from getting too heavy. It’s uncomplicated, and while I would love to see what more age and other casks can bring here, it’s clear that the distillers know what they’re doing – they’ve produced a clean and well-made spirit.

For someone wanting to explore South African whisky, it’s a good place to start. For a serious whisky nerd looking for layers of depth, I’d probably reach for something else.

The Heavy Char is an expression I’m excited to compare side-by-side with this – I’m keen to see what some additional oak influence could contribute to the spirit.

86 proof.

South Africa’s Fine Wine Scene Has Grown Up with the Help of These Families

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

South Africa has been making wine for nearly four centuries. Yet the wine industry we know today is surprisingly young. How can both be true? For much of that history, winemaking was dominated by merchants and the cooperative system. Only in the last 50 years or so have estate bottlings and own-label wines given rise to a culture of fine wine.

The Love Story That Started It All

The country’s first crush took place in 1659. Another sort of crush blossomed not long afterward, between an enslaved woman and a German immigrant. Ansela van de Caab and Laurens Campher’s love story is intertwined with the genesis of the South African wine industry, helping create the mythology surrounding the historic Stellenbosch estate, Muratie.

The Muratie estate traces its origins to the late 17th century. Courtesy of Rijk Melck

In the early Cape, such relationships were not unusual; a European settler marrying an enslaved person, however, was virtually unheard of. Van de Caab (“of the Cape”) lived in a Dutch East India Company slave lodge and likely worked in the Company’s garden, where she is thought to have met Laurens, a soldier. They had three kids out of wedlock between 1686 and 1692. Laurens lived about 30 miles east of the Cape, in the Stellenbosch region, and would travel by foot or ox-drawn wagon to visit Ansela, who remained enslaved. After she was emancipated in 1695, Laurens brought her and the children to live with him at De Driesprong farm, which would later become the Muratie estate.

Courtesy of Muratie

Visit Muratie today, and it’s like the clocks stopped in 1699, the year Laurens officially took over the property. The wooded farmyard is enclosed by whitewashed Cape Dutch buildings. Sprawling across the cellar entrance is an ancient oak tree that’s said to have been planted by Ansela to bless their marriage. Standing beneath this tree in the dappled shade, it’s easy to imagine Ansela and Laurens in conversation, as they are in the play inspired by their lives, Ansela van de Caab. In one scene, Laurens declares, “One day, I’ll come and fetch you. I want to marry you and have you always beside me, next to me, and you must be mine.”

Ansela replies, “How can I belong to you when I don’t even belong to myself?”

The Camphers planted 8,000 grapevines on the property and started making wine. The farm passed through a series of owners before Martin Melck bought it in 1763. It remained within the Melck-Beyers family for 134 years and was sold off again; in 1987, Ronnie Melck bought it back.

His son, Rijk Melck, the current owner, carries the soulful air of a poet. We meet in the tasting room beside the cellar, which is draped in thick cobwebs. Portraits of previous inhabitants hang on the crumbling stone walls, including nude studies of someone’s mistress, Amber. She, like Ansela, has a wine named after her; in fact, all the wines bear the names of characters from the farm’s past.

Current Muratie custodians Rijk Melck and his wife, Kim, continue the family dynasty. Courtesy of Rijk Melck

We head outside, and Rijk leads me to the original homestead, built by Laurens in 1685. Inside are thick, rough walls of clay, mud brick, and stone, crossed overhead by heavy timber beams. Rijk tells me that while the Campher bloodline is extensive, no one in the family is currently working in wine. Remarkably, though, his nephew married a direct descendant of Ansela and Laurens.

Muratie is one of the few Stellenbosch wineries that remains in family hands. “If you think about what’s around us, who’s left?” says Rijk, referring to the recent spate of historic estates being sold to foreign entities or divided up for property development.

Bringing Bordeaux to the Cape

Head south from Muratie toward the Eerste River, with the glittering Atlantic in the distance, and you’ll find another Stellenbosch family that’s still holding fast. Here lies South Africa’s oldest, and one of its most respected, family-owned estates. Meerlust, Dutch for “pleasure of the sea,” has been in the Myburgh dynasty since 1756.

At the end of the long, oak- and palm-lined drive, the white-gabled manor house glows gold through latticed windows. I arrive as they are throwing a party to celebrate the 50th anniversary of the first Meerlust-labeled wine, a 1975 Cabernet Sauvignon. The tinkle of glassware and laughter floats on the soft night air, as it has for eight generations of Myburgh family gatherings here.

Children have chased one another through these rooms for centuries. In a way, nothing changes. It stays the same.

Hannes Myburgh, Meerlust Wines

The Meerlust farm was first established in 1693 and acquired by Johannes Albertus Myburgh in 1756. For the next six generations, the family sold wine in bulk, until a 1967 visit to France convinced Nico Myburgh that the estate’s maritime terroir was not dissimilar to Bordeaux’s. This revelation inspired Rubicon, a Bordeaux-style blend first made in 1980 with Italian winemaker Giorgio Dalla Cia. They named it for Julius Caesar’s river crossing, the “point of no return;” for them, it was a poignant coming of age. Released in 1980, Rubicon positioned the estate at the forefront of the country’s fine-wine movement.

Nico and Jean Myburgh of Meerlust Estate in 1967. Jean spearheaded the meticulous restoration of Meerlust’s derelict manor house and historic buildings, which culminated in their being declared national monuments in 1987. Courtesy of Meerlust Estate

Nico’s son, Hannes Myburgh, the current owner, is at the party. We’re in an old house, so naturally the topic of ghosts comes up. He hasn’t seen any himself but says a female specter has been spotted descending the staircase and rearranging the flowers in the vestibule. Hannes feels that the past generations are very alive here. “Children have chased one another through these rooms for centuries,” he says. “In a way, nothing changes. It stays the same.”

Hannes was, as he puts it, “promised to the land.” He says he had a carefree childhood, roaming the farm barefoot, catching fish in the dam. During school holidays, he was put in charge of irrigation in the vineyard. “It was damn hard work, but I loved it.”

After growing up in the family business, Hannes needed “free rein to become myself.” In 1976, he set out for Germany’s Rheingau region. Walking amongst vineyards that plunge toward the Rhine, something clicked: Suddenly he felt part of wine’s 8,000-year history.

He came home briefly in 1977 under sad circumstances: His mother was ill, and his parents were divorcing. The family stress prompted him to take a gap year in Paris, where he worked as a professional claqueur, someone planted in fashion-show audiences to start the applause. More consequential for his career was meeting members of the Rothschild family.

Hannes returned to Stellenbosch in 1982 and took over the vineyard, but went back to France the following year to work the harvest at Château Lafite Rothschild. This opportunity led to a watershed moment for both him and South African fine wine: He was invited to a dinner where Lafite 1961 was poured.

That bottle was his aha moment. “I experienced just how great wine could be,” he says.

His father died five years later, when Hannes was 31. “There I was, with the full responsibility of Meerlust,” he says.

Back at the party, in another room, someone drops a tray of wineglasses, snapping us back into the present. “Such a boring family, still living in the same old house!” Hannes jokes. “I’m 70 now, and having lived here all my life, this place is under my skin.”

Meerlust owner Hannes Myburgh with his constant companions. His pack has included as many as eight dogs at times. Courtesy of Meerlust Estate

Hannes has no children of his own. When asked the question of who takes over next, he smiles evasively. He reveals that there are some family members he is “really fond of.” In the cellar, succession has been rock-solid: Wim Truter is only the third winemaker since the first estate bottling in the 1970s. Hannes knows the wine is in safe hands.

‘You Can’t Make Good Wine in a Place That Looks Like a Hospital’

We move northeast now into the Simonsberg foothills, where Kanonkop, like Meerlust and Muratie, helped establish the modern Stellenbosch estate model half a century ago. Often compared to a Bordeaux First Growth, the winery has become a benchmark for age-worthy South African reds.

Kanonkop means “cannon hill,” and fittingly, a black cannon greets you at the top of the driveway. Centuries ago, the mountain peaks rang out with cannon booms to alert farmers that trading ships had entered Table Bay.

Unruly bushvines give way to old oaks on the approach to the cellar, which sits behind whitewashed walls. My visit coincides with the release of their 2023 Paul Sauer, arguably South Africa’s best Bordeaux-style red blend. After the tasting is a traditional snoek braai (fish barbecue), thrown by the estate’s owners, brothers Johann and Paul Krige, grandsons of former owner Paul Sauer.

A young Johann (left) and Paul Krige (center) of Kanonkop at the family table with their dad, Jannie Krige (right) in 1972. Courtesy of Kanonkop Estate

Like many people who grow up on a vineyard, Johann once thought about leaving the family business. “Two years after I qualified as a lawyer, I realized I was too honest for the profession,” he tells me as he works the grill.

Kanonkop was once part of a larger farm called Uitkyk. In 1903, politician J.W. Sauer bought the property and lived there with his wife, Mary Cloete Sauer, and their children, including their only son, Paul. Mary, already of famous wine stock, was the daughter of Hendrik Cloete, owner of Groot Constantia, the Cape’s oldest wine estate, established in 1685.

After J.W. died in 1913, Mary remained at Uitkyk, where Paul began farming fruit and wine grapes; he also got into politics like his father. In 1929, Mary sold Uitkyk to a German nobleman, but excluded 85 hectares of undeveloped land from the sale. She gave this portion to Paul, who named it Kanonkop.

Paul is credited with saying, “You can’t make good wine in a place that looks like a hospital.” In other words, a cellar should have character. While he vastly improved the Kanonkop visitor experience, the vineyard still operated on the coop model and didn’t produce estate bottlings.

Two years after I qualified as a lawyer, I realized I was too honest for the profession.

Johann Krige, Kanonkop Wine Estate

His daughter, Mary, inherited Kanonkop with her husband, Jannie Krige. In 1973, Jannie and winemaker Jan “Boland” Coetzee persuaded Paul to bottle a portion of the year’s yield under the Kanonkop name; a decade later, their son, Johann Krige, broke with co-ops entirely.

Kanonkop’s commitment to Cabernet Sauvignon and Bordeaux-style wines made a strong case for Stellenbosch’s fine-wine potential. The estate has also championed Pinotage, proving to the world that the South African native grape can produce wines of complexity and ageability. Their Black Label Pinotage, made from a single block, is a sought-after, small-production wine with many devotees. Johann, in his typically unsentimental way, says, “One does not set about making a cult wine; it is a status bestowed upon you.”

As Kanonkop grew in prominence, Jan “Boland” Coetzee (second from right) became a leading figure in Stellenbosch wine circles. Courtesy of Kanonkop Estate

Continuity in the cellar has been crucial: The reins passed from Coetzee to Beyers Truter to Abrie Beeslaar, whose two decades at the helm further cemented Kanonkop’s international reputation. Earlier this year, Dirk van Zyl was appointed head winemaker, bringing experience from the esteemed Glenelly Estate; he’s poised to make his mark.

“Nothing is promised,” says Johann of succession. “You have to work, prove yourself, be consistent.” Paul’s daughter, assistant winemaker Suzaan Krige, is doing just that. She is the first family member to ever join the Kanonkop winemaking team.

The fifth generation has arrived, and just in time. Urbanization is one of the greatest threats to South African wine, with housing developments eating away at prime viticultural land, taking pieces of these histories with them. Low grape prices, rising replanting costs, and the struggle to turn a profit are driving families to sell off their sprawling estates.

But with the likes of the Melcks, Myburghs, and Kriges championing both family and fine wine, you get the feeling the next act in this ongoing saga is a new beginning.

Before the first kitchen timer goes off, international trade duties have already driven up the cost of your holiday dinner. Between a historic shortage in American cattle herds raising beef prices sky-high, tariffs on tinplate steel inflating the price of canned goods, and foreign trade duties hitting European cheeses, wines, and sweets, hosting a holiday meal in 2026 can feel like walking a tightrope. I’ve been seeing these price hikes firsthand while shopping, and it’s definitely made me rethink a few of my usual grocery picks.

And while we can’t control international trade policies, we can outsmart them at the grocery store!

Your Guide to a Budget-Friendly Holiday Feast

Hosting family shouldn’t mean taking on a mountain of stress or spending your whole paycheck on dinner. With a few smart, budget-savvy pivots, I’m here to help you put together a celebratory feast that tastes incredible, looks festive, and sidesteps the latest markups on the shelves. Alongside my favorite budget-saving swaps and shopping tips, we’ve also put together a complete holiday menu that feeds eight people for around $45!

TL;DR: The 4 Smartest Swaps for a Tariff-Proof Holiday Table

Instead of buying the exact same grocery list you’ve used over the holidays for years, we found making these four simple adjustments will help keep your receipt total down:

  1. Ditch the canned goods: Tariffs on tinplate steel and imported aluminum have turned these former budget heroes into surprise budget busters. Buying canned pumpkin puree, canned green beans, canned yams, and canned cranberry sauce means you’re paying a hefty markup just for the packaging. Stick to fresh, bagged, or frozen U.S. produce instead. It’s cheaper, avoids the packaging surcharge, and often tastes noticeably better.
  2. Rethink the centerpiece protein: Avoid expensive beef roasts and foreign seafood this holiday season, both of which are carrying markups right now. Focus on domestic whole turkeys, bone-in turkey breasts, pork roasts, or glazed holiday hams. These proteins rely on local agricultural supply chains and are largely insulated from foreign trade taxes. We found whole turkey for as little as $0.85 per pound at Walmart, making it a fantastic budget-friendly centerpiece!
  3. Keep your dairy and drinks domestic: European cheeses like French Brie and Italian Parmigiano-Reggiano, along with wine and imported European butters, are carrying heavy import taxes. The U.S. produces both dairy and wine, so if you’re avoiding tariffs, they make a great swap. Switch to Wisconsin aged cheddar, Vermont goat cheese, domestic sweet cream butter, and West Coast wines.
  4. Focus on seasonal fruit, grain, and spice desserts: High tariffs have sent the price of specialty chocolates soaring, but we’re not giving up dessert! Lean into fruit-forward desserts like a pear galette or an apple crumble. You could also try an egg-rich chess pie, or any number of bread-based desserts. Other great options include sweet potato pie or pecan pie using pantry grains and domestic sweeteners.

“Kicking the Can” at Thanksgiving

A stress-free Thanksgiving dinner often relies on convenience canned products like jellied cranberry sauce, condensed cream-of-mushroom soup bases, and pumpkin puree. But in 2026, paying a premium for steel packaging is the quickest way to run up your total at the register.

Aside from straight facts about how tinplate steel and aluminum tariffs are impacting our grocery carts this holiday season, let’s talk about yield, which applies to pre (and post–let’s hope!) tariff times, too. For example, if you want to give 8 people an honest half-cup portion of carrots, you cannot rely on the canned carrot’s label touting that 3.5 servings are ready to be enjoyed inside. You’d need at least 4 cans, yielding roughly 5 to 6 cups total; at $0.96 per can, that’s $3.84, and over a third of what you bought was salt water and packaging.

On the flip side, a 4 lb. bag of raw carrots yields a solid 14 to 16 generous side servings. Even after peeling and trimming the tips (about a 10% prep loss), you are left with roughly 57 to 58 oz of pure edible veg. At $4.77 for the 4 lb bag, you feed twice as many guests for roughly 40% less out of pocket than buying the 6 to 7 cans needed to match that yield, paying around 8.3 cents per edible ounce compared to over 13 cents for canned solids, while skipping the canned brine (and tinplate steel and aluminum tariffs) completely!

Here is how to ditch the cans and build a fresher table for less money with just a little bit of extra elbow grease:

Make a 15-Minute Fresh Cranberry Sauce with 3 Ingredients

A can of jellied cranberry sauce seems cheap until you compare its price per ounce to a simple bag of fresh, American-grown cranberries. Simmering whole cranberries on the stove with a little sugar, a splash of water, and fresh orange zest takes less than twenty minutes. It thickens naturally as it cools, costs less, and delivers a vibrant flavor that makes the canned jelly completely obsolete. Only need a small batch of cranberry sauce? Put the rest of those fresh cranberries to good use and make my sugared cranberries. They’re a delicious, festive, budget-friendly treat that doubles as a gorgeous garnish for your turkey platter and holiday desserts.

Swap Out the Green Bean Casserole

Traditional green bean casserole often relies on canned french-cut green beans and canned cream soups…a double hit of packaging markups! Instead, grab two pounds of fresh green beans from the produce section or a couple bags of frozen from the freezer section. Sauté them in a skillet with a drizzle of olive oil or domestic butter, garlic, and a pinch of other ingredients you probably already have on hand. It’s crisp, colorful, and feels like something you’d order at a nice restaurant.

Elevate the Bread Basket

Instead of spending $8 to $12 on a small wedge of imported cheese to snack on before dinner, make the bread basket your showstopper instead. Bake a quick batch of warm homemade dinner rolls or a skillet of golden cornbread, and serve it alongside whipped domestic sweet cream butter drizzled with local honey and sea salt. They cost pennies per serving, actually fill people up, and feel deeply comforting. Short on time? Don’t feel bad about grabbing a bag of ready-made dinner rolls at the grocery store like we did this time. Prepped carrots, celery, and potatoes in deli containers with cold water.

Admittedly, scratch-cooking fresh foods takes more prep than opening a row of cans with a can opener, but that doesn’t mean you need to be glued to the stove all day. Make your cranberry sauce two days in advance. Peel and chop your carrots, celery, and potatoes the day before and keep them refrigerated in water. Then on the holiday morning, you’re just assembling, roasting, and mashing, not stressing!

Christmas & Holiday Entertaining: Luxury Without the Luxury Tax

Christmas dinner and New Year’s Eve often lean toward luxury imports like Champagne, ornate cheese boards, and expensive cuts of beef and roasts. You can still create an indulgent spread without absorbing tariffs:

I recently had to defend my Cosmopolitan at a dive-adjacent Manhattan bar. 

“It’s a classic cocktail, actually,” I told my friend, who smirked while sipping a whiskey on the rocks. I blathered on about Carrie Bradshaw and the Cosmo’s historic significance as an emblem of New York City, admittedly striving to downplay its pink, fruity hallmarks and prove it qualifies as a “serious” drink, whatever that means. 

Perhaps linking a “girly” cocktail to a “girly” television show (albeit a prestige HBO hero series) wasn’t doing me any favors. But as new micro-labels hit the streaming era zeitgeist, the latest being the female-targeted “white wine thriller,” it’s evident that both entertainment and the beverage world are still plagued by limiting gender stereotypes.

The Era of ‘White Wine Thrillers’

In August, The Hollywood Reporter published a deep dive on the trending subgenre, linking the origin of the term “white wine thriller” to an unnamed Peacock executive, who used it during a conversation about female-led thrillers.

Anyone who has seen All Her Fault, Big Little Lies, or The Perfect Couple—the latter two series graced by Nicole Kidman in a wig—have experienced the mildly hair-raising buzz of a white wine thriller.

“A white wine thriller can get dark but not too dark,” wrote reporter Mikey O’Connell. “The body count is kept low. … There must be aspirational elements, typically wealthy characters wearing Brunello Cucinelli while emotionally unraveling in Nancy Meyers-worthy kitchens.” 

ALL HER FAULT – Episode 108 Sarah Snook as Marissa Irvine, Dakota Fanning as Jenny Kaminski. Photo by: Sarah Enticknap/PEACOCK

Often book adaptations that center female protagonists and target female audiences, these series are alluring and sexy yet fun and palatable … just like the stereotype of white wine. “White wine feels lighter, refreshing, enjoyable,” Lisa Katz, president of scripted content at NBC and Peacock, told THR. “It goes down easy.”

Its edgier sister, the “red wine thriller,” is as dark and complex as an aged Napa Cab. She leaves a gripping sensation in the mouth, a bleak feeling that sticks with you long after the credits roll. With more kills and more blood, “a red wine thriller stains your teeth, it’s tannic,” a producer told THR, which cited Sharp Objects and Hulu’s 2026 hit Furious as prime examples of red wine thrillers with mile-long finishes. 

The phrase “white wine thriller” is lighthearted and whimsical, but it’s hard to ignore the Chardonnay misogyny of it all. Entertainment centering women often acquires labels that are rarely given to media geared toward men. Think about it, what’s the male equivalent of a “chick flick?”

Beyond the world of entertainment, people have long wielded white wine as a rhetorical tool to minimize the significance and complexity of women, all while disparaging their taste. From satire articles depicting a request for ice cubes in a glass of “Chardy” as a womanhood milestone to Fox News deeming the middle-aged women who protest ICE “Chardonnay Antifa,” white wine-related nicknames for women are seldom kind, let alone accurate. 

Some of the most complex, age-worthy, and collectible wines are white. I think it teeters into misogyny when the type of wine a woman drinks becomes a proxy for her sophistication, emotional stability, or seriousness.

Michelle Chen, sommelier

The idea that all women prefer beverages and entertainment that lean light and refreshing over dark and complex is a notion that women wine professionals would like to dispel. Behind its playful veil, “white wine thriller” is just another infantilizing trope. 

White Wine and Stereotypes

Despite the gendered associations, the language we use to talk about red wine vs. white wine is often rooted in biochemical reality. 

“In general, white wine is meant to be consumed earlier and is overall less complex,” Rustic Canyon Family Wine Director Kathryn Coker tells Wine Enthusiast. “Red wine is generally more ‘serious’—they are considered more chemically and structurally complex than white wines because they ferment with the grape skins, seeds, and stems.”

Red wine is commonly described as bold, tannic, heavy, and spicy, while white wine is associated with crispness, lightness, and fruitiness. These are vital wine descriptors with true meaning, but other wine language perpetuates gendered stereotypes: A strong red equals masculine, while a sweet white equals feminine. 

“Whites have often been described in tasting notes using more feminine-coded language, while reds are described as ‘muscular’ or ‘masculine,’” says sommelier Michelle Chen. 

To Chen, the misconception that white wine is “easy, unserious, or entry-level,” perhaps a novice choice, is absurd.

“Some of the most complex, age-worthy, and collectible wines are white,” Chen says, calling Riesling a god-tier food-pairing option. “I think it teeters into misogyny when the type of wine a woman drinks becomes a proxy for her sophistication, emotional stability, or seriousness.” 

In Coker’s 15-plus years as a sommelier and wine bar owner, she can attest that women drinkers contain multitudes. A woman may enjoy a floral Riesling as deeply as a powerful red blend, and neither choice is indicative of her character or level of sophistication. “There is absolutely no ‘norm’ for women’s go-to in terms of wine,” she says. 

While Charlotte Marshall, sommelier and beverage director of Nic and Junior’s Tavern in Chicago, has observed women wine drinkers gravitating towards Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc, she emphasizes that those happen to be “among the most approachable and widely available wines in the American market.” 

“Stereotypes have a place, and there’s data behind them, but staying too within them does a disservice to the guest and the wines,” Marshall continues. Indeed, there is some research to support the blanket statement that women prefer white wine, and it involves taste buds. 

Studies have shown that supertasting abilities appear in women more often than men. As for how that impacts wine preference, wine educator Dr. Liz Thach, MW, has noted that these “hyper-sensitive” tasters tend to avoid tannic and acidic wine, opting for whites and rosés. While research seems to indicate that many women gravitate toward white wine due to biological factors (a.k.a. superior fungiform papillae density), it hardly excuses any derogatory generalizations.

Natalie Dial, co-owner and wine director of Los Angeles’ Tomat, feels that the derogatory association between white wine or rosé and women is a distinctly American construct. 

Betty Friedan, women’s liberation movement pioneer and author of The Feminine Mystique, raises her hands in the air to make a point. October 23, 1981. Getty Images

“My guess is that the association between women and white wine is the modern-day equivalent of ‘suburban tranquilizers’ described by Betty Friedan in The Feminine Mystique,” Dial says. 

The 1963 book highlights the emptiness and discontent felt by many post-World War II American women, who were expected to find fulfillment in homemaking, modesty, motherhood, and marriage. Popping pill-form tranquilizers like Valium (a k a “Mother’s Little Helper”) and drinking alcohol offered an escape from domestic hell. 

“Once those prescriptions became more difficult to come by, of course we were reaching in the fridge for a cooling white wine to take the edge off of a long day jam-packed with patriarchy and repression,” Dial adds.

Chardonnay Girl Syndrome

Popular culture and entertainment certainly influence gender stereotypes in wine, and vice versa. “I think it’s a feedback loop between media and the cultural narratives that media then reinforces,” Chen says.

This interplay is painfully present in early-aughts film and literature. Renée Zellweger’s 32-year-old chain-smoking single gal Bridget Jones is the poster child for Chardonnay girl syndrome.

Bridget Jones’s Diary film still. Courtesy of Working Title Films

“I’ve failed again. I’ve poured an enormous glass of Chardonnay and I’m going to put my head in the oven,” reads one of her diary entries in the original 1996 novel, Bridget Jones’s Diary. The 2001 film adaptation of the same name is a complex piece of media when revisited; it was directed by a woman, Sharon Maguire, and the titular character herself is the exaggerated alter ego of her creator. Though endearingly pathetic and eternally relatable, a 136-pound, 30-something single woman who wishes to lose weight and “find a nice, sensible man” is a female character profile that has me needing something stronger than a glass of Chardonnay in 2026. 

The ABC (Anything But Chardonnay) movement of the mid-’90s spilled into the early-2000s, making it cool to turn one’s nose up at the buttery Napa Chardonnay that had oversaturated the market. By the time it became loveless Bridget Jones’ vice, the horn effect had already interfered and British sales sank.

“Chardonnay became associated with the single woman who is struggling and coping with her love life. Once that association was established, it became self-perpetuating,” Chen says of the romcom’s impact. This shallow association hasn’t entirely evaporated from mainstream culture, which is why terms like “white wine thriller” exude a similar level of sexism as “chick flick.”

To associate the seriousness of women to the seriousness of white wine, in this case Chardonnay, is already ridiculous, but it’s especially absurd given that some of the world’s most prestigious wines are Chardonnay-based.

“An oaky California Chardonnay might get stereotyped as ‘mom wine’ or saddled with more offensive terms like ‘cougar juice,’ while Grand Cru white Burgundy is treated as the domain of serious collectors, who are still culturally imagined as male,” she says. “In both cases, they’re drinking Chardonnay.”

The Horrors of Womanhood

The reality is, women are mocked no matter what they drink—Jones drowns her romantic sorrows in red wine, as well—or watch. Women are derided for being the predominant consumers of true crime, a gruesome genre that doesn’t exactly “go down easy.” If a woman can gulp a full-bodied Malbec while hitting “Next Episode” on a disturbing Netflix true-crime docuseries, then a white wine thriller can contradict the nickname it never asked for: they don’t always feel so fun and refreshing. 

Liz Meriwether’s red wine thriller Furious is a post-Epstein crime series that deals with sociopolitical issues like sex trafficking and domestic violence, with an overarching theme of female rage. Created by a woman, female-fronted, and undeniably feminist, the series’ red wine thriller label suggests its grisly elements somehow give it a masculine edge, as opposed to “lighter” white wine thrillers like All Her Fault and Big Little Lies, both of which centralize the nuances of motherhood and female friendship. 

A heartwarming allyship between Sarah Snook and Dakota Fanning’s All Her Fault characters may soften the blow of a child kidnapping mommy witch hunt story, and Big Little Lies’ central murder is tonally alleviated by its ensemble cast of A-list Hollywood divas portraying the Monterey Five, a group of suburban wine-guzzling moms. A cardigan-wrapped Kidman gripping a glass of wine had become a TikTok meme, despite her character, Celeste Wright, being a battered wife. Vulture even released a BLL drinking game in 2017, leaning into the obvious wine mom coping phenomenon weaved into the show. “Mommy juice” and “rosé all day” vibes seem to cancel out what would otherwise be a tannic domestic violence plotline applicable to the red wine thriller label. 

Media depictions of women and wine are always evolving. Chen suggests Scandal’s powerful Washington D.C. “fixer” protagonist Olivia Pope and her oversized glasses of Shiraz have broadened associations between women and wine.

In the end, I will happily watch Queen of AMC Theatres Nicole Kidman in a wig on HBO alongside a bold Nebbiolo. Another night may call for a chilled glass of Pinot Grigio in my feminine little hand as Lola Petticrew (also in a wig) murders New York City’s most vile men. There are no rules.

Publisher’s Platform: Twenty-two years of asking whether we should ban petting zoos: After Brookshire Farms, Bengtson’s and the State Fair of Texas, it is time I answered my own question

I have been asking the same question in print since 2004. In an op-ed called “E. coli and the Fair,” I asked, “So what do we do? Banish state and county fairs? Eliminate Petting Zoos?” and answered myself in three words: “Of course not.” In 2012 the headline was “Ban Petting Zoos?” In 2015, after a toddler died following a small-town Maine fair, it was “As I have said before – is it time to ban petting zoos?” In 2019, after a two-year-old died following the San Diego County Fair, it was “Seriously, time to ban petting zoos?”

A question asked four times over twenty-two years is not really a question anymore. It is a way of not answering. This week three health departments in three states opened investigations into E. coli infections tied to animal attractions at fall events. So here is my answer.

This week, in three states

At Bengtson’s Pumpkin Farm in Homer Glen, Illinois, five people with confirmed Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC), ages 4 to 38, four of them hospitalized. The petting zoo closed on October 23; the rest of the farm stayed open, and more than 200 visitors have filled out the county’s symptom survey. At Brookshire Farms in San Luis Obispo, California, five confirmed cases and an exposure window of October 16 through 26; the farm closed itself down. And in Dallas, the county is investigating up to seven possible cases in children, some treated in intensive care, and whether they trace to the petting zoo at the State Fair of Texas. The county is not yet calling it an outbreak.

I laid out what we know about each investigation yesterday, and this morning I posted twenty-five years of outbreaks just like them and the prevention playbook that everyone already has. This post is about something else: what I have been telling fairs, legislators and parents for two decades, what came of it, and what I think we should do now.

Read in a row, those posts tell a story I am not especially proud of. I have been right about the risk for twenty-two years and mostly wrong about how much signs and guidelines would fix it.

What two decades taught me

Signs alone have never been enough – but the right sign helps. Every post on that list asked for signs. The State Fair of Texas’s old Children’s Barnyard relied on hand-sanitizing gel and signs before the fair scrapped it in the mid-2000s. A sign that says “Please wash your hands” is wallpaper. The Compendium found that awareness of the risk actually protected visitors at the 2004 North Carolina State Fair. The lesson is that a sign has to tell parents what is at stake, in plain words and in the languages families at that fair speak: animals in this area can carry E. coli, it can cause kidney failure in young children, and soap and water – not sanitizer – is how you protect them.

Guidelines are not requirements. The CDC issued recommendations in 2001. State public health veterinarians have published the Compendium since 2005. As I wrote this morning, there is still no federal law on disease risk where animals and the public mix, and most states rely on voluntary guidance. Twenty-five years of outbreaks is the result of voluntary.

Laws work only as well as their weakest line. North Carolina did the most after its 2004 outbreak – Aedin’s Law, a permit and inspection for every animal exhibition at a sanctioned fair, and detailed rules that put soap-and-water stations within ten feet of exhibit exits and kept sanitizer dispensers out of the transition areas. It still had outbreaks in 2011 and 2012. Washington’s rule lets operators substitute an “alternative hand sanitizing method” approved by the local health officer. That is the loophole the Arizona State Fair walked through in 2025, and the Big Fresno Fair walked through in 2005.

The market is already banning petting zoos – one sick child at a time. Texas scrapped its fair petting zoo when insurers raised rates. By 2015, North Carolina Health News was reporting that petting zoos were becoming a thing of the past at the N.C. State Fair. After last year, the Arizona State Fair dropped its petting zoo for 2026 and the Maricopa County Fair dropped direct animal contact. Each of those decisions came after children were hurt. A ban by lawsuit and insurance premium is the most expensive, least predictable ban there is.

I was wrong about testing. In 2004 and again in 2015 I suggested testing animals before they arrive. The veterinarians have persuaded me that a negative test means little: these animals shed intermittently, and in San Diego in 2019 every animal and environmental sample came back negative after a child had died. Prevention has to assume every goat, calf and lamb is positive.

So – should we ban petting zoos?

Not all of them. I said in 2015 that petting zoos were not going to be banned and perhaps should not be, and I still believe a child who learns where food comes from by standing next to a cow is better off for it. But “not all of them” is not “none of them.” Here are the bans I now support:

1.     No direct contact for children under five with the highest-risk animals. Preweaned calves, lambs and goat kids, and animals that have recently given birth, are exactly what draws toddlers and exactly what sheds the most. The 2023 Compendium already says they are not appropriate where high-risk groups are expected. Children under five can see them over a double barrier. They should not be hand-feeding them.

2.    No animal contact anywhere that cannot provide soap and running water at the exit. No sanitizer-only petting zoos. If a pumpkin patch, a festival or a traveling operator cannot plumb a sink, the exhibit is look-don’t-touch.

3.    No permit, no petting zoo. Every operator, every location, every season – including traveling operators who work several fairs a year. The same operator name turned up at the Big Fresno Fair in 2005 and the Arizona State Fair in 2025.

The Petting Zoo Preservation Act – the short version

In 2015 I promised a Model Act. Here it is in outline, built from Pennsylvania’s law, North Carolina’s Aedin’s Law and its rules, Washington’s rule, the Arizona Public Health Association’s proposal after last year’s outbreak, and the 2023 Compendium:

4.    A state permit and pre-opening inspection for every animal-contact exhibit open to the public, renewed each season and each location.

5.    Soap, running water and paper towels within a few steps of every exit, at a height children can reach, sized for peak crowds. Sanitizer is never a substitute.

6.    A staff member at every exit whose job is to make sure hands get washed.

7.     Plain-language warning signs at every entrance and exit, in the languages local families speak, naming E. coli, kidney failure and the risk to children under five – and the same information sent home before school field trips.

8.    No food, drink, bottles, pacifiers, sippy cups or toys in animal areas; stroller parking outside; no feed sold in ice cream cones; food sold and eaten only in animal-free areas.

9.    The under-five and recently-birthed-animal rules above.

10. Veterinary health certificates, and records of where every animal came from and where it has been, available to investigators on request.

11.  Proof of liability insurance as a condition of the permit.

12.  Mandatory reporting and public notice: operators report illnesses they learn of, and when a health department links cases to a venue, it names the venue, publishes case counts, ages, hospitalizations and HUS on a schedule, and alerts local physicians. It is the same principle behind my FDA petition on outbreak firm disclosure – families cannot protect themselves from a source no one will name.

13.  Civil penalties and permit suspension for violations.

None of that is radical. Most of it has been on the books in one state or another for twenty years. It just has not been on the books in all of them, and it has rarely been enforced.

To the people who can do something about it

To legislators in Texas, Illinois and California: you have three investigations open this week. Pass the Act. To Arizona: bring back the bill that died. To fair boards and pumpkin patch owners: you do not need to wait for a law. Plumb the sinks, staff the exits, keep the bottle-fed lambs away from the toddlers, and tell parents the truth on the sign at the gate. To insurers: ask for those things before you write the policy, not after the claim.

And to parents: wash with soap and water as soon as you leave the animal area, even if your child did not touch anything. Keep food, bottles and pacifiers out. If your child develops bloody diarrhea within about ten days of a farm or fair visit, see a doctor, tell them about the visit, and watch for decreased urination, paleness and lethargy – signs of hemolytic uremic syndrome. More on staying safe around farm animals is at Fair-Safety.com.

In 2004 I wrote that ignoring these risks “makes good business for lawyers like me. But I’ll gladly give up that business if it means not having to see four-year-olds hooked up to kidney dialysis machines.” I meant it then. I mean it now. I would very much like this to be the last October I write this post.

His position now is not a blanket ban, but bans on direct contact for children under five with the highest-risk animals, on animal contact at any venue without soap and running water at the exit, and on operating without a state permit.

In 2012, Eugen Beer, a British publicist, and Barbara McMahon, a Scottish journalist, were working on a project at home in the U.K. While searching through the University of Nevada’s online archives, they came across an old menu from Café Anglais in Paris, which closed in 1913. It featured a little boy feeding a piece of meat to the moon

Courtesy of Vintage Menu Art

“We were just completely charmed,” Beer says. “It opened a giant rabbit hole for us.”

The couple became engrossed in a world of menu collectors, archivists, and dealers they never knew existed. More than a decade later, they’ve pored over thousands of menus pulled from cardboard boxes and library shelves across the U.S.

They’ve found hand-painted cards from late-19th-century New York banquets, keepsakes from the golden age of cruise liners, and mid-century masterpieces made for Hawaiian hotels. They’ve unearthed forgotten commercial work by Saul Bass and Paul Rand, two of the 20th century’s most influential graphic designers. They even found a menu from 1947 that William Steig—the creator of Shrek—designed for Welton’s in Worcester, Massachusetts, listing Cognac at 75 cents.

Today, Beer and McMahon run the business Vintage Menu Art, selling menu prints from their ever-growing, 1,500-strong catalog. But if you ask Beer, the commercial appeal is almost beside the point. It’s more about their passion for preserving dining culture. “It’s a slice of Americana that Americans forgot,” he says.

Dining at restaurants became a part of American life just before the turn of the 20th century. As manifest destiny led Americans westward, cities sprang up across the heartland, creating new demand for hotels, restaurants, and bars.

“There was no dining-out culture in the U.K.—except for the very rich—until the ’60s,” Beer explains. “But in the U.S., whenever a place like Dodge City emerged, one of the first things they did was establish a hotel and import a bartender from the East Coast.”

Then, after Prohibition, restaurants boomed nationwide, and their owners began commissioning artists to make their menus stand out in an increasingly crowded field.

“The food at that time was all the same. It was steak, it was lobster, it was burgers,” Beer says. Whether it was promoting the zombie cocktail in Waikiki or the art of enjoying wine, the menus were a colorful form of marketing that helped turn ordinary meals into nights to remember.

Courtesy of Vintage Menu Art

In retrospect, they also reveal how restaurants kept drinks cheap to compete with other spots.

“Immediately after Prohibition, in 99% of places, cocktails would have started at 25 cents,” Beer says. “We have one menu from Tail o’ the Cock in the 1940s. The most expensive dish, a filet mignon, was $4.95, and a bottle of Château Margaux was $5.75.”

The U.S. was fertile ground for menu hunting for another reason: Americans held onto them. For generations, people kept menus tucked into scrapbooks, a practice Beer attributes to both rising purchasing power and a distinctly American appetite for souvenirs.

It’s a slice of Americana that Americans forgot.

Eugen Beer

The golden age of menu design flew under the radar for decades, but some of them eventually found their way into private and institutional collections. By the early 2010s, the Culinary Institute of America in New York alone had up to nearly 50,000 menus. Getting access to them was surprisingly difficult, though.

“At the New York Public Library, you’d have to book an appointment. You could only see two menus at a time,” Beer says.

Courtesy of Vintage Menu Art

Private collectors offered another way in. One was Henry Voigt, a former DuPont executive who started buying menus at auctions in the 1990s and eventually amassed more than 10,000. Beer considers him one of the foremost custodians of American menu art, and he agreed to let the couple work with his collection.

Ever since, Voigt has been a key inspiration and resource for Vintage Menu Art, which sells prints from his collection. Voigt has also donated more than 2,600 pre-20th-century menus to the American Antiquarian Society. Voigt would say he’s simply preserving “the history of everyday life.”

“[These menus] not only reflect what the people were eating but also what they were doing and what they valued—the symbolism of food,” Voigt tells me.

He says a menu can transport you to a moment in history, like one in his collection from Springfield, Massachusetts, dated April 16, 1865, the day after Abraham Lincoln’s assassination. Its black border looked like a printing error at first. Then Voigt realized it was printed that way to mourn the president’s death.

“You put yourself in that dining room,” he says, “and through dinner, you’re hearing church bells all over Springfield because they rang all day that Sunday.”

Courtesy of Vintage Menu Art Courtesy of Vintage Menu Art

For Beer and McMahon, rescuing forgotten menus is a labor of love. First, they scour auctions and follow leads for unique pieces. “We don’t care if it comes from Poughkeepsie or Punxsutawney. Does the menu speak to us?” Beer says of their search criteria.

Those that make the cut are scanned and cleaned up, pixel by pixel, removing creases, stains, and other damage. They also research the stories behind the designs, packing as much history as they can into each reproduction, including original menus, prices, and all. Then, they print them on archival paper and sell them online, at their permanent booth in L.A.’s Farmers Market, and at art and wine fairs on the West Coast.

Now, between museums and commercial print sales, these menus are reaching new audiences again. Beer says they still achieve what their creators intended.

“These were designed to entertain, to make people anticipate a nice time, and they’re still doing that,” he says. “When people pick up a print [at our stall in L.A.], they go, ‘Wow, have you seen this?’ They whip themselves up into a frenzy of excitement.”

Andrew Reiner

Mexico City is a colossal, beautifully overwhelming metropolis and an absolute paradise for those who live to eat. Across thousands of restaurants, markets, street stalls, bakeries and fondas, food from every corner of the country converges. So deciding where (and what) to eat can feel daunting.

I’ve spent over a decade writing about Mexico’s food scene, along with popping up on TV shows to talk about it occasionally. My favorite way to navigate it all is still right here on the ground with my food tours. Sidestepping the obvious spots and encouraging travelers to go deeper into the food culture here constantly reminds me of the fascinating nature of how we chilangos eat.

Andrew Reiner

To me, that means having the flexibility to move between completely different culinary worlds. We can spend lunchtime standing on a crowded sidewalk, leaning over a taco drenched in salsa and lime juice, then dress up that same evening for a multicourse dinner at a dimly lit restaurant. We’re fiercely loyal to the neighborhood places that have fed us for years and know our names, but we remain insatiably curious about the next opening or dish worth crossing town for. 

That same flexibility shapes the city’s food. CDMX knows exactly how to pull tradition into modernity without losing its spirit. These 12 spots offer a taste of the city’s versatility: dishes and bites deeply rooted in history but shaped by the wonderfully chaotic, ever-evolving way we eat today. 

Scrambled Eggs at Fonda Margarita

Adolfo Prieto 1364 B, Tlacoquemecatl del Valle, Benito Juárez
+52 55 5559 6358

Andrew Reiner

I remember being dragged out of bed early on a weekend as a teenager to have breakfast here. Although I was still drowsy, the smell of the wood-fired stoves, the café de olla (traditional “pot coffee”) bubbling away, and sounds of the older musicians playing classic tunes on their guitars were enough to bring me around. I was fully awake by the time a plate of refried beans with scrambled eggs and a stack of warm tortillas made it to the table. It’s the kind of humble combination you’d find in almost any Mexican home. At Fonda Margarita, these eggs, which are cooked over a wood fire and enriched with lard, take on a different life. Getting here early is a requirement, as they open around 6:30 a.m. and by 10 a.m., all the food is gone.

Chilaquiles at Chilpa

C. Chilpancingo 35, Colonia Condesa,
+52 55 5264 4976

Andrew Reiner

Few breakfast menus are complete without chilaquiles. What started as a way to use up day-old tortillas is now one of the country’s quintessential breakfasts: triangles of tortillas, deep-fried until crisp, then bathed in salsa. At Chilpa in leafy Condesa, several salsas are available, from a red, tomato-based version to the bright, tangy tomatillo number, all with different spice levels. Pile on your choice of cecina, shredded chicken, or fried eggs. Crema, grated cheese, and chopped onion are standard, making it easy to order a different version every visit. 

Chocolate Concha at Panadería Gala

Tonalá 143, Roma Norte

Andrew Reiner

If you asked my Mexican grandma what breakfast looks like, she’d say coffee and a pan dulce—more often than not that would mean a concha. The quintessential Mexican pastry makes an appearance in virtually every bakery in town, from the most traditional versions made with a combination of lard and shortening to the tender butter-forward renditions found in most modern bakeries. At Panadería Gala, a speakeasy-style bakery, the extra-bouncy conchas get an extra layer of indulgence when topped with a white or dark chocolate ganache quenelle.

Margarita at Despacho Margarita

Córdoba 115, Roma Norte

Andrew Reiner

The Margarita may be known worldwide as the quintessential Mexican cocktail. Legend has it that the iconic drink was first conceived in Ensenada, Baja California, and its ties to coast and borderlands are still strong. But chilangos tend to save it for specific occasions and places such as a beach vacation, a favorite cantina, or a particular restaurant where we know they’ll make a killer one. At Despacho Margarita, the classic shaken version gets a new life, alongside spicy and frozen variations. In the slushy machine there are usually two flavors: a classic tequila and lime version and a seasonal mezcal-based one that changes monthly, with unique flavors such as nopal and shiso or raspberry and cayenne pepper. 

Banderas at Cantina Covadonga

Puebla 121, Roma Norte
+52 55 5533 2701

Andrew Reiner

Cantinas have long been places to gather over good food and several rounds of drinks. In these laid-back establishments, both are given equal importance. At Covadonga, a cantina without background music, generations mix at the tables, from the young and cool to sixty-somethings who come to play dominoes (available on request). 

Banderas have been a popular order here from the beginning: three shot glasses lined up, one with tequila, another with lime juice, and a third with sangrita, a red-hued blend of tomato and fruit juices. Together they mimic the three colors of the Mexican flag (hence the name). They’re not meant to be knocked back but rather sipped slowly, alternating between glasses.

Tlacoyo de Haba at Tlacoyos Medellín

Eje 3 Poniente Medellín, Roma Sur

Andrew Reiner

There’s a certain type of stand you’ll see repeatedly throughout the city centered around a large, round comal, usually fired with charcoal, with a bucket of corn masa and, more often than not, two women working side by side. Gorditas, quesadillas, and sopes are all on the menu, but the tlacoyo may be the most emblematic of central Mexico and Mexico City. 

Football-shaped and stuffed with your choice of fava bean purée, requesón (a soft cheese similar to ricotta), or refried beans, tlacoyos are typically topped with cactus salad for a tangy contrast, grated queso fresco, and red or green salsa, depending on your preference. They’re the kind of morning or afternoon snack you might grab during errands or before wandering into the adjacent market.  

Torta de Pastor con Queso at El Vilsito 

Petén 248 y, Avenida Universidad, Narvarte Poniente, Benito Juárez
+52 55 5536 3060

Andrew Reiner

The idea of stacking meat on a rotating spit was brought to Puebla by Lebanese immigrants. Once it reached Mexico City, it evolved into one of the city’s great late-night bites: the taco al pastor. At El Vilsito, a garage by day that moonlights as one of the city’s best taquerías, they take the pastor one step further, turning it into the most Chilango version possible by making it into a torta. A warm, lightly toasted telera gets a smear of refried beans, avocado, onion, tomato, and freshly shaved pastor, all finished with melted cheese. 

Fresh Oysters at Taller de Ostiones by FISM

Calle Versalles 113, Casa Versalles, Juárez
+52 55 8568 7397

Andrew Reiner

You can’t talk about Mexico City without talking about its obsession with seafood. It may seem strange for a city far from the coast, but seafood from both of Mexico’s coastlines makes its way here through the second-largest seafood market in the world (surpassed only by Tokyo’s Toyosu) before being distributed across the rest of the country.

At Taller de Ostiones, clams, barnacles, and tuna arrive three times a week by air from Baja California Sur, alongside sustainably farmed oysters. Those bivalves pair perfectly with a house-made sauce that’s rich, briny, deeply umami, and slightly spicy.

Comida Corrida at Nuevo Tehuacán

C. Dinamarca 47, Juárez
+52 55 1313 8562

Andrew Reiner

Mexico City runs on fondas and street food. These casual eateries serve homestyle food that’s quick, affordable, and feels like a warm hug. Stepping inside a fonda means being a part of a daily ritual known as comida corrida, where a meal typically unfolds in four acts: a steaming bowl of soup or broth to start, followed by your choice of rice or pasta. Next comes the main course, which at some places, like Nuevo Tehuacán, is what dictates the price of the meal; meatballs in chipotle, pork with tomatillo and purslane, and chicken in peanut sauce are classics. Everything is washed down with a pitcher of freshly made agua fresca and finished with a sweet treat like a cup of gelatin. Look around, and you’ll see schoolchildren sitting elbow to elbow with office workers all savoring the comfort of one of the city’s most democratic meals.

Flan at Siembra Tortillería

Avenida Isaac Newton 300, Polanco

Andrew Reiner

Mostly homemade, flan is a staple of casual Mexican cooking, but it’s one of those desserts that look deceptively simple. Getting the texture just right (silky, firm, not overly sweet) is way harder than it seems, which may be why the best versions are often found in homes. Siembra may be a temple dedicated to heirloom corn masa, tacos, and antojitos, but its flan has all the right qualifications to receive just as much attention. 

Nieve de Mamey at Nevería Roxy

Fernando Montes de Oca 89, Cuauhtémoc, Colonia Condesa
+52 55 5286 1258

Andrew Reiner

Ice cream parlors used to be an almost daily afternoon ritual for families and couples alike. Nevería Roxy was one of the early ones, and its 1950s-diner palate of teal and red has barely changed, even in the newer locations. Alongside the classic milk- and cream-based flavors, sundaes, floats, and banana splits, you’ll find nieves. 

The word literally translates to “snow,” and the closest comparison might be a sorbet, though the texture tends to be a little coarser and the flavors typically center around seasonal fruits. Mamey, an endemic fruit, may have a short season, but Roxy keeps its nieve de mamey year-round. With a faintly nutty flavor reminiscent of baked sweet potato, it’s become a local favorite. And thanks to mamey’s lush, natural creaminess, it’s easy to forget there’s no dairy in the cup. 

Candied Lime Peels With Shredded Coconut at Dulcería de Celaya

Avenida 5 de Mayo 39, Centro Histórico de la Ciudad
+52 55 5521 1787

Andrew Reiner

Stepping into Dulcería de Celaya feels more like entering a 19th-century jewelry store than a candy shop. Founded in 1874, this Centro Histórico institution still retains its original art nouveau interior, complete with French mirrors, tiled floors, and hand-carved oak cabinets, all filled with traditional sweets made in-house. Among the colorful sweet treats are candied lime skins stuffed with shredded sweetened coconut. The peel is candied and tender and, unlike fresh lime rind, is not bitter, so you can go ahead and bite into the whole thing at once.

Gelatin-Rich "Liquid Gold" Chicken Stock (Instant Pot, Pressure Cooker, or Stockpot)

📅 Oct 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I keep canned chicken broth in my pantry, and I also make my own for excellent flavors that tilt toward Asia. I've been making chicken stock for decades and used to use only chicken parts. With the price of chicken going up and parts not easy to obtain, I've changed my recipe to include meaty bone-in parts because they are easier to get, chicken feet for their rich collagen and round mouthfeel, and everyday vegetables. You can omit the vegetables but scraps are what go into my chicken stock. It's affordable and sophisticated but you don't have to spend a lot of money or time.

The reward is luxurious "liquid gold" to enhance your recipes, whether it's noodle soup, a stir-fry, or braise. Or, just warm 1 cup in a pot, add leafy greens and or tofu, for a nourishing soup. I also love to sip on the broth as a nourishing life giving beverage.

Below you'll find my recipe to make flavorful stock fast in a pressure cooker (it takes about 1 hour of actual cooking and you don't have to monitor the pot much), tips for cooking stock in stockpot on the stove, and an explainer on why I add certain vegetables to my chicken stock formula. From there, you can confidently put your own imprint on your chicken stock.

The Secret to "Liquid Gold" Stock: Chicken Feet & Vegetable Scraps

After years of making stock from scratch, I know this is true: choose chicken parts and veggies wisely for flavorful results.

Chicken parts like legs and breasts alone don't yield super-tasty stock because, although they are meaty but they don't have enough skin or fat for richness. Given that, combine chicken parts with chicken feet (often sold as 1-lb | 450g packages at the market). Or, use backs, though they can be harder to find sometimes. may be fatty, they aren't meaty enough. Don't see chicken feet or backs? Ask for them at the meat counter or, save backs when you break down a whole chicken. If backs and feet are totally unavailable, use all meaty parts.

To build flavor, add produce. You don't need much and can use scraps. Over-the-hill veggies that are mildly dehydrated work well because they have concentrated flavor. Here are my favorites, which are listed in the recipe below. You don't have to use all of these vegetables! HOWEVER, ginger and onion are required.

  • Ginger and onion lend sweet pungency.
  • A sweet apple, such as Fuji, lends a mild sweetness.
  • Carrot lends sweetness and golden color. Scrub and don't peel because the peel adds complexity to the stock.
  • Corn cobs have sweetness but add too much and the stock will have a corny flavor. After cutting off kernels, freeze the cobs.
  • Asparagus ends are full of umami. Trim them when prepping asparagus and freeze them for stock, if you're not using them ASAP.
  • Mushrooms add earthiness and umami but don't add too much or they may darken the broth or take over flavor. Use dried shiitake mushroom, or fresh cremini or white button mushroom. Save the dried stems from dried shiitake prep for stock.
  • Cabbage leaves (napa or green kinds) add verdant sweetness and minerality. Wilted leaves that you'd discard actually have extra potent flavor. Red cabbage will color your stock reddish!
  • Celery has a verdant minerality to support the chicken vibe well. Include the leaves of the rib too.
  • Dried kombu (kelp) add gelatinous texture and umami flravor.
  • Cilantro sprigs or coriander seeds contribute a pungent citrusy notes.

Instant Pot vs pressure cooker vs. stockpot chicken stock

Which to use? Despited being branded a multicooker, the Instant Pot is basically a pressure cooker but you can set it and forget it. The pressure cooker requires a little monitoring but is easier to clean than the Instant pot. Pressure cookers extra flavor fast and efficiently. I like it for stock. (If you need assistance, check my guide to buying a pressure cooker and Instant Pot.) On the other hand, the stockpot produces lovely stock too. It just takes about 30 to 40 percent longer and you need to monitor water level.

Chicken stock tips

Some bonus tips to further guide you to beautiful chicken stock:

  • Chicken part swaps: When whole chickens are on sale, buy a couple, cut them up (or ask a butcher to do the deed) and use the backs and some of the meaty parts for stock. You want about 2 ½ (pounds total.
  • Parboil parts or not? If your chicken parts are hacked though the bone, parboil them for about 1 minute in water to cover to remove the scummy denatured protein bits. That will yield cleaner tasting and clearer stock. Parboiling does not diminish the flavor.
  • To make stock in a stockpot: Put all the ingredients in a 6 to 8-quart stockpot. Add 8 cups water. Bring to a gentle simmer and continue simmering, uncovered, for 2 hours. Add water if the liquid level falls below the solids. Remove from the heat and let cool and rest for 15 minutes before straining. If needed, boil to reduce to about 6 cups, or add water if the stock is too strong.
  • Save the harvested fat for stir-frying, refried beans, and other dishes. Freeze it. If it's liquidy, watch out when reheating because it will spit.

Once things get going, gingery and chicken-y aromas will soon fill your kitchen, and you'll have a rich, viscous stock to enhance your food.

Stock tips for using your liquid gold

A good chicken stock in the fridge or freezer is like money in the bank. Add quick cooking vegetables, meat or tofu to a cup for a nourishing soup lunch. Employ it for my fast chicken pho and yours will be extra delicious. You'll have a headstart on a sensational batch of Viet asparagus, shrimp, and egg drop soup too!

Finally deploy this stock to seed flavor in my shortcut Vietnamese rotisserie chicken noodle soup (mi ga tiem) and Hainan chicken rice (com ga Hai Nam). Both recipes are at my newsletter, Pass the Fish Sauce.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can this recipe be made gluten-free?

Yes! Substitute gluten-free tamari for the soy sauce.

Can I use regular (non-silken) tofu?

You can! Many restaurant versions of agedashi tofu are made with silken tofu, but regular tofu works too. It will be a little firmer and less custardy inside, but it’s also easier to handle if you’re new to frying tofu.

Can I bake my agedashi tofu?

I don't recommend it. The cornstarch coating needs a heavy, even distribution of oil in order to crisp up properly, and silken tofu is too delicate to toss or rub with oil after coating. In the oven, you're likely to end up with dry, powdery patches of cornstarch instead of the thin, crisp coating you get from frying.

How should I store my leftovers and how long will they keep?

Store your broth, garnishes, and tofu in separate airtight containers in the fridge for up to 3 days. To reheat the tofu, place the pieces on a baking sheet and in a 400°F oven until they start to crisp back up and sizzle.

When cooks talk about basil, they often mention the varieties used in the Mediterranean but basil is popular in many parts of the world. In fact, basil was domesticated first in India and Southeast Asia circa 3000 BCE. It took thousands of years and trade routes to bring basil to ancient Egypt around 350 BCE, and then to the Mediterranean. Basil's modern association with pesto and the like wouldn't have existed if basil didn't first arrive on the scene in Asia. In this post, I'll share some fun facts about basil and delve into three kinds of Asian basil: Thai basil, holy basil, and lemon basil.

From royal embalming to pesto: the journey of the "King of Herbs"

Basil got its start in India and through trade routes going eastward across the Indian Ocean to the South China Sea, variations of Ocimum basilicum (sweet basil) traveled to the areas we now know as Southeast Asia -- Thailand, Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia, plus Taiwan. Plant selection by local farmers resulted in specialized varieties. There are three main kinds of basils used in Asia today.

Thousands of years later, around 350 BCE, trade brought basil from Asia to ancient Egypt. Basil's preservative quality made it great for embalming bodies in ancient Egypt. Alexander the Great likely brought basil from Egypt to Greece, and from there migrated further into the Mediterranean. Basil's modern name comes from basilikon (Greek for "royal" or "king"). So it's the King of Herbs. During the 16th and 17th centuries, basil went westward in Europe and to the Americas with European colonists in the early 1600s. But, you're here for Asian basil insights to let's get to it!

Thai basil (O. basilicum var. thyrsiflora), the most common Asian basil

What's widely known as Thai basil (Ocimum basilicum var. thyrsiflora) was bred to withstand Southeast Asia's hot rainy, humid weather, have favorable flavors of anise, licorice, and mint, and have a sturdy stem that won't easily wilt! It became a cornerstone for Thai cuisine and southern Vietnamese cooking. Thai speakers know Thai basil as horapa and Viet speakers know it as rau húng quế. Most other people know it as the go-to Asian basil. 

Thai basil literally means cinnamon basil in Vietnamese but know that there is a cinnamon basil variety that is a different cultivar. There are over 40 cultivars of basil so it's easy to be confused! If you cannot find Thai basil, use dark opal basil, which has similar flavor.  Thai basil has a stronger flavor than Italian/Mediterranean basil so if you sub one for the other remember to use more (or use less!). 

  • Thai basil cultivars: What's sold as Thai basil at Asian markets has a thicker, sturdier stems and fleshier leaves than what is sold as starter plants at typical nurseries. The Asian market's Thai basil often has a stronger flavor. 
  • Thai basil uses: Add to Thai curry for aroma and flavor at the last minute. Serve as a garnish for southern Vietnamese-style beef pho. Stuff into a sandwich! 
  • Thai basil sourcing: Thai basil is sold year-round at Asian markets. Grow your own for the freshest. Seeds are available here on Etsy.  I buy starter plants from Southeast Asian markets like Battambang or Thien Thanh in San Jose.

Holy basil or Tulsi (Ocimum tenuiflorum), a mighty and precious herb

Holy basil has softly serrated leaves with a tiny bit of fuzz. It's one of the oldest varieties of basil, having originated in India. It was revered not as a mere plant, but as the earthly manifestation of the goddess Lakshmi. Its holiness was not just spiritual. Early Sanskrit medical texts identified tulsi as a major adaptogen which can help the body deal with stressful situations. Nicknamed "The Mother Medicine of Nature", was valued for treating respiratory conditions, fevers, and snakebites.

In Thai, holy basil is known as กะเพรา (gaprao) and in South Asia, it's called tulsi. Thai holy basil comes as green or reddish purple cultivars. Compared to familiar Thai basil, holy basil has a spicier, more peppery flavor than Thai basil. Some describe holy basil as have a punch clove-like scent. It's unique and distinct.

  • Holy basil and tulsi cultivars: Indian tulsi has slightly thicker leaves than Thai gaprao. You can use tulsi in the Thai stir-fry and vice versa for Indian tea.
  • Holy basil and tulsi uses: Steep in hot water for a calming, soothing tea that's great for sleep. Pluck off the leaves for the Thai classic - Pad Gaprao stir-fry. You'll need about 1.5 oz (45g) of holy basil leaves plus lots of garlic and chilies for one pound (450g) of protein (chicken, pork, beef, or tofu). Holy basil seeds are steeped for a thickish pudding like dessert (think chia seed like). Vietnamese chè hột é (chè hat é) features holy basil seeds in a dessert sweet soup with cooling grass jelly and other ingredients.
  • Holy basil and tulsi sourcing: Buy during warm weather season from Cambodian markets or Southeast Asian farmers who will bring them to farmer's markets. Grow tulsi and gaprao from seeds or starter plants (I buy mine from Battambang, a Cambodian market in San Jose). Grow it or buy it for this sensational Bougie Pad Graprao stir-fry.

Lemon basil (Ocimum × citriodorum), delicate looking yet flavor packed

Lemon basil has a lovely citrusy scent that may evoke lemongrass. The leaves are a lighter green than Thai basil and smaller, with delicate features. It's classified as Ocimum × africanum but most people know it as lemon basil, hoary basil, Thai lemon basil, or Lao basil.

Interestingly, lemon basil is a hybrid basil (Ocinum basilicum) and American basil (Ocinum americanum). The primarily grows in northeastern Africa and Southeast Asia ior its lemony perfume and flavor. Many cuisines employ lemon basil, from Middle Eastern, Indonesian, and Filipino to Lao, Malaysian, Indian, and Thai.

  • Lemon basil cultivars: Mrs. Burn's lemon basil is easy to grow but is not as aromatic as the cultivar grown by Southeast Asian farmers. Use extra if you've got Mrs. Burn's.
  • Lemon basil uses: Enjoy raw or stir leaves into a curry at the last moment. Lemon basil is delicate when the sprig is long. Use it soon if it's leggy.
  • Lemon basil sourcing: During summer, Cambodian markets seem to have a good supply of lemon basil, sold in bags or as starter plants. Hmong and Lao farmers grow it during the warmer months too. Buy "Thai lemon basil" seeds if you want the Southeast Asian variety. Persian (Iranian) markets often sell lemon basil too.

Asian basil uses and substitution in recipes

These three Asian basils have certain "proper" uses but the certainly can be used as substitutes. For instance, true Thai pad gaprao should feature holy basil because the herb defines the flavor and name of the dish. I've shared a premium recipe called Bougie Pad Gaprao from Cooking Thai, a book I wrote with Michelin-starred chef Pim Techamuanvivit.

But, I've cooked off-road to use lemon and/or Thai basil in this chicken and mushroom rendition of Thai holy basil stir-fry. If you use other basils, change the name to Pad Horapa (Thai Basil Stir-Fry), for instance!

Beyond the Thai repertoire, hearty Thai basil is fabulous with beef pho, but for chicken pho, I prefer lemon basil's lighter flavor. Viet people also eat Thai basil raw along with certain foods like hột vịt lộn (half-hatched fertilized duck eggs, aka balut).

For nasi ulam, a Malasian rice salad, you can use whatever you like! When making my grilled lemon basil chicken, you can swap out Thai basil for wonderful results too. There's so much more to basil than Genovese and pesto. Asian basils unlock more flavor opportunities for your culinary rotation.

A Guide to Shrimp Crackers: How to Buy & Fry Indo and Viet Styles

Shrimp crackers, aka shrimp chips, are the Southeast Asian equivalent of the American potato chip. Vietnamese speakers know them as bánh phồng tôm and Bahasa Indonesian speakers call them krupuk udang. People from other Southeast Asian cultures enjoy them too. How are shrimp crackers made? Pretty simply. You mix a dough of primarily ground shrimp and tapioca starch, steam it, slice it, and set the slices out in the hot sun to dry. The hard crackers are then deep-fried in oil, puffing and expanding to about twice their original size. Good shrimp crackers capture the essence of shrimpy flavor.

Most cooks buy the dry crackers, rather than make them. The frying is fast and neither messy nor oily. Also, you can fry the crackers hours in advance, making a bowl of the crunchy, rich snack an easy accompaniment to cocktails. When used more like a cracker, they're great for scooping up Vietnamese salads called gỏi. In this post, I cover how to select good Indonesian krupuk udang and Vietnamese bánh phồng tôm, plus how to fry how to fry shrimp crackers. There's a how-to video, as well.

How to buy krupuk udang and bánh phồng tôm

As with all snack foods, not all shrimp crackers and shrimp chips are equal. The inexpensive colorful ones sold in boxes are pretty but not much else. When you shop for them, you'll basically have two choices -- Indonesian and/or Vietnamese shrimp crackers.

Indonesian krupuk udang: Indonesian shrimp chips, called krupuk udang, are consistently good and packed with real shrimp flavor. I like the larger chips for easy frying, as you'll see in the video below. Indonesian krupuk (kurupuk) udang are my go-to; look for them labeled as "shrimp crackers" and "bánh quy tôm" for Vietnamese speakers. Indonesian producers make fish and garlic krupuk, too.

Vietnamee bánh phồng tôm: Dried shrimp crackers imported from Vietnam, labeled as "bánh phồng tôm" are getting steadily better. They're often produced in Sa Dec, a city known for its tasty shrimp. That said, Vietnamese shrimp crackers tend to a milder shrimp flavor than the Indonesian kind. I introduced my mom to Indo krupuk udang and she's never gone back to the Viet shrimp crackers.

Where to buy shrimp crackers? Shrimp crackers are sold at East Asian and Southeast Asian markets. Look in the Indonesian section for krupuk. Vietnamese bánh phồng tôm may be nearby too. Or, you can purchase the crackers here.

How to fry shrimp crackers (krupuk udang and bánh phồng tôm)

Watch our step-by-step video and follow these tips for the perfect crunch! Frying shrimp crackers in oil is tastiest for bringing out the crunch and shrimp flavor. It's thrilling but happens fast. Watch this short video to get all my tricks. If you buy Vietnamese bánh phồng tôm, fry them up the same way, though see the recipe below to guide the size of your batches.

What to eat with your shrimp crackers and chips?

There are so many things! The fried shrimp crackers add a richness to lean salads. Start simple with a tofu "egg" salad, or do something fun like serve the crackers with this grapefruit and shrimp salad or ginger chicken salad.

Instead of fried wonton chips, serve the fried crackers with my ginger salmon poke or tofu poke. It's the natural pairing for my herbacious Vietnamese fish salad (gỏi cá), too. The poke and fish salad are Pass the Fish Sauce newsletter recipes.

Let me know how you enjoy the crackers! And what brands you're buying and frying.

Fried Shrimp Chips (Shrimp Crackers, Bánh Phồng Tôm, Krupuk Udang)

Eat the crackers the day you fry them or keep in an airtight container or paper bag overnight (if humidity is low). Fry a lot because people gobble them up!

Ingredients

  • Dried shrimp chips or shrimp crackers
  • Neutral oil for deep-frying such as canola, peanut, or rice bran

Instructions

  • Line a large plate with paper towels and place next to the stove. Pour oil to a depth of ¾ to 1 inch (2 to 2.5cm) into a wok or a Dutch oven and heat over medium heat to 325 to 350°F on a deep-frying thermometer. (If you don't have a thermometer, stand a dry bamboo chopstick in the oil; if small bubbles immediately gather on the surface around the chopstick, the oil is ready.)
  • For each batch, drop in 1 big one or 3 to 5 small chips, keeping in mind that they will double in size. If you fry too many at a time, they will cook unevenly. Each will sink to the bottom, start to bubble, maybe curl, and then expand before rising to the top, where it will finish cooking. All of this happens in 4 to 5 seconds, so don't turn away. Once the chips float to the top, let them fry for only a few seconds longer. Don't let them brown or their delicate flavor will vanish. Using a skimmer, transfer the chips to the towel-lined plate to drain. This is fast-paced frying, so regulate the heat and adjust the size of your batches so that you are working at a comfortable speed. Eat at room temperature.

One of the first “recipes” on this blog was No-Recipe Cherry Jam, posted in 2005, which wasn’t quite a recipe. Nevertheless, a lot of people successfully used those guidelines to make cherry jam over the last fifteen years. The basis for it was how I’ve been making jam forever, using 3 parts sugar to 4 parts fruit puree. The standard ratio is to use equal parts sugar to fruit puree but I like to use less.

I often make cherry jam with sweet cherries, which are what one usually finds at the market. But if you can find sour cherries, those are wonderful in the mix, but when fresh, they have a short shelf life so should be used, pronto.

So feel free to mix things up, cherry-wise!

Unlike other recipes, making jam can require a bit of using your instincts, rather than relying on set times and temperatures. Checking the jam texture on a chilled plate is the best way to tell you when it’s done. (As an example, jam usually sets between 218-220ºF,103-104ºC, but this one set a few degrees higher.) Because cherries are low in pectin, this one often “sets” a few degrees higher, although don’t expect cherry jam to be as stiff as other jams that you might be used to, or buy. It’ll be a bit runny, which is just how it should be.

One tool I do recommend is a cherry pitter. Not everyone has one, and they do fall into the “single use” category, but come cherry season, mine gets a good workout. I wrote more about why you should get a cherry pitter here.

The biggest question I get about jam recipes is people asking about reducing the amount of sugar in them. Sugar in jam does a couple of things. One, it helps the mixture jell, along with any natural pectin in the fruit. And two, it preserves it. If you use less, the jam will be runnier and won’t last as long.

Europeans tend to like their jams runnier than their American counterparts, so in the morning, you can have a European experience when you spoon some of this jam on your morning toast, as we do.

Unlike other jams that wrinkle when you do the wrinkle test (putting a spoonful of jam on a frozen plate, returning the plate to the freezer and when the jam wrinkles with you nudge it, it’s done), this one will be more like thickened syrup. It’s done when it’s thick and sticky and holds its shape for a few moments after you drag a spoon through it (below.) The cherries will be shiny and glossy, like beautiful ruby-colored jewels.

Print

Cherry Jam

If you only have 1 pound (450g) of cherries, feel free to cut the recipe in half. The liqueur is optional but heightens the flavors of the jam. Use only enough so it complements the fruit – you don't want to overwhelm it. As mentioned, this jam will be a bit on the runny side when "set." Sour cherries will produce a softer-set jam than sweet cherries. Course Breakfast Cuisine American Keyword cherries, cherry, jam

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds (900g) sweet or sour cherries or a mix
  • 2 1/2 cups (500g) sugar
  • freshly squeezed juice of one medium lemon plus additional juice if desired
  • 1-2 drops of almond extract or 1-2 teaspoons of kirsch or another liqueur, such as amaretto or vodka

Instructions

  • Remove the stems and pit the cherries. Put them in a large non-reactive pot. If using only sweet cherries, add 2-3 tablespoons of water to the pot. (Sour cherries will exude enough liquid on their own so no need to add additional water.) Put a small plate or saucer in the freezer.
  • Cover the pot and cook over medium heat, stirring occasionally, until the cherries are wilted and cooked through, about 10 minutes.
  • Remove the lid and stir in the sugar and lemon juice. Increase the heat to medium-high and cook, stirring frequently until the mixture thickens. It's hard to say how long it will take, but when the mixture resembles the sixth picture in the post, the one in the pot with the bubbles, turn off the heat and test the jam by putting a small spoonful on the frozen dish. Return the dish to the freezer for a few minutes until the jam on it is cool. Run a spoon through it and if the liquid is very thick and leaves a clear patch, as shown in the photo in the post, it's done. If not, return the plate to the freezer and continue to cook the jam, testing it at various intervals, until it's done. (If you want to use a thermometer to check the jam, it should be ready somewhere between 220º and 225ºF, 104º-107ºC.)
  • When ready, turn off the heat and stir in the almond extract or liqueur (or both, if you wish). If desired, you could add an extra squeeze of lemon juice to balance the sweetness. Ladle into clean jars.

I was recently gifted an overload of strawberries: I bought four baskets from a vendor at the market, whose booth I shop at often, and he threw in two extra baskets for free, the equivalent of a carte de fidelité (loyalty card) in Paris. So I’ve spent the past few days washing, hulling, cutting and cooking my unexpected bounty.

I’ve been making a number of things with the gorgeous strawberries. But I also realized that while I have a Strawberry-Rhubarb Jam recipe on the site, I didn’t have a strawberry jam. So here it is.

Although I’m giving you a recipe for the amount of strawberries that I used, you can use the same proportions if you have just a basket…or a bushel. You’ll just need to do a slight amount of math. Only the cooking time will change, so keep an eye on things.

Jam-making is not something you walk away from and you need to rely more on look and feel, rather than exact times. Another thing about jam-making is that fruit and berry jams are always better made in small batches; the shorter the cooking time, the better the flavor. So if you have more than 2-pounds (900g) of strawberries, consider making jam in a couple of batches rather than one big one.

Print

Strawberry Jam

Many traditional recipes use an equal amount of sugar to fruit, by weight, but I find it’s okay to decrease it to these proportions. Sugar is both a thickener and a preservation agent. If you reduce it too much more, your jam may not thicken and won’t keep for a long time. (Which is okay if you want thinner jam, you keep it in the refrigerator, and eat it relatively quickly.) This recipe can be scaled up or down, depending on how many strawberries you have. But if you want to make a lot more, best to make it in a couple of batches. To prepare the jars, pour boiling water into them, as well as into the lids. Let them sit 5-10 minutes, then carefully pour out the hot water and set them upside down on a cooling rack until ready to fill. Since I keep my jams in the refrigerator, I don’t put them up them in a traditional sense. I usually just overturn the jars and let them cool, which provides a reasonable seal, then refrigerate them. I find my jams keep well this way for up to one year. Check the National Center for Home Food Preservation website for instructions on how to can fruit jams. Note: Last season strawberries tend to have less natural pectin than ones that arrive earlier. So your jam may be less jelled. One strategy is the finely grate a green apple into the strawberries before cooking them, which adds pectin. (There are guidelines here for using liquid and powdered pectin.) I don’t mind the slightly runnier jam and it’s terrific on yogurt or served with fresh goat cheese for dessert.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds (900g) strawberries
  • 1 1/4 pounds (2 2/3 cups, 580g) sugar
  • one lemon organic or unsprayed
  • optional: kirsch (or eau-de-vie, or crème de cassis)

Instructions

  • Wash and hull the strawberries and cut them into quarters. If some are larger or smaller, just make sure the pieces all about the same size.
  • 2. Toss them in a large pot, such as a Dutch oven casserole made of a nonreactive material, with the sugar. Cut the lemon in half, squeeze the juice into the pot, and add the lemon halves to the berries. Stir well, cover, and let sit a couple of hours (up to 8 hours) at room temperature, stirring one or two times while they marinate.
  • Put a small plate in the freezer. Turn the heat on under the strawberries to medium-high and cook the strawberries, stirring occasionally, as they reduce and the juices thicken. If a lot of foam rises to the surface, skim it away. When the syrup is the consistency of warm maple syrup – it will take about 10 to 15 minutes or so, depending on the pot and berries, turn off the heat and put a spoonful of the jam on the frozen plate and return the plate to the freezer. Check it in a few minutes; if it wrinkles when you nudge it, it’s done. If not, return the plate to the freezer and cook the jam a little more, testing it again. It may take a few times before you get it to the right consistency.
  • When the strawberry jam is done, remove the lemon and stir in a few drops of kirsch, if using, then ladle the jam into the prepared jars.

When I baked at Chez Panisse, we used something we made, that someone dubbed “Moon Dust.” Which, because I can never leave anything alone, I nicknamed “Lunar Lint.” It was a mix of flour, almond meal, sugar and crushed amaretti cookies.

Because it was Sunday in Paris, and most stores are closed, I couldn’t go on a hunt for amaretti cookies. But no matter what the day – they’re rather hard to find. So I used to buy them and keep them in my cabinet, but found I was snacking on them, rather than saving them for lunaire lint. (And yes, I’ve got a recipe for amaretti in Ready for Dessert, but I didn’t have time to DIY everything, especially after making several batches of jam, and cleaning up after each one.)

The apricots that I used were a little more restrained, and not much juice gushed out during baking. Still, no one called me out, saying the frangipane wasn’t necessary. In fact, my guests loved the thin, flavorful layer of almond cream under the fruit, which complimented the apricots and cherries, which, as the younger generation says these days, was a sick combination.

I’m still not on board with that word, so I’ll go back to my California roots and say the combination was “awesome,” or in French, they’d say it was chouette (an owl). Depending on where you live, you can make this galette with other stone fruits, or even figs, pears or apples, when they’re in season. I don’t give a hoot which ones you use, but do give it a go with the frangipane. One bite, and you’ll agree that it’s a wise move to use it.

Summer Fruit Galette

Normally apricots give up a lot of juice when baked, but these late-season ones didn't. Fruit varies in moisture but the nice thing about the layer of frangipane is that it keeps the tart dough crisp on the bottom, in case your fruit is juicy. Almond flour is sometimes called almond meal. You can use blanched or unblanched, which has a darker color. If you're avoiding almonds, feel free to swap in hazelnut or pistachio flour. You can certainly use other fruits in this tart, such as sliced nectarines, peaches (peeled or unpeeled), plums, or figs. You'll need about 1 1/2 pounds (700g) of fruit. More important than precise numbers to follow, it's best to try to push the fruit as close together as possible, or if using sliced fruit, to overlap it as much as possible, because it'll cook down during baking. You can also make this without the frangipane but you may want to sprinkle the bottom of the tart dough with some crumbled cookies (such as speculoos or Graham crackers) or moon dust if the fruit is particularly juicy. You can also swap out raspberries or blueberries for the cherries, using them to fill in the spaces between the fruit. I add the cherries at the beginning, but if using raspberries or blueberries, you can add them during the last 15 minutes or so of baking. Course Dessert Keyword apricot, cherries, galette, tart Servings 8 servings

Ingredients

For the galette dough

  • 1 1/4 cups (175g) all-purpose flour
  • 2 teaspoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 7 tablespoons (100g, 3 1/2 ounces) unsalted butter cubed and chilled
  • 3 1/2 to 4 tablespoons ice water

For the frangipane

  • 3/4 cup (95g) almond flour
  • 1/4 cup (50g) sugar
  • 2 tablespoons (20g) all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons (30g, 1 ounce) unsalted butter at room temperature
  • 1/4 to 1/2 teaspoon pure almond extract
  • 1 large egg, at room temperature

For the fruit galette

  • 1 1/2 pounds (700g) fresh apricots, halved and pitted, or quartered if large (see headnote for other fruits that can be used)
  • 18-20 cherries, sweet or tart, pitted
  • 1 tablespoon (15g, 1/2 ounce) unsalted butter melted
  • 2 tablespoons coarse sugar (or granulated sugar)
  • strained apricot jam, for glazing the tart

Instructions

To make the dough

  • To make the dough, mix together the flour, sugar, and salt in a medium bowl, or in the bowl of a stand mixer.
  • Add the cubed butter and mix, using a pastry blender (or the paddle attachment if using a stand mixer), until the butter is in small, but still visible pieces, about the size of corn kernels.
  • Add the 3 1/2 tablespoons of ice water and mix until the dough starts to come together. If it feels dry, add an additional 1/2 tablespoon of ice water. Form the dough into a disk with your hands, wrap it in plastic wrap and refrigerate for at least 30 minutes.

To make the frangipane

  • In a small bowl, mix together the almond flour, sugar, and all-purpose flour. Add the butter and almond extract, and mix in with a spatula until smooth. Mix in the egg.

To make the tart

  • Preheat the oven to 375ºF (190ºC). Line a baking sheet with parchment paper.
  • On a lightly floured surface, roll the dough to a 14-inch (35cm) circle, turning it over and dusting lightly with flour, to keep it from sticking to the counter.
  • Brush off any excess flour and transfer the dough to the prepared baking sheet. (At this point, you may want to chill the dough for a few minutes, as the frangipane may be a bit tricky to spread if the dough feels warm.)
  • Smear the frangipane on the tart dough, leaving a 2-inch (5cm) rim around the edges. (If the frangipane is quite thick, you can dollop it in 5-6 places over the tart dough, which'll make it easier to spread.) Place the apricots on the frangipane and put cherries in the spaces between the apricots. Fold the edges of the dough over the fruit to make a crust. After pleating and folding the rim, make sure there are no "gullies" in the pleats for fruit juices to run out of. Pinch together any cracks.
  • Brush the rim of the crust generously with melted butter then brush the remaining butter over the fruit. Sprinkle the rim with about 1 tablespoon of coarse or granulated sugar. Then sprinkle another 1 tablespoon over the fruit. (If using fruits like nectarines and peaches, you can reduce the sugar to a few teaspoons.)
  • Bake the tart until the crust is well-browned and the fruit is cooked through, about 40-50 minutes. Remove from the oven and let the tart cool for 5 to 10 minutes, then brush the fruit with apricot jam.

Notes

Serving: Serve with a compatible ice cream, such as vanilla, crème fraîche or noyau ice cream. (The last two are in my books, The Perfect Scoop, and My Paris Kitchen, respectively), or another favorite flavor. A dollop of crème fraîche, sabayon, or softly whipped cream are other options. (Although often I like it just as it is.) Storage: The dough can be made up to two days ahead and refrigerated, or it can be frozen for up to two months. The tart is best enjoyed the same day it’s made, but is okay the second day.

Easy Ground Beef and Potato Skillet

If you grew up eating Hamburger Helper, this ground beef and potato recipe will feel instantly familiar—but it’s made entirely with real, recognizable ingredients. It’s a wholesome, naturally gluten-free, and budget-friendly dinner that delivers all the cozy comfort of the boxed classic without the mystery packets. Course Dinner Cuisine American Prep Time 10 minutes minutes Cook Time 30 minutes minutes Total Time 40 minutes minutes Servings 6 Calories 441kcal Author Michelle Tam

Equipment

  • 1 Large, deep skillet with a lid

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon avocado oil or other high temperature cooking oil
  • 1 medium yellow onion diced
  • 2 pounds lean ground beef 90/10 works great
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 2 teaspoons Magic Mushroom Powder or Diamond Crystal kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon Italian herb blend optional
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 cup chicken broth or bone broth
  • 1 cup full-fat coconut milk
  • 3 tablespoons nutritional yeast
  • 16 ounces frozen mixed vegetables
  • 16 ounces frozen hashed browns or peeled and shredded Yukon Gold potatoes
  • 5 cups baby spinach
  • Freshly ground black pepper to taste

Instructions

  • Heat a large, deep 12-inch skillet over medium-high heat. When the pan is hot, swirl in the avocado oil. Add the onion and cook until softened, about 3 minutes.
  • Add the ground beef and cook, breaking it up with a spatula, until no longer pink and most of the liquid has cooked off, about 6 to 8 minutes.
  • Next, stir in the tomato paste, Magic Mushroom Powder (or salt and pepper), Italian herb blend (if using), and garlic. Cook for about one minute, or until fragrant.
  • In a large measuring cup or bowl, whisk together the chicken bone broth, coconut milk, and nutritional yeast until smooth. Pour the sauce into the skillet, scraping up any browned bits from the bottom.
  • Add the bag of frozen mixed vegetables and frozen shredded potatoes directly to the skillet (do not thaw). Stir well to coat everything in the sauce.
  • Increase the heat to high and cook, stirring occasionally, until the mixture comes to a steady simmer. Reduce the heat to medium or medium-low, or just enough to maintain a simmer. Cover the skillet and cook for 6 to 8 minutes, stirring once halfway through, or until the potatoes are tender and the frozen veggies are cooked through.
  • Remove the lid and continue to simmer uncovered for about 5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the sauce reduces and thickens to a saucy consistency.
  • Add the baby spinach and stir just until wilted, about 1 to 2 minutes.
  • Grind some freshly ground pepper on top and mix it in. Take a bite of the finished dish and adjust seasoning, with extra Magic Mushroom Powder or salt, if needed. Remove from heat and serve hot.

Notes

  • Frozen vegetables and hash browns go straight from the freezer—no washing, no chopping, no squeezing.
  • Don’t squeeze shredded potatoes; their natural starch helps thicken the sauce.
  • The finished  dish should look saucy, not dry.
  • Leftovers reheat beautifully and thicken a bit more overnight.

Despite the ever-widening swath of restaurants serving Prince’s signature dish around the globe, you have to visit the original purveyor to get real-deal Nashville hot chicken. As the story goes, the ultra-spicy sandwich was created as punishment by a jilted lover of Thornton Prince; but, instead of catching on fire, Prince loved the spice so much that he’d go on to perfect the recipe that launched his business. Today, Prince’s remains the best in class, serving up fried chicken spiced with cayenne pepper oil atop a slice of white bread with a pickle (a foil to all that cayenne). If you’re sticking closer to downtown, swing by Prince’s outpost in the Assembly Food hall to get your hot chicken fix. 

Wendell Smith’s

407 53rd Avenue North
(615) 383-7114

Courtesy Wendell Smith’s

Wood-paneled walls line the dining room of this classic meat ’n’ three restaurant that’s been serving up Southern soul since 1952. Every day, the fourth-generation owners welcome a regular crowd hungry for roast beef, baked ham, and pit barbecue, plus daily specials like fried catfish, chicken and dumplings, and throwback fixins like candied yams, turnip greens, baked apples, fried corn, and creamed potatoes. Wendell Smith’s is a slice of Nashville that has hung on to its corner lot despite unchecked development all around it, still sharing space with the adjacent liquor store of the same name that’s been operating just as long. 

Loveless Cafe

8400 Highway 100
(615) 646-9700 

Courtesy Loveless Cafe

What began 70 years ago as a humble motel and café at the start of the Natchez Trace Parkway has been thriving from the moment Lon and Annie Love began serving biscuits and fried chicken to travelers out of their home kitchen. After their continuing success, the couple turned their home into a restaurant and opened 14 rooms as a motel to accompany their new dining destination. The motel went out of operation years ago, and its rooms are now home to retail space, with smoked hams, jams, and biscuit mix on offer. Though there is now expanded outdoor seating and an outdoor bar serving bloody Marys when the wait is long, there’s nothing better than grabbing a seat in the tiny dining room that still feels like home.  

Sperry’s Restaurant

5109 Harding Pike
(615) 353-0809

Courtesy Sperry’s Restaurant

An institution since 1974, this legendary West Nashville steakhouse is a windowless dining room featuring backlit stained glass, carpeting, a stone fireplace with a roaring fire (most nights), and sporty portraits of the hounds and horses that once lived on the grounds of the abutting Belle Meade Mansion. Martinis and Manhattans pair perfectly with Sperry’s steaks and seafood, from a filet Oscar smothered in crabmeat and béarnaise to king crab legs served with drawn butter. Nashville’s very first salad bar remains gloriously intact here, and diners can pile their plate with neon-green goddess and bacon crumbles (though the plated iceberg wedge salad topped with bacon and black olives is my choice). Bananas Foster, another highlight, is served tableside in a plume of booze-soaked flames and cinnamon-fueled sparks. The doors open at four for happy hour, when sausage-stuffed mushrooms and discounted cocktails draw a steady stream of locals each day.
