  • Garlic: Adds a little bite without overpowering the sweet pepper. Use a small clove for a milder dip.
  • Ground cumin and smoked paprika: Add earthy warmth and smokiness.
  • Nutritional yeast: Adds savory depth and a lightly cheesy flavor.

🌱 Substitutions

This recipe is versatile and easy to adapt. Below are some simple ingredient substitutions.

  • Chickpeas: Use any tender cooked beans or lentils in the same measured amount. Drain well before blending.
  • Sprouted variation: Substitute thoroughly cooked, drained sprouted legumes. Cook until tender enough to mash easily.
  • Fresh roasted pepper: Roast a large red bell pepper until softened and blistered. Peel, remove the stem and seeds, and drain before using.
  • No nutritional yeast: Omit it for a slightly less savory hummus.
  • Milder garlic: Use half a clove or substitute a soft roasted garlic clove.
  • Spicy hummus: Add a pinch of cayenne or red pepper flakes.
  • No smoked paprika: Use sweet paprika for a milder flavor without the smokiness.

📝 How to Make Bell Pepper Hummus

The complete recipe with exact amounts is below. These additional tips will help you get the best results.

  1. Prepare the legumes: Drain and rinse canned chickpeas, beans, or lentils. If using home-cooked legumes, cook until tender and drain well. For an especially creamy texture, simmer firm canned legumes in water until very soft, then drain.
  1. Using sprouts? Cook sprouted legumes thoroughly until tender, then drain well before blending.
  1. Whip the tahini: Add the tahini, lemon juice, and ice water to a high-speed blender or food processor. Blend for about one minute, until pale and creamy. Scrape down the sides as needed.
  1. Combine the ingredients: Add the legumes, drained roasted pepper, garlic, cumin, smoked paprika, nutritional yeast, and salt, followed by water.
  1. Blend until smooth: Blend, scraping down the sides and pausing as needed according to your appliance's instructions. Add more ice water, one tablespoon at a time, until the hummus is smooth and fluffy.
  1. Adjust the flavor and texture: Taste and add more lemon juice or salt as needed. Leave the hummus slightly looser than your desired consistency because it thickens as it cools. Transfer to a serving bowl or refrigerate for later.

✔️ Storage

Refrigerator: Store in an airtight container for up to 4 days. Refrigerate promptly after preparation.

Before serving: Stir well. Add a little cold water or lemon juice if it has thickened.

Freezer: Freeze in small airtight portions for up to 3 months for best quality. Thaw in the refrigerator, then stir or briefly blend to restore the texture.

🙋🏽‍♀️ Recipe FAQs

Can I make bell pepper hummus without chickpeas?

Yes! Substitute the same amount of tender cooked beans or lentils. White beans make a particularly creamy dip, while darker beans and lentils change the color and flavor. Drain well and adjust the water as you blend.

Can I use canned beans or lentils?

Yes. Canned chickpeas, beans, and lentils make this recipe especially convenient. Drain and rinse them before measuring. If they feel firm, simmer until soft for a smoother hummus.

Do I need to remove the chickpea skins?

No. Tender chickpeas and thorough blending are usually enough for a creamy hummus. Removing the skins is optional if you prefer an especially silky texture.

Can I use sprouted legumes?

Yes. Use thoroughly cooked, drained sprouted legumes instead of regular cooked chickpeas, beans, or lentils. Cook until they mash easily, then follow the recipe as written.

Can I use a food processor instead of a blender to make hummus?

Yes. A food processor works well, although a high-speed blender usually produces a silkier consistency. Scrape down the sides and add water gradually as needed.

Bell Pepper Hummus Recipe

Creamy bell pepper hummus with tahini, lemon, and smoky paprika. An easy, flavorful way to add plant-based iron using chickpeas, beans, or lentils. Course Condiment, Side Dish Cuisine Vegan Diet Vegan, Vegetarian Prep Time 10 minutes minutes Cook Time 0 minutes minutes Total Time 10 minutes minutes Servings 8 servings Calories 328kcal Author Nisha Melvani

Equipment

  • Blender or food processor

Ingredients

  • 3 cups cooked beans or chickpeas, or lentils (see notes)
  • ½ cup tahini
  • 3 tablespoons fresh lemon juice plus more to taste (about 1 ½ small lemons)
  • ⅓ cup ice water plus more as needed
  • 1 large roasted red bell pepper jarred, drained
  • 1 clove garlic
  • ½ teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon smoked paprika
  • 2 tablespoons nutritional yeast
  • ¼ teaspoon salt plus more to taste

Instructions

  • Prepare the legumes: Drain and rinse canned chickpeas, beans, or lentils. If using home-cooked or sprouted legumes, cook until thoroughly cooked and tender, then drain well.
  • Whip the tahini: Blend the tahini, lemon juice, and ice water until pale and creamy.
  • Blend until smooth: Add the prepared legumes, roasted red pepper, garlic, cumin, smoked paprika, nutritional yeast, and salt. Blend until silky, scraping down the sides as needed. Add more ice water gradually until smooth and fluffy.
  • Adjust and serve: Taste and adjust the lemon juice and salt. Leave slightly looser than your desired consistency-it thickens as it cools. Serve with vegetables, crackers, or toast.

Notes

Legume options: Use chickpeas, white beans, other beans, lentils, or a combination. Measure after cooking and draining. You will need approximately two 15-ounce cans. For extra-creamy hummus: Simmer firm cooked or canned legumes until very soft. Drain and blend while warm. Sprouted option: Cook sprouted legumes thoroughly until tender before using. Cooking time varies by variety and sprout size. Find my seeds here. Water: The amount needed varies with the legumes and how well the roasted pepper is drained. Add gradually. Storage: Refrigerate in an airtight container for up to 4 days. Stir before serving and loosen with a little cold water or lemon juice if needed. Freeze for up to 3 months. Nutrition: Values vary depending on the chickpeas, beans, or lentils used.

This Stuffed Pepper Soup has all the cozy flavors of classic stuffed peppers, with browned, savory ground beef, tender bell peppers, onions, and garlic simmered in a rich, generously seasoned tomato broth. I honestly could not stop eating it! It’s easy, hearty, satisfying, and you get all that familiar flavor without the extra steps of stuffing peppers. I serve it over fluffy rice and, don’t worry, I didn’t forget the cheese! A sprinkle of mozzarella gives you that irresistible cheese pull. Better yet, it’s ready in just 45 minutes and costs only $1.97 per serving. Perfect for soup season, or any season!

Big Flavor from a Simple Shopping List

I already love classic stuffed bell peppers, but this soup makes enjoying those same flavors so much easier on a weeknight. In most stuffed pepper recipes, the rice goes straight into the filling. For this soup, though, I prefer to keep it separate until serving. Rice soaks up liquid like a sponge, and I found that stirring it straight into the pot made the rice mushy. And by the next day, there was barely any broth left! Instead, I like to spoon the rice into individual bowls and ladle the soup over the top. It’s such a simple step, but it makes a big difference…especially when I’m saving leftovers.

I also make the most of my cooking time by getting the rice going while I prepare the soup. Once the ground beef and veggies (bell peppers, onion, and garlic) are cooked, I simmer everything for 15 minutes in a rich tomato broth packed with herbs. And, of course, I wanted to keep this soup easy on the grocery budget, too. I use green bell peppers (which are usually cheaper than red, yellow, or orange), canned tomatoes, dried herbs, and Better Than Bouillon for the broth. Nothing fancy or expensive, but plenty of flavor! These simple ingredients help me stretch just one pound of ground beef into six hearty servings. 😋

Recipe Success Tips

  1. Let those peppers stand out! I cut my bell peppers and onion into a medium dice because I like them to have some texture and really stand out in the finished soup. Keep the pieces roughly the same size so they cook evenly. After the sauté and 15-minute simmer, the peppers should be tender enough to pierce with a fork but still hold their shape. If they’re a little firm, give the soup a few more minutes.
  2. Remove the excess grease. Depending on the type of ground beef you use (I use 27% fat), quite a bit of grease can collect in the pot as it cooks. Make sure to carefully blot up the excess with a paper towel so your soup doesn’t end up oily. You can also spoon the grease into a heatproof container, let it cool, and discard it in the trash. Just don’t pour it down the sink!
  3. Make it as brothy as you like. Prefer a slightly thinner soup? Stir in a splash of extra beef broth or water until you’re happy with the consistency. If there’s more liquid than you’d like, let it simmer uncovered for a few extra minutes, stirring occasionally. I recommend tasting and adjusting the seasoning before serving.
  4. Got extra veggies? Throw them in! This soup is a great way to use up whatever you’ve got. Diced carrots or celery can go in with the peppers and onion, although carrots may need a little longer to soften. Corn can be added with the tomatoes and broth, and fresh spinach only needs a minute or two at the end. You can even add diced potatoes (I’d dice them small, like in our vegetable beef soup), but they’ll need a longer simmer and possibly some extra broth!

Budget-Saving Tip

When I make stuffed peppers at home, I typically use ground beef. However, the ground beef makes up about half the cost of this entire pot of soup, so it’s also a great place to save some money. Feel free to use ground chicken, turkey, pork, or sausage instead. (You can even try lentils!)

Higher-fat ground beef is often cheaper than lean, and it’s totally fine to use here. Just drain off the excess fat after browning so your soup doesn’t end up oily. If you still want some beef in your soup but need to stretch your budget further, try ½ lb. ground beef with 1 cup cooked brown or green lentils. Brown the beef as directed, then stir in the cooked lentils with the tomatoes and broth to heat through during the final simmer. We use a similar meat-stretching trick in our lentil and sausage stew, and it’s a great way to make a little meat go further.

how to make Stuffed Pepper Soup step-by-step photos

Gather all of your ingredients: Cut 2 green bell peppers into a medium dice, finely dice 1 onion, and mince 2 garlic cloves. I like to get the chopping done first since the rice and soup cook at the same time.

Cook the rice: Add 1 cup uncooked long-grain white rice and 2 cups water to a medium saucepan with a lid. Bring to a boil, then reduce the heat to a gentle simmer. Cover and cook for 20 minutes, or until the rice is tender and the water has been absorbed. Turn off the heat and set aside. (This is the same technique we use in our ‘how to cook rice’ guide).

Brown the ground beef: While the rice simmers, heat 1 Tbsp vegetable oil in a large pot over medium heat. Once the oil is hot, add 1 lb. ground beef and cook for about 5 minutes, breaking it into small crumbles as it cooks. The beef should be cooked through, with no pink remaining.

Remove the excess grease: Carefully blot up any excess grease from the beef using paper towels. This helps prevent an oily layer from forming on top of the finished soup.

Soften the vegetables: Add the 2 diced green bell peppers, 1 diced onion, 2 minced garlic cloves, 1½ tsp salt, and 1 tsp black pepper to the pot with the beef. Cook for 5 minutes, stirring occasionally. The onions and peppers should start to soften but still have some texture.

Add the tomatoes and seasonings: Pour in one 14.5 oz. can of diced tomatoes (including the juices), one 15 oz. can of tomato sauce, and 2 cups beef broth. Add 1 tsp dried oregano, ½ tsp dried basil, ¼ tsp dried parsley, and ¼ tsp paprika. Stir everything together until the seasonings are evenly distributed throughout the tomato broth.

Simmer the soup: Bring the mixture to a gentle simmer and cook for 15 minutes to allow the flavors to develop and the broth to thicken slightly. The bell peppers should be tender enough to pierce easily with a fork while still holding their shape. If they’re still too firm, let the soup simmer a little longer.

Serve with rice and cheese: Fluff the cooked rice with a fork and divide it evenly among six serving bowls (about ½ cup per bowl).

Ladle a heaping cup of stuffed pepper soup over each portion of rice, then sprinkle with the shredded mozzarella (1 cup total). Serve warm and enjoy! (For a bonus, I sprinkled fresh parsley on top. Most fresh herbs would be a great garnish, like basil, thyme, or chives, or you can enjoy the recipe as is!)

Storage & Reheating

I recommend storing any leftover rice and soup separately because the rice will continue absorbing the broth as it sits. Let everything cool promptly, then transfer to separate airtight containers and refrigerate for up to 3-4 days.

Reheating

Reheat the soup in a saucepan over medium heat, stirring occasionally, or in the microwave until heated through. Warm the rice separately in the microwave (with a splash of water, stirring every 15-30 seconds until hot), then spoon the soup over the top. If you’ve already mixed the rice into the soup and it’s become too thick, add a splash of beef broth or water to thin it out as it reheats.

Freezing

You can freeze leftover stuffed pepper soup for up to 3 months in freezer-safe containers. I recommend freezing the rice separately to keep it from getting mushy. Thaw overnight in the refrigerator, or use the thaw setting on your microwave, then reheat thoroughly before serving.

Jalapeno Corn Pudding is a sweet and savory cornbread with a spicy kick. With a golden brown crust and a soft, custard-like center, it’s American comfort food at its best.

I’ve loved corn pudding for as long as I can remember. Adding jalapenos gives the casserole a little bit of heat that I find irresistible.

Jalapeno Corn Pudding

Growing up, my mom made corn pudding for almost every holiday meal or special occasion. It’s a tradition I’ve carried on with my own kids, although I make my corn pudding from scratch instead of from a boxed mix.

Jalapeno Corn Pudding has all the nostalgic creamed corn flavor of the original but with a spicy twist. The heat from the jalapenos gives a satisfying balance to the sweet corn without lighting your mouth totally on fire. I love this so much that I’ve even been known to sneak bites of the leftovers cold, straight from the fridge! I hope it brings the same level of joy to your table.

Ingredients and Substitutions

Corn – You’ll need both whole kernel canned corn and creamed corn.

Dry Ingredients – I use cornmeal, along with all purpose flour, baking powder, and salt to give the pudding structure.

Eggs and Sugar – These create the custard-like consistency and enhance the natural sweetness of the corn.

Sour Cream and Butter – I use full fat sour cream for moisture and tang, along with salted, melted butter for rich flavor.

Jalapenos – You can use any jarred or canned jalapenos to add heat and spice.

Notes on Equipment

  • Chef’s knife. Food prep is so much easier when you have a quality knife! I first tried this knife 10 years ago, while visiting a professional test kitchen and you could say it was love at first slice. (And I was thrilled to discover how reasonably priced they are!) That’s why mine has been in my kitchen ever since.
  • Cutting board
  • Large mixing bowl
  • Spatula or spoonula
  • Measuring cups and spoons
  • 9×13 Baking dish. I adore Pyrex dishes. They’re one of those kitchen staples that that just makes cooking easier. And, I can vouch for how sturdy and durable they are (I’ve had some of mine almost 20 years). And, who doesn’t love a dish that comes with a tight-fitting lid?

How to Make Jalapeno Corn Pudding

Preheating the Oven: Set your oven temperature to 350°F. While my oven preheats, I grease a 9 x 13 pan.

Preparing the Jalapenos: Drain the jalapenos from the jar and chop them into small pieces.

Combining the Eggs and Sugar: You’ll add the eggs and sugar to your mixing bowl. Whisk them together until well combined.

Adding the Wet Ingredients: Next, I add the sour cream, followed by the creamed corn, canned corn kernels, butter, and jalapenos. Give the mixture a good stir after adding each ingredient.

Mixing in the Dry Ingredients: We’ll add the flour, cornmeal, baking powder, and salt to the corn mixture. Stir until well combined.

Baking the Corn Pudding: I pour the batter into my greased baking dish and place the baking dish in the oven to bake until golden brown, about 50 minutes.

Cooling and Serving: I let the it cool for about 10 minutes before serving. You can serve in square slices or scoop it onto plates with a big serving spoon.

Expert Tip

The finished dish has the very mildest of heat, just a hint really. That said, if you want a dish that will make everyone at the table sit up and take notice, you can substitute diced green chiles with fantastic results. The canned green chiles will give the corn pudding an earthier, smokier heat that pairs beautifully with creamy corn – and you can choose anything from mild to extra hot heat levels too.

Bake Times May Vary – Ovens all have distinct “personalities” that can affect your overall baking time. The first time you make the recipe, check it after 50 minutes of baking. If the top is still pale and the center looks runny, leave it in the oven for another 5 minutes, then check again.

Serving Suggestions

Jalapeno corn pudding pairs well with southwest flavors like Green Chile Chicken Cordon Bleu or Pan Fried Southwest Chicken. And it’s also terrific with a BBQ potluck spread of Pulled Pork, Memphis Style Coleslaw, and Slow Cooker Baked Beans.

Storage and Reheating

How to Store: Store leftovers tightly covered in the refrigerator for up to 2 days or in the freezer for up to one month. You can also freeze the entire casserole or divide it into individual portions for easy reheating.

How to Reheat: Reheat individual servings in the microwave until warmed through. You could also reheat the entire pan, covered with foil, in the oven. Thaw (if frozen), cover the pan with foil, and reheat at 300°F until warm.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use Jiffy Mix instead of cornmeal?

Not in this recipe. Jiffy cornbread mix has flour and other added ingredients, so it won’t work as a one-to-one substitute here.

Can I leave out the sugar?

The sugar really is necessary here. It not only adds sweetness to the pudding, it’s also responsible for some of the texture. Sugar binds to the egg yolks creating the consistency you recognize in custards and baked cheesecakes.

How can I tell when corn pudding is fully cooked?

You want the pudding to look lightly golden brown on top and a bit firm. The center should still have some “give” to it but not be runny like cake batter.

Can I make this gluten free?

You bet! Just substitute 4 tablespoons of brown rice flour and 2 tablespoons of potato starch for the all-purpose flour in this recipe. Alternatively, you can simply substitute 1:1 with the GF blend of your preference, if you already have a favorite.

Jalapeno Corn Pudding

This savory sweet jalapeno corn pudding is an American classic side dish. Kick it up to a new level with the addition of spicy peppers. Course Side Dish Cuisine American Prep Time 10 minutes minutes Cook Time 50 minutes minutes Total Time 1 hour hour Servings 12 servings Calories 212kcal Author Mary Younkin

Ingredients

  • 4 eggs
  • ½ cup sugar
  • ½ cup sour cream
  • 15 ounces canned creamed corn
  • 15 ounces whole kernel corn drained
  • 4 ounces canned or jarred jalapenos diced small, about ½ cup
  • 4 tablespoons butter melted and slightly cooled
  • ½ cup all-purpose flour plus 2 tablespoons
  • 6 tablespoons cornmeal
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • ¼ teaspoon kosher salt

Instructions

  • Preheat the oven to 350 degrees. Grease a 9×13 baking dish and set aside.
  • Whisk together the eggs and the sugar and then add in the sour cream, creamed corn, whole corn, jalapenos, and butter, stirring between each addition.
  • Add the flour, cornmeal, baking powder, and salt to the corn mixture. Stir until well combined.
  • Pour into the prepared pan and bake for 50-55 minutes, depending on your pan. When the pudding is golden brown and slightly firm to the touch, it is done.
  • Let cool at least ten minutes before serving. Scoop with a spoon or slice into squares to serve.

Notes

* Chopped green chiles may be substituted for the jalapenos in this recipe for extra heat.

Has Substitution Culture Gone Too Far?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

This excerpt was originally published in Pre Shift, our newsletter for the hospitality industry. Subscribe for more first-person accounts, advice, and interviews.

When Chanita Chayaluk decided to open her restaurant and wine bar Taan, she hoped “to showcase Thai food in Chicago in a different light,” she says. “The first [wave of] immigration of Thai people in Chicago was in the 1970s, and a lot of [Thai] food here still feels like it’s from that time.” In September, she opened with a menu including miang, a Northern Thai dish made with fermented tea leaves, and a whole, deep-fried fish in a fish-sauce caramel. 

Another quirk of operating in Chicago? “We’re still pretty ‘Midwest nice,’” says Chayaluk. “We try to overaccommodate and appease the masses.” In the early days of service, guests made frequent requests for substitutions or accommodations due to preferences and dietary restrictions. “It would slow down service and speed, and that was not just affecting us, but the guest experience in many ways,” she says. With a small kitchen and staff, it was hard to keep up. “It just felt like we were Chipotle.” When a guest asked for green curry mussels “but without the mussels,” it was a turning point. 

Chayaluk implemented a limited substitution policy at Taan, stating on Instagram that ingredients like fish sauce and shrimp paste are “the fabric that defines our cuisine,” and therefore, could not be removed from any dishes that include it. 

A decade or so ago, substitution culture was generally frowned upon and derided. Recently, though, some restaurants report it bubbling back up—and in full force. There’s a swirl of factors that got us here: Lunch slop bowl spots primed customers to expect a detailed level of customization with their orders. Unreasonable Hospitality pushed an intensified “customer is always right” mentality. And influencers entering the food-review space have made criticism of anything—including a restaurant choosing not to honor certain dietary preferences—ripe for an immediate, viral takedown. Couple all of that with the fact that it is hard to keep a restaurant afloat these days, and you’ve got several operators giving in, whether to specific requests, or to altering their menus altogether (see also: the great de-spicification of menus.)

At the same time, restaurants like Taan and New York’s Ha’s Snack Bar—which proudly announces in its Instagram bio that everything contains fish sauce—are standing their ground.

For Lom Wong in Phoenix, Arizona, substitution and modification requests have ramped up after receiving national attention. Chef Yotaka “Sunny” Martin was recognized by the James Beard Awards last year, and the restaurant was named a Michelin Bib Gourmand in August. “[After the award,] we had more guests than we were used to picking things apart. They were asking us to modify dishes or not understanding our service style and why we weren’t coursing things in a European fashion,” says chef and co-owner Alex Martin. “[There is a] European distinction of what an award-winning restaurant looks like, so people equated that recognition with a white-tablecloth restaurant. We were just like, ‘Well, that’s not who we’ve ever been, and it’s never what our restaurant will ever be.’”

Like Taan, Lom Wong took to social media to set expectations, including that the restaurant would not “make substitutions simply to make a dish more familiar,” and that certain dishes might differ from Western expectations of them; beef might have gristle, fishes will be whole, and both choices are fully intentional. “Sunny is our guiding light of cultural context,” says Martin. “[She advises, considering if] changing something about a dish compromises the integrity of the dish.” For example, the mango salad can be ordered with the shrimp on the side, but the restaurant won’t remove beef or shrimp paste from dishes that necessitate them. 

Chayaluk takes a similar approach at Taan; there is always a vegan, gluten-free option (currently a fried rice) that she describes as a “catchall” for those who can’t have other items on the menu. “We make sure that that dish is also really, really good and substantial; it’s not just an appetizer,” she says. “It’s not an afterthought.” 

Staff at both restaurants are encouraged to uphold the boundaries, which both Chayaluk and Martin say are as much in place for protecting their staff’s peace as they are for ensuring quality control for guests. While there has been some backlash, the expectation-setting at the start of the meal (and before, thanks to the social media posts) has for the most part been taken well. “The way we explain [our policy] to guests is kindly saying we respect your preferences, but they’re your preferences,” says Martin, “and steering them toward dishes on the menu that might already align with those.”
