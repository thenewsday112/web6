There are precious few food products that have been enjoyed for as much of human history as Prosciutto di Parma. The signature silky texture and buttery, sweet-savory flavor of the “King of Hams” is recognized around the world as the result of carefully controlled production methods, dedication to quality, and traditions continued for centuries.

The history of Prosciutto di Parma reaches back more than 2,000 years, to Roman times, when the art of preserving pork through salting and natural drying was already well established in the Po Valley. As early as the 3rd century BC, Cato the Elder described methods for curing pork legs with salt and allowing them to dry and mature. Today, Prosciutto di Parma is produced exclusively in a strictly defined area of northern Italy, in the province of Parma in the Emilia-Romagna region, where local know-how combines with distinctive climatic conditions. Humid breezes from the Tyrrhenian Sea become drier as they flow over the Apennines and down onto the hills. Prosciutto producers discovered that this created an ideal environment for dry aging, and they built storerooms with tall windows that could be opened and closed as necessary, allowing the air to circulate through the racks of hams for gradual drying.

The specifics of aging, like every step in the process, are governed by a consortium—the Consorzio del Prosciutto di Parma—that was founded in 1963 to protect the authenticity of the product. In 1996, Prosciutto di Parma obtained the European Union’s Protected Designation of Origin (PDO). These have a red and yellow seal to show that a product is made in a certain geographical area using time-honored, traditional methods. Grana Padano, a nutty, aged hard cheese also from Northern Italy that’s often paired with Prosciutto di Parma on a charcuterie plate, was awarded a PDO the same year.

Grana Padano PDO and Prosciutto di Parma PDO also share the distinction of being fire-branded—a sign for consumers that you are buying the real thing. Only Parma hams that meet the PDO’s specifications can receive the five-pronged Ducal Crown that signifies a true Prosciutto di Parma. 

Well before that brand is applied, production starts with the pigs. Which breeds, where they can be bred and raised (in 11 Italian regions), and what they are fed (a list that also includes whey from local cheesemakers) are all precisely detailed. Within the 28th day from birth, the piglets receive a breeder tattoo that will also appear on the final product along with the crown brand. The fresh pork (hind legs only) sent to prosciutto producers must weigh between 26 pounds and 40 pounds, with a set amount of fat coverage. After they arrive at the production facility, the legs are trimmed to form the characteristic rounded shape, chilled to a precise temperature, and salted by hand with sea salt, which joins the pork as the only two ingredients that go into the making of Prosciutto di Parma PDO.

Following a resting period of at least 90 days, the hams are washed then hung in windowed storerooms to dry slowly. The next step is called sugnatura: The parts of the ham not covered by rind are rubbed with sugna—a mixture of pork fat, salt, and pepper—to keep the meat from drying too quickly. For final aging, the hams hang in cool, dimly lit cellars. From start to finish, the process cannot last less than 14 months (many producers continue aging for 24, 30, or even 36 months and beyond). The last step before inspection may be the most fascinating: The hams are tested using a needle made from horse bone, selected because the bone is able to absorb the aroma and quickly shed it. By inserting the needle into different parts of the cured meat and smelling it, experts can determine if a whole ham leg has the qualifications to be named Prosciutto di Parma PDO. 

The Consorzio, which started with 23 producers, now numbers 125, and in 2025 they turned out 6.75 million Prosciutto di Parma PDO hams. Beyond its economic significance to the region, the “King of Hams” is a cultural icon and a tourism magnet. The annual Parma Ham Festival is held in September, and there’s even an entire museum dedicated to the region’s signature product—with a tasting room, of course. 

However you experience the exceptional flavor and texture of Prosciutto di Parma—on a visit to the museum or from your local specialty store—look for the crown and PDO seal, and take a moment to remember that you are tasting a distinctly delicious slice of history.

This is a collection of the pie recipes that have become little traditions in my kitchen, the ones that feel the most familiar, delicious, and satisfying to bake and share. I hope you love them as much as I do!

Start Here: Pie Crust

The cornerstone of many of the pies I bake is an all-butter flaky pie crust. It’s a mash-up of everything I’ve learned about pie dough over the years and delivers a beautifully golden, super flaky, all-butter pie crust. Even better, it’s a crust that is easy to make. I use my electric mixer with the paddle attachment to smear butter into flour quickly, and it makes less of a mess than doing it all on a countertop (which I still love to do on occasion). Seriously, this is the one.

  1. All-Butter Flaky Pie Crust

    Most pages about pie crusts are going to point you to their “best ever” pie crust, but I’m taking a slightly different approach. I’m going to share a few of the inspirations that have impacted my pie baking over the years along with the pie dough recipe I’ve tweaked and evolved until it’s my version of perfection. This is it.

Fruit & Classic Pies

  1. Shaker Apple Pie

    The defining detail of this traditional Shaker apple pie is the use of rose water in the filling. Sliced apples, brown sugar, and a splash of floral results in a special combination. Pair it with an all-butter crust and you have a beautiful apple pie on your hands.

  2. Berry Pie

    When you have an abundance of blueberries, blackberries, raspberries, or huckleberries, this is the berry pie to make. It’s classic, with a juicy, sugar-kissed, mixed berry filling. Pair it with a flaky, all-butter crust and you have the ideal berry pie on your hands.

  3. Pumpkin Pie

    A classic pumpkin pie recipe with a couple wildcards to make it extra special. The creamy pumpkin custard is made with with a fragrant five spice blend and baked in a flaky, walnut-lined homemade flaky pie crust.

  4. Shaker Lemon Pie

    The defining detail of this lemon Shaker pie is the use of whole lemons for the filling. Thin slices of lemon including the peel, pith, and flesh are macerated in sugar and then turned into a vibrant, flavor-packed, custardy filling. Pair this with a flaky all-butter pie crust and you’ve got something special on your hands.

Cream, Custard & Chocolate Pies

  1. Homemade Coconut Cream Pie

    There are a couple details that take this coconut cream pie to the next level. Like many versions, it is made with a homemade, flaky, all-butter pie crust filled with a creamy coconut pudding. It is finished with clouds of whipped cream and plenty of toasted coconut flakes. Using buttermilk and a confident amount of salt to offset some of the sweetness is how you take this pie from good to great.

  2. Buttermilk Pie

    Buttermilk pies are simple, unassuming, and a breeze to throw together if you keep pie dough at the ready. The buttermilk filling here is a standout – simple, creamy, and maple-sweetened with a kiss of lemony tang and sea salt.

  3. Heavenly Chocolate Pie

    A decadent chocolate pie featuring a honey-sweetened graham cracker crust and a wildcard cream cheese chocolate filling. From the book Country Wisdom & Know-How – the recipe calls for equal parts tofu and cream cheese in the filling. This combination creates a decadent, mousse-like texture that’s also a breeze to cut into precision slices.

Savory Pies

  1. Vegetable Pot Pie

    This hearty vegetable pot pie is made with a flaky, all-butter pie crust encasing a creamy, herbed potato, carrot, pea and onion filling. A homemade savory pie like this is such a treat! There are a couple tips you should know to help you take it from good to exceptional.

  2. Deep Dish Quiche

    The deep dish quiche of your dreams. It’s made with a flaky all-butter crust. The filling is silky smooth and creamy, while still being perfectly sliceable. Switch up the add-ins based on the seasons – roasted cherry tomatoes in summer, winter squash later in the year.

  3. Vegan Shepherd’s Pie

    Imagine a Shepherd’s Pie with the flavors of an Indian samosa – that’s what’s going on here. Vegan, make-ahead, one-dish meal with a split pea (or lentil) base, mashed potato crust, and spices.

  4. Spinach-Mushroom Quiche

    This is a spinach mushroom quiche recipe that features no dairy, no eggs, and no butter. Sounds like it would lack something in the taste department, right? Wrong. It was delicious.

Hand Pies

  1. Apple Cinnamon Hand Pies

    These apple hand pies are the seasonal treat you’d line up for – nostalgic, portable, and flat out delicious. The filling is a slow-baked, miso-swirled apple butter, and the crust is crisp with sesame-sugar spiked with a bit of chile flake.

  2. Peach Hand Pies

    These peach hand pies are next-level good! Made with a flaky, fool-proof, all-butter pie dough and filled with ripe peaches, goat cheese, plus a bit of green chile and basil, they ride the delicious line between savory and sweet.

  3. Mushroom Hand Pies

    Savory pie people, these mushroom hand pies are for you. Mushrooms, charred scallions, sesame, and miso come together in these portable pockets of deliciousness. You get creaminess from a bit of cheese, and each bite delivers the magic of a golden, flaky pie crust.

  4. Jamaican Veggie Patties

    A favorite recipe from Bryant Terry’s book, Vegan Soul Kitchen. A richly spiced potato, pea, corn, carrot, and cabbage filling is wrapped with a turmeric-hued pastry crust made with coconut oil.

And if you’re looking to improve your pie crust designs and move beyond a simple, basic crust this season, there are a host of next-level pie crust design techniques out there to inspire your creations.

With just a hint of honey to sweeten the deal, this Honey Mustard Dressing is the perfect balance. Mustard hasn’t always been my favorite, but I’ve been pouring this on just about everything from chicken nuggets, salads, and even used it to dip roasted vegetables!

My friends, I’ve made a LOT of salad dressings over the years, but honey mustard is special (I liked the combination so much I even made a honey Dijon vinaigrette). It really does nail the balance between the sweet honey and bold mustard. If you’ve found it too sweet or spicy before, give this a try. And, since I’m making recommendations, you’re going to want to put it on this crunchy spinach salad too. It’s quite literally the salad I made to go with this dressing.

Honey Mustard Dressing

Ingredients and Substitutions

The Mayo – I use mayonnaise for the smooth base of this dressing. It contributes a bit of richness. You can swap for a low-fat mayo if you’re concerned about it, but I wouldn’t recommend substituting for Miracle Whip due to the added sugar and tanginess (unless you want it extra sweet).

The Mustards – Here we use regular yellow mustard and Dijon mustard.

Lemon – You’ll find that a squeeze of lemon juice helps to brighten up the flavors without making it taste “sour”. You can use store-bought lemon juice, but I really think it’s worth squeezing fresh.

Honey – The other half of this classic dressing’s name! The honey in this dressing helps to balance the spicy/tangy mustard.

How to Make Honey Mustard Dressing

Juicing the Lemon: I first slice and juice the lemon.

Combining the Ingredients: Measure all ingredients into a small bowl and whisk until fully combined.

Chilling: You can cover the mixing bowl with saran wrap or transfer the dressing to an airtight jar. Then I just let it chill in the fridge until I’m ready to use it.

Expert Tip

I have a confession to make. Sometimes, I know the expert tip, but still don’t always follow it. And here, the tip is harder than it sounds. Be patient. While I regularly drizzle the freshly whisked dressing over my lunch salad, I do have to admit that it tastes even better after chilling in the fridge for at least an hour. Some of these ingredients have big personalities (looking at YOU, mustard), and they need time to mingle before their personalities really are able to shine.

Whisk Thoroughly – It doesn’t take long to whisk this dressing. But, you do need to make sure that all of the ingredients are fully incorporated. I use a spatula to scrape the sides of the bowl to make sure there are no remaining ribbons of mayo or mustard.

Use an Airtight Container – Believe it or not, it does make a difference. If you leave a dressing uncovered, you’ll end up with a drying layer on top of the dressing that will have to be re-incorporated later. For dressings, my go-to choice are mason jars with these leak-proof Ball jar lids. I’ve tried the cheaper ones, and these are still my favorite.

Adjust to Taste – After this has been chilling in the fridge, it’s worth giving the dressing a taste test before you use it. If the flavors have fallen out of balance, then add what it needs. If you have any specific questions, check the FAQ or ask me in the comments!

Serving Suggestions

While I love drizzling this dressing on the salad that I made for it, I also have been known to use it with my copycat Applebee’s Asian chicken salad. And, you can’t go wrong using it with this Southwestern steak salad or my shrimp avocado salad.

I have to admit that I take this dressing outside the salad lane pretty often. These chicken cordon bleu egg rolls are fabulous dipped in it. And, I like to put it on the counter beside our fresh vegetable box for a snack. Keep a jar in the fridge and see where it ends up.

Make Ahead & Storage

Make Ahead: Oh yeah. I make this dressing in advance and then use it during the week until it runs out. Just make sure to give it a shake after it’s been sitting in the fridge for a day or longer.

How to Store: You can just store it in an airtight container or jar in the refrigerator. Use within 3 days for the best results.

Frequently Asked Questions

Help! My dressing separated in the refrigerator.

No worries, this is a common occurrence with homemade dressings. Just give it a shake or whisk it to recombine. Store-bought varieties don’t separate because the manufacturers add emulsifiers and stabilizers that prevent it from happening.

Will vinegar work instead of lemon juice?

In a pinch, you can use vinegar here. The acid will still balance the other ingredients, and it will smooth the consistency because it’s a liquid. But, I don’t care as much for the flavor and recommend using lemon if possible.

What does this dressing pair well with?

My family really loves pairing this dressing with breaded chicken and the fresh spinach salad. It’ll make a great dipping sauce for vegetables, too!

A Guide to Shrimp Crackers: How to Buy & Fry Indo and Viet Styles

📅 Sep 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Shrimp crackers, aka shrimp chips, are the Southeast Asian equivalent of the American potato chip. Vietnamese speakers know them as bánh phồng tôm and Bahasa Indonesian speakers call them krupuk udang. People from other Southeast Asian cultures enjoy them too. How are shrimp crackers made? Pretty simply. You mix a dough of primarily ground shrimp and tapioca starch, steam it, slice it, and set the slices out in the hot sun to dry. The hard crackers are then deep-fried in oil, puffing and expanding to about twice their original size. Good shrimp crackers capture the essence of shrimpy flavor.

Most cooks buy the dry crackers, rather than make them. The frying is fast and neither messy nor oily. Also, you can fry the crackers hours in advance, making a bowl of the crunchy, rich snack an easy accompaniment to cocktails. When used more like a cracker, they're great for scooping up Vietnamese salads called gỏi. In this post, I cover how to select good Indonesian krupuk udang and Vietnamese bánh phồng tôm, plus how to fry how to fry shrimp crackers. There's a how-to video, as well.

How to buy krupuk udang and bánh phồng tôm

As with all snack foods, not all shrimp crackers and shrimp chips are equal. The inexpensive colorful ones sold in boxes are pretty but not much else. When you shop for them, you'll basically have two choices -- Indonesian and/or Vietnamese shrimp crackers.

Indonesian krupuk udang: Indonesian shrimp chips, called krupuk udang, are consistently good and packed with real shrimp flavor. I like the larger chips for easy frying, as you'll see in the video below. Indonesian krupuk (kurupuk) udang are my go-to; look for them labeled as "shrimp crackers" and "bánh quy tôm" for Vietnamese speakers. Indonesian producers make fish and garlic krupuk, too.

Vietnamee bánh phồng tôm: Dried shrimp crackers imported from Vietnam, labeled as "bánh phồng tôm" are getting steadily better. They're often produced in Sa Dec, a city known for its tasty shrimp. That said, Vietnamese shrimp crackers tend to a milder shrimp flavor than the Indonesian kind. I introduced my mom to Indo krupuk udang and she's never gone back to the Viet shrimp crackers.

Where to buy shrimp crackers? Shrimp crackers are sold at East Asian and Southeast Asian markets. Look in the Indonesian section for krupuk. Vietnamese bánh phồng tôm may be nearby too. Or, you can purchase the crackers here.

How to fry shrimp crackers (krupuk udang and bánh phồng tôm)

Watch our step-by-step video and follow these tips for the perfect crunch! Frying shrimp crackers in oil is tastiest for bringing out the crunch and shrimp flavor. It's thrilling but happens fast. Watch this short video to get all my tricks. If you buy Vietnamese bánh phồng tôm, fry them up the same way, though see the recipe below to guide the size of your batches.

What to eat with your shrimp crackers and chips?

There are so many things! The fried shrimp crackers add a richness to lean salads. Start simple with a tofu "egg" salad, or do something fun like serve the crackers with this grapefruit and shrimp salad or ginger chicken salad.

Instead of fried wonton chips, serve the fried crackers with my ginger salmon poke or tofu poke. It's the natural pairing for my herbacious Vietnamese fish salad (gỏi cá), too. The poke and fish salad are Pass the Fish Sauce newsletter recipes.

Let me know how you enjoy the crackers! And what brands you're buying and frying.

Fried Shrimp Chips (Shrimp Crackers, Bánh Phồng Tôm, Krupuk Udang)

Eat the crackers the day you fry them or keep in an airtight container or paper bag overnight (if humidity is low). Fry a lot because people gobble them up!

Ingredients

  • Dried shrimp chips or shrimp crackers
  • Neutral oil for deep-frying such as canola, peanut, or rice bran

Instructions

  • Line a large plate with paper towels and place next to the stove. Pour oil to a depth of ¾ to 1 inch (2 to 2.5cm) into a wok or a Dutch oven and heat over medium heat to 325 to 350°F on a deep-frying thermometer. (If you don't have a thermometer, stand a dry bamboo chopstick in the oil; if small bubbles immediately gather on the surface around the chopstick, the oil is ready.)
  • For each batch, drop in 1 big one or 3 to 5 small chips, keeping in mind that they will double in size. If you fry too many at a time, they will cook unevenly. Each will sink to the bottom, start to bubble, maybe curl, and then expand before rising to the top, where it will finish cooking. All of this happens in 4 to 5 seconds, so don't turn away. Once the chips float to the top, let them fry for only a few seconds longer. Don't let them brown or their delicate flavor will vanish. Using a skimmer, transfer the chips to the towel-lined plate to drain. This is fast-paced frying, so regulate the heat and adjust the size of your batches so that you are working at a comfortable speed. Eat at room temperature.

Miso walnut chocolate chip cookies

📅 Sep 19, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

There are two things I need to tell you about these cookies.

One is that they’re crazy-good. And the other is that they’re huge. The recipe makes just five cookies but each one is so packed with flavor, that you’ll be glad you can have one all to yourself. Your other four friends or guests will be delighted as well. (But they’re quite generous, so it’s okay to share…)

What makes these cookies extra special is their slightly savory, umami note, due to walnuts glazed with white miso. Miso is fermented soybean paste that can be purchased in Asian markets, but has become so popular, you can often find it in grocery stores. In these cookies, it adds a little je ne sais quoi, since it adds a subtle, yet complex note of flavors. Umami is often described as a flavor that’s craveable, and it certainly makes a compelling case for that description in these cookies.

If you don’t have white miso, or don’t want to make the candied walnuts, no problem. Just use toasted walnuts. Pecans, or another favorite nut will also work. You can also leave the nuts out, or swap in dried fruit for them. Do-ahead tip: The walnuts can be made a few days in advance and kept in an airtight container. The glaze will get a little sticky, but the walnuts are perfectly fine to use in these cookies.

Another “secret” to these cookies is to incorporate large chunks of chocolate into the dough. While it may be tempting to use chocolate chips, they’re really best if you chop up chocolate from a bar of good-quality chocolate. I leave the pieces quite rough and irregular, which – when baked – create large pockets and bites of dark chocolate.

I’ve adapted the recipe from SIFT: The Elements of Great Baking by Nicola Lamb. Her book explains many of the hows and whys of baking, as well as offering an array of creative recipes. Some are completely original and others are riffs on classics, and it’s a book that I refer to often and these cookies are a big hit whenever I bake them. I hope you enjoy these cookies as much as I do!

Miso Walnut Chocolate Chip Cookies

Adapted from SIFT by Nicola Lamb When you form the five mounds of dough, avoid compacting them too tight. You want to form them into loose balls rather than rolling them in your hands into smooth rounds. They're best when you chop the chocolate from bars of good-quality dark chocolate, and I leave the pieces fairly large – about the size of almonds, but you can use store-bought chocolate chunks if you wish. I baked my batch of cookies for 15 to 16 minutes, although the original recipe says 18 minutes. All ovens are different, but they're done when they're light brown across the top. The cookies shown in the post were baked on a cloudy day, so may look a bit dark. You want to err on the side of underbaking rather than overbaking. Another tip is that you can sprinkle extra flaky salt on top of each cookie when they come out of the oven. These cookies are best enjoyed the day they’re made but are delicious the next day as well. Store them in an airtight container at room temperature. Course Dessert Cuisine American Keyword chocolate, chocolate chip cookies, cookies, miso, walnuts Servings 5

Ingredients

Miso walnuts

  • 3 tablespoons mirin rice wine
  • 3 tablespoons granulated sugar
  • 3 tablespoons (50g) white miso
  • 1 cup (100g) walnut halves toasted

Cookie dough

  • 6 tablespoons (90g) unsalted butter at room temperature
  • 6 tablespoons (85g) light brown sugar
  • 6 tablespoons (85g) granulated sugar
  • 1 large egg at room temperature
  • 1 3/4 cups (220g) all-purpose flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 1/4 teaspoons flaky sea salt or 1 teaspoon Diamond Crystal kosher salt, plus more for sprinkling (optional)
  • 9 ounces (255g) bittersweet or semisweet chocolate chopped in large chunks

Instructions

  • To make the miso walnuts, in a medium saucepan or skillet over moderate heat, warm the mirin, the granulated sugar, and the miso, stirring to incorporate and smooth out the ingredients. When the mixture starts to boil, add the walnuts and cook, stirring frequently, until the walnuts are completely coated and the miso mixture has almost all been absorbed by the walnuts, which will take a couple of minutes. They're done when there's only about a tablespoon of the miso syrup in the pan. Remove from heat and let cool, giving them a stir a few times as they cool.
  • To make the cookies, preheat the oven to 375ºF (190ºC). Line a baking sheet with parchment paper.
  • In the bowl of a stand mixer fitted with the paddle attachment on low speed, or by hand, cream the butter and sugars until well-incorporated for about 1 minute. Do not beat the mixture until it's too light or overly fluffy, which can cause the cookies to spread too much during baking.
  • Mix in the egg. Sift the flour and baking soda together in a small bowl, then mix that into the dough along with the salt.
  • Break or cut the walnuts up a little, but not too much, and stir them into the dough, then stir in the chunks of chocolate. You may need to get your hands in there to get everything thoroughly combined, but don't overmix. The dough can be somewhat irregular.
  • Shape the dough into 5 equal mounds on the baking sheet, each about 6 ounces (180g), placing them evenly spaced apart. Bake the cookies for 15-18 minutes, sprinkling them with additional flaky sea salt after they come out of the oven, if you wish. The cookies are done when they are light golden brown across the top. Err on the side of underbaking rather than overbaking.

Notes

Mirin is a sweet rice wine, available in Asian markets and well-stocked supermarkets. There are some substitutes for mirin here.

Nourishing, quick & easy one pan casserole packed with nutrition. Course dinner Cuisine American Diet Gluten Free, Vegan Prep Time 10 minutes minutes Cook Time 1 hour hour 5 minutes minutes Total Time 1 hour hour 15 minutes minutes Servings 8 Calories 356kcal Author Danielle Brown Cost $16

Equipment

  • 1 9×13 baking dish
  • 1 blender
  • 1 strainer for rinsing quinoa & lentils
  • 1 knife
  • 1 cutting board

Ingredients

  • 1 yellow onion finely diced
  • 1 cup red lentils rinsed
  • 1 cup quinoa rinsed
  • 4 cups vegetable broth
  • 1 bag chopped frozen spinach 12 oz
  • 2 tablespoon nutritional yeast
  • ½ cup fresh parsley chopped
  • 2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon pepper
  • 1 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 2 teaspoon paprika

Cashew Cream

  • 1.5 cups cashews raw
  • 1.5 cups water
  • salt to taste

Instructions

Cashew Cream:

  • In a high speed blender like a Vitamix, combine cashews, water, and salt.
  • Blend on high until smooth and creamy.

Casserole:

  • Preheat the oven to 400 F.
  • Into a large 9×13 casserole dish, add the onions, lentils, quinoa, parsley, vegetable broth, spinach, nutritional yeast, half of the cashew cream, and spices.

  • I usually double the cashew cream recipe for a big batch so I have extra to use in other dishes throughout the week. It’s amazing stirred into pastas, soups, other casseroles/one pot dishes, or even used as a sour cream alternative.
  • Soak the cashews in boiling water for at least 10 minutes to soften them before blending for an even creamier cashew cream.
  • Rinse your lentils and quinoa before adding them to the casserole dish. It helps to remove any excess starch.

Chicken with Cajun Sauce

5 Stars 4 Stars 3 Stars 2 Stars 1 Star

5 from 11 reviews

  • Author: Lindsay
  • Total Time: 40 minutes
  • Yield: 4 servings 1x
Print Pin

Description

Pan-fried chicken in a luscious Cajun cream sauce, served with mashed potatoes and green beans – loving this easy, cozy weeknight dinner!

Ingredients

Units USM

Chicken:

  • 1 tablespoon avocado oil
  • 1 lb. chicken breast
  • 1 1/2 teaspoon Cajun seasoning (I used McCormick)

Sauce:

  • 1 tablespoon butter
  • 1 shallot, minced or thinly sliced
  • 1 tablespoon tomato paste
  • 1 teaspoon Cajun seasoning (I used McCormick)
  • 1 tablespoon flour
  • 3/4 cup heavy cream
  • 1 cup chicken broth (I use water + 1 tsp Better Than Bouillon)

For serving: 

  • Mashed Potatoes
  • Green beans or salad
Cook Mode Prevent your screen from going dark

Instructions

  1. Prep the Chicken: Cut the chicken into thinner pieces horizontally, or pound them out a bit so they’re thinner.
  2. Cook the Chicken: Season the chicken filets with Cajun seasoning. Heat the oil in a skillet over medium-high heat (I use a nonstick for this). Sauté the chicken on each side for 3-4 minutes, until the internal temperature reaches 165 and the outside is golden; remove from the pan and set aside on a plate.
  3. Sauté Shallots: Wipe off any overly browned bits from the pan. Turn the heat to medium-low so the butter and shallots don’t burn. Add butter and shallots; saute until softened and fragrant.
  4. Make The Roux: Add tomato paste and Cajun seasoning; saute for 3-4 minutes until the tomato paste has developed a rich color. Add flour and mix; cook for 2-3 minutes to remove any floury taste.
  5. Finish Sauce: Add broth gradually; whisk to incorporate after each addition. Once it’s smooth, add heavy cream and whisk again to incorporate. Let it simmer for 5-10 minutes to thicken slightly. Add chicken back to the pan so it is partially submerged with sauce.
  6. Serve: Serve pieces of chicken with a spoonful of the sauce over the top. I love this SO MUCH with mashed potatoes and broccoli – see notes!

Notes

To air fry the chicken: Air fry at 375 for 10-12 minutes, or until at least 165 degrees internal temperature.

Roasted Potatoes and Broccoli (Oven): Toss 3-4 cups halved baby potatoes with avocado oil and salt on a sheet pan. Roast the potatoes at 425 for 15 minutes. Scoot the potatoes over, add 3-4 cups broccoli and ¼ cup water to the same sheet pan, and roast for another 15 minutes.

Roasted Potatoes and Broccoli (Air Fryer): Toss 2 cups halved baby potatoes with avocado oil and salt. Air fry at 375 for 12 minutes. Add 2 cups frozen or fresh broccoli; air fry at 400 for another 10-12 minutes.

A Colombian Deep Dive

Courtesy True Colombia Travel

In the Antioquia region, a custom itinerary with True Colombia Travel balances exclusive access to three of the country’s best producers with myriad adventures (like coffee in a village where locals still get around on horseback). Enthusiasts will marvel at coffee trees on volcanic slopes and learn about fermentation techniques. A visit to Café Cocondo Orgánico, where coffee is cross-­pollinated with mango, avocado, orange, and soursop trees, is especially thrilling.

An Invigorating Tanzanian Tour

Courtesy Arusha Coffee Lodge by Elewana

Known for high-quality ­arabica and robusta beans, this East African nation is paradise for those who crave a brightly flavored brew. Arusha Coffee Lodge by Elewana is conveniently located between Mount Kilimanjaro and the Ngorongoro Conservation Area. It’s also surrounded by 100 acres of coffee trees. Hotel guests can tour the roasting facilities and sample as much of the final product as they’d like. Don’t miss the day’s rotating selection of java-flavored treats, which might include nutty cakes, tiramisu, or scones.

Guatemalan Harvests and Hikes

Courtesy Nossa Familia Coffee

Every year, Augusto Carneiro—founder of Nossa Familia Coffee in Portland, Oregon—leads tours of Guatemala City’s third-wave scene. Travelers start by sharing breakfast with the owners of the trendy café Coffee District, then help with the harvest at a farm partway up Volcán de Agua. Later, they head to Finca San Jerónimo Miramar in the Atitlán region to learn about grafting arabica plants onto robusta roots. There are also many sorts of excursions, from a hike on Acatenango mountain to swimming in Lake Atitlán.

Moelleux of Summer Fruits

📅 Sep 12, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

This may or may not happen to you, but sometimes when I’m a guest in someone’s home, where everyone pitches in to make dinner, all eyes (or just the host’s eyes) fall on me to make dessert. Gulp. I happy to pitch in with the cooking or bringing along a few baked goodies for the weekend, but I’m not always prepared to bake on the spot.

If you’re lucky, there may be a scale, a cake pan, a sharp knife, and hopefully sugar, butter, eggs, and flour. If you’re really lucky, there might be a whisk and a spatula, too. But if you’ve ever tried to peel a dozen apples with a steak knife because it’s the only one that’s even close to sharp, or eyeball 1 cup/140g) of flour, you know what I’m talking about. I once did a post on Things I Bring When I’m a Guest for the Weekend, which raised the ire of some, but I require a minimum of a knife, a spatula, a bowl, and a cake pan if I’m going to bake something, like a cake.

You’re likely familiar with the Moelleux au chocolat, the soft warm individual melting cakes that everybody loves. (If you have the kind of friends that have six to eight ramekins, well, that’s great.) I recently saw French food writer and journalist François-Régis Gaudy (his book, Let’s Eat France, translated into English, is a lot of fun and pretty amazing) make a Moelleux à la abricot on his Instagram page, which didn’t require a mixer or any special equipment. Not even a whisk!

His Apricot Cake was a very casual affair, baked in a rectangular mold on a sheet of parchment paper, and came out of the oven rather free-form, looking exactly like something that would come out of a French home cook’s kitchen, when baking dessert for friends. I’m fine with that but didn’t know if you were, so I thought I’d tinker with it and make it in a cake pan, which is easier to slice. I also fiddled around with the ingredients, as well as putting the brown granulated sugar on top (rather than underneath), to give the moelleux a nice sheen.

I used a springform cake pan but you can use any similar-sized vessel; an 8-, 9- or 10-square or round cake pan would work, or even a baking dish. The baking time will vary but if your experience with using a variety of ovens in other people’s homes is anything like mine, you’ll find that each one is different, and what one says is 350ºF/180ºC will be different than another whose dial says 350ºF/180ºC. I don’t travel with an oven thermometer but may add that to things I bring when I’m staying with people.

Tip: If you’re staying in someone’s place for the weekend, you can mix the dry ingredients in a container or zip-top bag then put the dessert together when you get there.

I made one cake with apricots first, which was sensational, but knowing fresh apricots are harder to come by elsewhere, I made another one with plums, which came out great as well. It would work with other summer fruits and berries but I really like the tanginess of apricots or plums against the moist cake layer, so stuck with those two. A handful of berries wouldn’t hurt either, but I do recommend keeping it simple.

Moelleux aux fruits d'ete

Inspired by a recipe from French food writer and journalist François-Régis Gaudry The ground almonds are really delicious in the dessert, but if you can't get ground almonds (sometimes called almond meal or almond flour), you can use 1 1/4 cups (175g) of all-purpose flour in place of the mix. The picture in the post of the batter shows 3 eggs being added, and I took that photo when I first tested the recipe, which I did a few more times after that. The final recipe that I came up with, below, uses 2 eggs. Course Dessert Servings 8 servings

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds (680g) apricots or plums (or another summer fruit, see headnote)
  • 3/4 cup, plus 2 tablespoons (100g) almond flour
  • 1/2 cup (65g) all-purpose flour
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons baking powder preferably aluminum-free
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup plus 2 tablespoons (230g) granulated sugar
  • 5 ounces (140g) unsalted butter at room temperature
  • 2 large eggs at room temperature
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract (or 1/2 teaspoon almond extract)
  • 1 1/2 tablespoons coarse granulated brown sugar such as tubinado sugar (or granulated white sugar)

Instructions

  • Halve the fruit and remove the pits. Slice the fruit into 3/4- to 1-inch (2-3cm) wedges. Set aside. Preheat the oven to 350ºF (180ºC.) Grease a 9-inch (23cm) springform pan very well with butter or non-stick spray. (If you don't have a springform pan, you can use another cake pan but it may be harder to get the cake out so you may want to also line the bottom with parchment paper.)
  • In a medium mixing bowl, stir together the almond flour, all-purpose flour, baking powder, salt, and sugar. (My preferred tool to use for this step is a silicone spatula.) Add the soft butter and mix it in with the spatula until it's broken up into little pieces, roughly the size of kernels of corn. Stir in the eggs and vanilla extract, until it's almost smooth. It's fine if there are small pieces of butter visible.
  • Scrape the batter into the prepared pan and smooth the top. Arrange the wedges of fruit on their sides in concentric circles over the batter, snugly placing the wedges against each other, pressing them gently into the batter as you go. Avoid putting them right up against the sides of the pan. It's best to leave some room for the batter to rise between the fruit and the pan, to avoid juices from the fruit adhering to the sides of the pan as the moelleux bakes. (It's unavoidable - and natural - if using juicy fruits, like plums, to have juice run out. But placing the slices not right up against the sides of the pan will help you get the cake out of the pan later.)
  • Sprinkle the coarse sugar over the top and bake until the center just feels set; a toothpick inserted into the center should come out free of cake crumbs stuck to it, about 1 hour. Remove from the oven and set on a cooling rack. If any fruit juices have bubbled up and stuck to the sides of the pan, run a knife around the outside of the cake, which will help it release later.

Notes

Notes: If you want to make this gluten-free, I'd go with the almond flour rendition, and use a gluten-free flour substitute in place of the wheat flour. If you want to reduce the sugar in the recipe, you could cut it by 6 tablespoons/75g and skip the sugar on top, although I wouldn't.
