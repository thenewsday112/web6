Counter-culture sandwich destination pairs 1970s crash-pad style with a full-service bar and late-night hours

Norman, OK  (RestaurantNews.com)  A fresh stop for hot subs, cold drinks and laid-back vibes is getting ready to roll into Norman. Cheba Hut “Toasted” Subs, the counterculture sandwich shop known for its chill atmosphere, cottonmouth cures, and bold flavors that hit just right, opens its doors at 1800 24th Ave. NW on Thursday, Nov. 5.

To mark the occasion, Cheba Hut is throwing a grand opening bash with swag bags for the first 100 guests and DJ Terpenoid on the decks starting at 5 p.m.

Just off Interstate 35, the restaurant sits about 10 minutes from the University of Oklahoma campus and even closer to much of the city’s student housing. Downtown Norman is also a quick trip away, putting it within easy reach of longtime locals, weekend crowds and road-trippers needing a pit stop.

The Norman opening brings the brand’s Oklahoma shop count to three, all from franchise partners Rose Taylor and Steven VanCauwenbergh, who launched the Uptown location in Oklahoma City and followed with Edmond in August.

“Norman is personal for me,” Taylor said. “I graduated from OU, and this town has the personality, energy and sense of community this brand runs on. I’ve joked from the beginning that Cheba Hut needs Norman, and Norman needs Cheba Hut. So this was never going to be a copy-and-paste location. We wanted a place that feels like it’s been here all along, and now we finally get to make that happen.”

Like every stop in the Chebaverse, Norman tells a local story through its walls. Joshua Finley‘s custom artwork brings a groovy 1970s crash-pad aesthetic to life, featuring a retro-inspired roller girl, psychedelic details, and playful nods to Norman’s personality. Paired with eclectic vintage décor and unexpected design elements throughout, the space captures the nostalgia of another era while staying true to Cheba Hut’s counterculture roots. It’s a one-of-a-kind atmosphere designed to give guests something new to discover with every visit.

Beyond the walls, the shop has a patio and a full-service bar, built for midday sub runs, a round after work, and the stretch of the night when the munchies show up uninvited.

For customers stepping inside for the first time, Cheba Hut serves more than 30 signature toasted subs inspired by classic cannabis strains and available in Nug (4-inch), Pinner (8-inch) and Blunt (12-inch) sizes. Fan favorites include the White Widow, loaded with chicken, bacon and ranch; the Kali Mist, a spicy club stacked with turkey, bacon and pepper jack; the Dank, a pizza-inspired masterpiece layered with pepperoni, salami, marinara and melted mozzarella; and the Griefo, a veggie-forward favorite piled high with cream cheese, guacamole and fresh greens.

The menu also features munchie-approved sides like pretzel nugs and garlic cheese bread, sweet treats including Krispy Bars and cookies, plus the legendary Kool-Aid bar that’s been a go-to remedy for cottonmouth since day one.

Cheba Hut Norman will be open daily from 10 a.m. to midnight.

With its mix of toasted subs, playful stoner humor, neighborhood-first mindset and a disrespectful mascot named “Flip the Bird,” Cheba Hut is ready to make Norman a little more dank, one toasted sub or Krispy Bar at a time.

👩🏽‍⚕️ Dietitian's Note

Red cabbage is a cruciferous vegetable, like broccoli and kale. It contains glucosinolates, plant compounds that break down into substances studied for their potential health benefits. Its purple color comes from anthocyanins, which have antioxidant properties. It also provides fiber to support regular bowel movements and nourish beneficial gut bacteria.

For an iron-rich meal, pair this pickled cabbage with lentils, beans, or tofu. Cabbage contains vitamin C, which helps your body absorb plant-based iron, although heating and storage reduce some of it. Add a squeeze of fresh lime before serving for extra vitamin C.

This is a quick vinegar pickle rather than a fermented food, so it is not a reliable source of probiotics.

This pickled red cabbage recipe makes it easy to add more vegetables to your meals, and boost iron absorption. Plus, it's:

  • Quick to prepare: Just a few minutes of shredding and a simple homemade brine.
  • No added sugar or oil: Bright, tangy flavor from vinegar, lime, garlic, and spices.
  • Crunchy and colorful: An easy way to make simple meals more satisfying.
  • Made with low-sodium salt: Reduces the sodium added to the brine compared with regular salt.

👩🏼‍🌾 Ingredients

Below, I share ingredient notes from both a nutrition expert's and trained chef's perspective.

  • Red cabbage: Cabbage heads vary in size, so measure the shreds to keep the cabbage-to-brine ratio consistent.
  • Apple cider vinegar: Gives this pickled cabbage recipe a fruity tang. Choose vinegar labeled 5% acidity.
  • Lime juice: Adds a fresh citrus flavor that complements the vinegar.
  • Low-sodium salt (optional): Seasons the brine with less sodium than regular salt. The amount of sodium varies by brand.
  • Garlic: Lightly crush the clove to release its flavor into the brine.
  • Jalapeño: Adds fresh heat. Remove the seeds and inner membranes for a milder result, or leave it out.
  • Coriander: I use coriander seeds, but ground coriander also works. The seeds add a subtle citrusy flavor, while ground coriander disperses throughout the brine and may make it slightly cloudy.
  • Black peppercorns and red pepper flakes: Optional additions for warmth and extra heat.

🌱 Substitutions

This recipe is versatile and easy to adapt. Below are some simple ingredient substitutions.

  • Apple cider vinegar: Substitute white wine vinegar or distilled white vinegar labeled 5% acidity. Distilled vinegar gives a sharper flavor.
  • Lime juice: Use the same amount of lemon juice.
  • Red cabbage: Green cabbage works, although it won't have the same vibrant color or anthocyanin content.
  • Coriander: Use the same amount of coriander seeds or ground coriander. Both work well.
  • Less heat: Omit the jalapeño and red pepper flakes.
  • Salt: Regular salt works in the same amount, or omit entirely, if preferred.

📝 How to Make Pickled Red Cabbage

The complete recipe with exact amounts is below. These additional tips will help you get the best results.

How to Shred Cabbage:

  1. Cut the cabbage: Remove any damaged outer leaves and rinse the cabbage. Cut it into halves or quarters, depending on its size and the tool you are using.
  1. Shred: Cabbage peeler: This is my preferred method for thin, delicate ribbons. Place the cabbage securely on a cutting board and shave across a cut face, following the tool's instructions. Keep your fingers away from the blade and discard the tough core when finished. Shred it thinly. Thin ribbons absorb the brine quickly and are easier to eat in sandwiches, wraps, and tacos. Sharp knife: Remove the core, place each wedge flat-side down, and slice crosswise as thinly as possible. Mandoline: Use a thin slicing setting and a hand guard. Food processor: Use the slicing disc, cutting the cabbage into pieces that fit the feed tube.

Make the brine:

  1. Heat just until simmering: Stir to dissolve the salt, then remove from the heat. Prolonged boiling evaporates liquid and concentrates the brine.
  1. Pour over while hot: This helps soften the cabbage so it absorbs the tangy flavor.

Pickle the cabbage:

  1. Toss as it cools. The cabbage may initially sit above the liquid. Tossing helps coat all the shreds as they soften.
  1. Pack it down gently. Transfer all the brine to the jars and press the cabbage into the liquid. Give it time. This quick pickled red cabbage is delicious once chilled, but an overnight rest allows the flavors to develop.

For serving:

  1. Keep the cabbage covered (optional): If needed throughout the week, top up with freshly made, cooled brine using the same vinegar-to-water ratio-2 parts vinegar to 1 part water. Keep refrigerated and use within the original storage time.
  1. Serving tip: Use this pickled red cabbage in place of the raw cabbage in my Cabbage Kale Salad and skip the dressing-the tangy brine adds plenty of flavor.

✔️ Storage & Meal Prep Tips

Store in clean, covered jars in the refrigerator and use within 2 weeks. Keep the cabbage pressed into the brine and use a clean utensil when serving.

Top up with freshly made, cooled brine using the same vinegar-to-water ratio-2 parts vinegar to 1 part water as needed.

🙋🏽‍♀️ Recipe FAQs

How do you pickle red cabbage without sugar?

Pour a hot brine of vinegar, water, lime juice, salt, and seasonings over thinly shredded cabbage. Toss, cool briefly, and refrigerate. Sugar is not necessary for this quick refrigerator pickle.

What are the benefits of pickled red cabbage?

Red cabbage provides fiber and anthocyanins, the antioxidant pigments responsible for its purple color. This recipe is also made without added sugar or oil and offers an easy way to add vegetables to meals.

Is pickled red cabbage fermented?

This recipe is not fermented. Its tangy flavor comes from vinegar and lime juice, whereas fermented cabbage develops acidity through the activity of microorganisms.

Can I keep pickled red cabbage at room temperature?

No. This recipe must be refrigerated and is not intended for canning or room-temperature storage.

Pickled Red Cabbage Recipe (No Added Sugar)

This quick pickled red cabbage recipe has fiber and antioxidants, with no added sugar. A crunchy topping for sandwiches, salads, and bowls. Course Condiment, Side Dish Cuisine Vegan Diet Vegan, Vegetarian Prep Time 10 minutes minutes Cook Time 5 minutes minutes Total Time 15 minutes minutes Servings 6 servings Calories 41kcal Author Nisha Melvani

Ingredients

  • 1 small head red cabbage about 4 to 5 cups thinly shredded
  • 1 cup apple cider vinegar labeled 5% acidity
  • ½ cup water
  • 2 tablespoons fresh lime juice
  • ½ teaspoon salt preferably low-sodium (see notes)
  • 1 clove garlic lightly crushed
  • ½ jalapeño thinly sliced, optional
  • ½ teaspoon black peppercorns optional
  • ½ teaspoon coriander seeds or ground coriander, optional
  • ¼ teaspoon red pepper flakes optional

Instructions

  • Shred the cabbage: Remove damaged outer leaves and rinse the cabbage. Cut into halves or quarters. Use a cabbage peeler to shave thin ribbons from a cut face, keeping the cabbage stable and your fingers clear of the blade. Alternatively, use a sharp knife, mandoline with a hand guard, or food processor fitted with a slicing disc. Discard the tough core.
  • Measure: Transfer 4 to 5 cups shredded cabbage to a large heatproof glass or stainless-steel bowl.
  • Make the brine: Combine the vinegar, water, lime juice, low-sodium salt, garlic, and jalapeño in a small saucepan. Add the peppercorns, coriander seeds or ground coriander, and red pepper flakes, if using. Bring to a simmer, stirring to dissolve the salt, then remove from the heat.
  • Combine: Pour the hot brine over the cabbage. Toss well with tongs and gently press the cabbage into the liquid.
  • Cool and pack: Let cool briefly, tossing occasionally as the cabbage softens. Pack into clean jars, pour in all the brine, and gently press the cabbage down.
  • Refrigerate: Cover and refrigerate within 2 hours of preparation. Enjoy once chilled, or refrigerate overnight for the best flavor. Keep refrigerated and use within 2 weeks.

Notes

  • Salt: Optional. I used SaltWise Salicornia White Salt, a lower-sodium seasoning made from salicornia-an edible coastal plant with a naturally salty taste.
  • Coriander: I use whole coriander seeds, but the same amount of ground coriander works, too.
  • Milder flavor: Omit the jalapeño and red pepper flakes.
  • Refrigerator pickle: This recipe is not suitable for canning or room-temperature storage.

Chipotle’s Smoked Brisket starts with Responsibly Raised® beef. Food-Themed Fashion Is Starting to Feel Gluttonous

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Loewe | Loewe

There was a time, not too long ago, when a purse shaped like a baguette felt like a thrilling — even subversive — novelty. Now, we hardly bat an eye when we see that Moschino has made a trompe l’oeil celery purse, or that Big Bud Press is making a hot-dog-and-pretzel-print jumpsuit in collaboration with a Los Angeles bar. How did we get here? And has it all become a little too much? 

Fashion has long found inspiration from the culinary world, but the influx of food-fueled prints, graphics, and trompe l’oeil accessories that picked up steam during the pandemic have now reached a cultural tipping point. 

Today, there are truly countless examples of these food motifs and edible, wearable trompe l’oeil creations, at all price points. Loewe’s summer collection featured a $4,150 lambskin tomato clutch, while Walmart’s included a line of rotisserie chicken bag-like purses and charms starting at $5.97. As with many trends, this trajectory started in high fashion and worked its way down to mass-market ubiquity. In 2020, a chocolate chip cookie went down one of New York Fashion Week’s most buzzed-about fall runways. It protruded from a black purse by the designer Puppets and Puppets, a design that quickly went viral for its whimsical inclusion of a classic dessert on a high-end handbag. 

The then-emerging brand made food a central theme of its vision: Its cake-topped aviator caps, Ferrero Rocher heels, and beeswax-sculpted cheese hats — hyperrealistic, absurdist food designs capable of achieving instant social media virality. Prior to working in fashion, Puppets designer Carly Mark was working as an artist whose pieces revolved around candies and snacks. As she told Business of Fashion in 2023, the Puppets universe felt like the ideal pairing given what she considers food and fashion’s motivators: pleasure and money.

During the pandemic, the much-duped “strawberry dress” took over TikTok, and Tombolo Company found a hit on its hands with its vongole shirt, which debuted in 2019, but found peak popularity in 2021. The shirt appeared in Vogue in 2020, and InsideHook called it an “obvious bedfellow to day-drinks, rooftops and weekenders” — in other words, a status symbol. Katie Kimmel’s T-shirts made chicken-parm enthusiasm akin to being a devout sports fan, and in 2022, this very publication called “seafood kitsch” the look of the summer, as items like Rachel Antonoff’s super-popular seafood-tower dress flew off the digital shelves. 

Why was food so primed to become a motif in clothing at this point in culture? One could make the argument that it has roots in the millennial optimism years: Earlier in the 2000s and 2010s, the concept of “foodies” was new. Brands printed tongue-in-cheek food- and coffee-related motifs and slogans (a fervent love of avocados comes to mind) on hipster apparel, as the idea that “you are what you eat” developed growing fervor. That period was a key stepping stone in the evolution toward the far more elaborate and pervasive marketability of food-as-identity we see today. Content creator Cole Poska, who is a partner and creative director at Beyond Studios, tells Eater that “food brands have something most brands would kill for: decades of nostalgia, iconic identities, and consumers who are borderline obsessed with them.” Poska’s own Instagram often reimagines restaurant merch just for fun; his designs for Cheesecake Factory apparel went viral, such as a hat that resembles the iconic brown bread. He points out that people will defend their favorite fast-food order like it’s a family member, and with the kind of loyalty that’s hard to manufacture. Even for Desperate Restaurants, Fake Reviews Just Aren&#8217;t Worth It

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Yelp was first launched in 2004, it flopped. The company’s initial model of an email-based referral service failed to take off until its creators realized users were taking the platform into their own hands; in lieu of sending one another recommendations via email chain, they began using a “hidden” link at the bottom of the startup’s website to write unsolicited reviews of everything from doctors to restaurants. Yelp 2.0 launched in 2005, and gave those reviews a more visible place to live online — one that emboldened users to feel more authentic stewardship, by way of direct authorship, over hyping up the businesses they enjoyed (or didn’t). The startup bought a whopping 20-million-business database, and amplified a world of reviews outside of the restaurant- critic echo chamber. It was a fresh democratization of information, which was itself one of the more optimistic legacies of the Y2K internet. 

It’s been over 20 years since Yelp was founded. At its best, the platform still feels like a place where the voices of hungry, curious diners can share their personal experiences and offer recommendations and tips; at its worst, it feels like a bloated repository for reviews filled with misdirected anger, unreasonable expectations, or else completely fake customer reviews. Initially, unsubstantiated and unreliable reviews were more often written by a person — either someone from the business itself, or paid by the business; now, a fake review can be penned by a paid influencer, a detailed generative AI prompt, or purchased from third-party agencies that will produce fake bot reviews in bulk.

Fake reviews were perhaps inevitable. Bot-driven comments became ubiquitous during an overripe moment in the untamed internet’s lifespan. But how does a restaurant shamed for gamifying its reviews — even if it was, presumably, out of desperation in a tough economic landscape — regain customers’ broken trust? 

… The offending restaurant’s foot traffic went down by 8 percent, and dipped even lower around the holidays, a time when would-be diners rely even more on Yelp.

In 2026’s AI-addled digital ecosystem, researchers are starting to understand even more about how paid-for, planted reviews impact the restaurants behind them, and whether trying to gamify platforms to achieve high ratings is worth it at all for businesses. A recently published working paper on the consequences of restaurants flagged by Yelp for fake (planted, incentivized) reviews found that since 2023, more than 4,900 businesses have been flagged with Yelp’s “Consumer Alert” banner, which exists on the penalized business’ page for a 90-day period and lets Yelp users know that the business has been caught peddling fake reviews. Cooking polenta in modern times doesn’t have much in common with the way it was prepared for centuries in the regions that now make up Northern Italy. Polenta was traditionally cooked on a grand scale in copper cauldrons over open fire. There was a lot of stirring, ladling, and creative, resourceful uses of leftovers. Historically it was considered one of the humblest foods and when I came across this line about polenta in Honey From a Weed by Patience Gray (1986), it stuck with me. “The idea of polenta, planted firmly in the north Italian imagination, is the antithesis of modern aspirations. It therefore intrudes on modern life like a vague emotional regret.”

But, polenta is wonderful. And similar to other foods that were cast off in exchange for industrialized, refined counterparts – brown rice, naturally leavened breads, etc. — many people find their way back to the “old ways” eventually.

What is Polenta?

Think of polenta as a cornmeal porridge of sorts. In The Classic Italian Cook Book (1973), Marcella Hazan asserts the importance of polenta by saying, “…to call polenta a cornmeal mush is a most indelicate use of language. In country kitchens, polenta was more than food, it was a rite. It was made daily in an unlined copper kettle, the paiolo, which was always kept hanging at the ready on a hook in the center of the fireplace.” She goes on to describe a wonderful scene where, “…there was a moment of joy as it [polenta] was poured out in a steaming golden circle on the beechwood top of the madia…”

About This Polenta Recipe

Polenta is a regular preparation in my kitchen, and it can be cooked a number of ways. Not just in a cauldron lol.  I jump back and forth between stovetop polenta and oven-baked polenta depending on a number of factors. Today I’m going to talk through my technique for making beautifully creamy polenta on the stovetop. We’ll get to the oven-baked version in a future post. That one is great if you just want to combine ingredients in a baking dish, push it into the oven, and forget about it until the timer goes off. But it’s not as creamy. In either case, the ingredient list is short, and most of your attention should be focused on sourcing some great cornmeal / polenta. Beyond that, a bit of patience, and technique is all you need. I love it served topped generously with this mushroom ragù as you’ll see down below. 

Which Polenta (a.k.a. cornmeal or grits) Should I Use?

Look for stone-ground, whole grain, whole kernel polenta without any additives – preferably organic. Store-bought, quick-cooking, refined polenta is the starchy endosperm of the corn kernel, and much of the flavor from the bran and germ is stripped out in the milling process. These are a few favorites to look for.

  • Hayden Flour Mills Yellow Corn Polenta:  My local market carries Hayden and this is the polenta I purchase most often. It is coarse grind, so it takes a bit longer to cook, but the flavor and texture is wonderful. (Pictured above right)
  • Bob’s Red Mill Organic Medium Grind Cornmeal: Stone-ground, whole grain. Readily available and a solid choice. If I’m looking for a polenta that will cook a bit faster, this is the one to grab because the corn is medium coarse and a bit finer. (Pictured above left)
  • Rancho Gordo Fine Yellow Polenta:  If you like a finer grind, this is your choice. Organic, heirloom corn grown & ground by Anson Mills for RG. IYKYK.

Whole polenta is quite perishable. Use it quickly or store it in the freezer.

Do I Have to Stir Polenta with a Wooden Spoon?

If you read through old polenta recipes, many will mandate stirring with a wooden spoon. It’s a good suggestion, but your polenta will be just fine if you use a stainless steel spoon instead. That said, wood spoons are kinder to your pots, less likely to scratch (especially if your pots have any sort of coating). And, some metal spoons (especially very old ones) can react to acidic ingredients resulting in a metallic taste. If you’re making tomato sauce or sourdough, for example.

Polenta Variations

Knowing how to make a great pot of polenta sets you up with a foundation to keep it simple and straight-forward (like the creamy polenta recipe below), or to add your own twists and flavors. Here are a few ways you can switch things up.

  • Use broth: Instead of water as the base liquid in your polenta experiment with broths, teas, or other infusions. I often make mine with a favorite mushroom tea (topping the polenta with these baked mushrooms.
  • Make it herby: Polenta has a real affinity for certain herbs. My favorite is chives. Double down with a couple handfuls of snipped chives folded in along with some chive compound butter, near the end of cooking so the chives retain their brightness.

What to Do with Leftover Polenta

There are many things to do with leftover polenta. Patience Gray includes this wonderful paragraph (Honey From A Weed, 1986) that exemplifies the cycle of a pot of polenta, “…made one day to serve hot, what is left is sliced and fried in hot oil till golden (polenta fritta), or brushed with oil and grilled (polenta ai ferri), to eat the next day. If chopped salame, parmesan cheese, butter and egg yolks are added to the polenta once it is stiff, and, when it has cooled, the beaten egg whites are folded in, and the preparation put into an oiled oven dish, what emerges after 45 minutes in a hot oven is a torta di polenta.”

Here are a couple other ideas:

  • Baked Polenta:  Slice into strips, brush with olive oil and pan-fry or bake at 350°F into golden, tasty slabs. Serve topped with mushroom ragù.
  • Polenta in Sourdough: This is a favorite polenta-centric baking tip. Crumble cooked (and cooled) polenta into sourdough prior to baking. I tend to do this after my second set of folds. Alternately, you can fold into quick-bread batters.

I saw You Can See Everything — the new documentary about disgraced Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes, co-directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim — on Thursday, and I still feel like my brain is trying to punch its way out of a bag, or like I’ve just awakened from an Inception-esque dream within a dream. It’s difficult to describe the winding, twisting, nearly three-hour film to someone unfamiliar with its many layers of context, from Fielder’s Dadaist, boundaryless approach to his subjects to Holmes’s hypnotic affect, which is either highly effective gaslighting or blinding, self-deluded earnestness. (Which is it? I still don’t know.)

What happens when you take two unreliable narrators — make that three, if you include Holmes’s partner, Billy Evans, whose on-camera persona morphs from protective, naive tech bro to unscrupulous grifter — put them in a jar, and shake them? The result is that the more questions are asked, the further the truth drifts out of reach. It’s like staring at a Magic Eye image, slightly crossing your eyes, desperate to make out the hidden 3D subject but never quite managing to see it. Eventually, you start worrying your eyes might get stuck that way.

The discourse surrounding the film is already abundant, but we’re here to talk about the eggs.

If you’re familiar with the hallmarks of Fielderian programming, you know that Fielder is prone to hyperfixating on seemingly mundane details that later return to completely upend viewers’ expectations. In You Can See Everything, one such detail is observed in an exchange between Holmes and Evans as they make eggs. How much can be gleaned from watching someone make breakfast? A lot, apparently.

In the first half of the film, Fielder is fully embedded in the rental home of Holmes and Evans, who have invited the filmmakers to document the final weeks before Holmes reports to federal prison. (How and why they agreed to this arrangement is incomprehensible if they knew anything about Fielder’s work.) During this time, the narrative takes a surprisingly sympathetic turn for Holmes, who seems intent on convincing the filmmakers — and Fielder specifically — that she is actually a sweet, misunderstood victim of circumstance, not a Machiavellian con woman who defrauded investors. Frankly, aspects of this claim feel convincing at times. Viewers are invited to witness her ostensibly less guarded moments, including a scene in which she makes breakfast with Evans. As Fielder interviews her, Holmes somewhat unenthusiastically and limply beats a bowl of eggs with a fork. The camera lingers on her feeble whisking technique. Then, after a few seconds, another set of hands enters the frame: those of Evans. Without a word, he takes the bowl and fork from Holmes and begins whipping the eggs with considerably greater fervor.

Now, backseat driving in the kitchen is hardly uncommon among couples, though it feels like a fast track to bickering. (Evans tells Fielder that he and Holmes “never fight”; Holmes is also emphatic that she’s never lied in her life. Again, unreliable narrators.) What’s less common — or at least generally considered pretty rude and aggressive — is grabbing something out of your partner’s hands mid-task to take over. Fielder points this out, asking Evans why he felt the need to grab the eggs; Holmes explains that Evans doesn’t like the way she mixes them and thinks he can do it better. 

It’s a tiny interaction, but a remarkably revealing one. Evans feels comfortable taking control of something Holmes is doing, without so much as asking, and seems largely unaware of how his behavior might come across on camera. Holmes, meanwhile, appears accustomed to the intrusion, offering an explanation rather than an objection. (Granted, unlike sustained and unblinking eye contact, beating eggs does not appear to be one of Ms. Holmes’s strong suits. Typically, one does so vigorously to aerate them and make them fluffier for an omelet or scramble.)

But with this being Elizabeth Holmes, we must also ask: Could it be a case of weaponized incompetence? Is it possible that, not wanting the task of beating the eggs, she intentionally does so insufficiently to make Evans take over? Is she being controlled, or is she getting someone else to do her work for her? Or is this evidence of her “different” way of thinking, that she truly doesn’t know the “right” way to beat them? 

In interviews in the film, Holmes portrays the Theranos years as a period during which she was manipulated by her former boyfriend and business partner, Sunny Balwani, and maintains that her intentions were ultimately good for humanity, regardless of the consequences for investors and patients who received unreliable test results. Whether she actually believes this account of events, or simply knows how to sell it, remains one of the film’s central mysteries — as does the extent to which her romantic relationships lead her to take the fall for the actions of bad worse men.

These questions reverberate in the second half of the film, when — with Holmes now in prison — we learn some unsettling things about Evans. He has power of attorney over Holmes, and has been using her X account to post odd, profanity-laced remarks that seem entirely incongruent with her own manner of speaking. Suddenly, the eggs scene looks like an early indication that Evans is far more domineering than the protective-partner persona he’s been presenting. His ostensible vigilance toward Holmes may be genuine on some level, but it’s difficult to reconcile with his willingness to license her image for billboards promoting another (bizarre-seeming) entrepreneur’s Theranos obsession, or his apparent acceptance of the possibility that her AI-generated likeness could be used to create pornography or other content antithetical to her interests. 

And yet, even as Evans emerges as an increasingly unsavory character, Holmes remains inscrutable. Does she know what he’s doing? Does she approve? Is he exploiting her circumstances, or are they working toward a shared objective — reputational, monetary, or otherwise? The film offers kaleidoscopic evidence for competing interpretations, including that even if Holmes is being dominated, she may be content with that dynamic. 

What’s extraordinary about Fielder and Oppenheim’s documentary is its optical-illusion-like layers of “truth.” Despite the film’s many harrowing close-ups of microexpressions, there is what Holmes and Evans say, then there is what they do, or have done, or plan to do (launch a Theranos 2.0 called Haemanthus, apparently). There is also Fielder’s own history as a provocateur, a faux-earnest magician who will go to virtually any lengths to actualize a concept, and who seems remarkably capable of keeping a stone-cold straight face through any scenario. 

We know enough about Fielder to question his sincerity, too. There are several moments in the film where I — and the several friends I went to see the film with — wondered if there was palpable sexual chemistry between him and Holmes, and whether Fielder would act on it. If he did, we couldn’t agree on whether it would be genuine or an act of performance art, or whether he has any barriers between those things. 

The film’s most revealing moments are the ones that feel off-script for everyone involved: a fleeting facial expression, an odd word choice in an otherwise emphatic statement, a series of gestures exchanged over a bowl of scrambled eggs. The subjects, who have invited themselves to be watched, have few moments when they seem to forget that; those instances are when we ostensibly have the clearest insight into their reality. Frankly, it’s still murky, and that seems to be both the point and the irony in the film’s title.

You Can See Everything elevates Nathan Fielder’s reputation far beyond simply a master of cringe humor; there is, here, a glimmer of genius. This story feels almost fateful in its ability to showcase his gifts, illuminating the bottomless strangeness, the infinite complexities, of how humans interact with one another. Even when an idea is big, it’s the little things that matter the most. And in a documentary that promises you can see everything, we learn that it’s up to us to look closely — and even then, what we see might look different in another light.

Savory, spicy, and salty Thai condiments

When a recipe ingredient is labeled "Thai," such as Thai thin soy sauce, use a brand from Thailand. It makes a huge difference in how the recipe comes out. Stock your pantry with these condiments to craft great Thai food.

[1] Fish sauce น้ำำ˝ˇปลา is a liquid made from fermented fish and salt and is widely sold. The two brands I use the most are Thai- produced Trachang (scale logo) and Megachef. Two other commonly available Thai brands, Squid and Tiparos, are OK too. Those four Thai brands contain between 1,500 and 1,700 mg of sodium per tablespoon. If your brand differs from mine significantly, adjust as needed.

[2] Golden Mountain Seasoning Sauce ซอสภููเขา ฝาเขีียว has a distinctly savory, slightly sweet flavor that's akin to lighter Maggi Seasoning sauce. It harmonizes well with Thai ingredients, whereas Maggi easily overwhelms. If unavailable, substitute Thai thin soy sauce. 

[3] Sea salt เกลืือทะเล , particularly fine sea salt that clocks in at about 550 mg per ¼ teaspoon, is what I often use in recipes. Check the nutrition label on your sea salt and adjust the amounts called for in recipes if needed.

[4] Thai black soy sauce ซีีอิ๊๊วดำำ˝ˇ is sweet, salty, and slightly syrupy- like diluted Indonesian kecap manis; this condiment is sometimes labeled sweet black soy sauce. I like the sauces by Megachef and Dragonfly, which is made by the Kwong Hung Seng company in Thailand. 

[5] Thai oyster sauce น้ำำ˝ˇมัันหอย tastes salty-sweet with a hint of brininess to evoke the piscine origin of the original condiment long ago. I prefer a Thai brand with relatively low sodium. Maekrua brand is mild and fine, but harder- to-find Megachef brand is punchier and better (check online, including Weee!). If you use non- Thai oyster sauce, use about 25 percent less. For vegan oyster sauce, we use Lee Kum Kee brand. 

[6] Thai sriracha ศรีีราชา is a chili-based hot, slightly garlicky, and mildly sweet condiment that originated in the 1930s in Si Racha, a town on the Gulf of Thailand. Generally available in two heat strengths, medium and strong, Thai-style sriracha works best for my dishes, like the Kin Khao Wings on page 78. I favor Sriraja Panich and Shark brands.

[7] Thai thin soy sauce ซีีอิ๊๊วขาว is not as dark as Chinese, Japanese, or Korean soy sauce, but it has a strong salinity and deep umami flavor so it pairs well with and can stand in for fish sauce in Thai vegetarian recipes. Healthy Boy thin soy sauce and Megachef gluten-free soy sauce work well. 

Umami from land and sea for Thai dishes

Along with sauces and salt, these umami-laden seasonings help build exciting Thai dishes.

[8] Dried shrimp กุ้้˝ˇงแห้้ง are used whole or chopped to add savory depth. Check the cold case of a Chinese or Southeast Asian market for small or medium ones and keep them frozen to preserve flavor and freshness. When possible, buy dried shrimp without food coloring; those may not be the brightest orange, but they'll be better for you. 

[9] Gapi กะปิิ (Thai shrimp paste) is the grounding umami in many Thai dishes. The dark mauve paste is typically sold in plastic jars, shelved in the fermented condiment or Thai section of Southeast Asian and Chinese markets. It is also sold online. Gapi is made from fermented shrimp (krill to be exact) and salt, plus maybe some MSG and sugar. Buy true, pure gapi made from just shrimp and salt, if you can. Brands such as Prateepthong (shrimp logo) and Trachang (scale logo) are good. Store it in the fridge, where it will last forever. Artisanal gapi is hard to find, so use my good gapi hack 0 (see page 118) to mimic excellent gapi produced in Thailand. Perplexingly, some Thai products labeled "shrimp paste" are not gapi. If the list of product ingredients includes oil and other spices, that's not gapi!

[11] Hatcho miso is a dark, thick, strong- flavored paste made from pure soybeans with no other grains (unlike other types of miso), is the best substitute for dried fermented soybeans, or tua nao ถั่่่วเน่่่า, from Northern Thailand. To make a swap for tua nao, I dehydrate miso and my recipes offer fast- and slow- drying methods. Shop for quality brands of hatcho miso, like Hikari or Marukome, at Japanese markets. When unavailable, use a dark aka (red) miso. If you are lucky enough to find real Thai tua nao, use it.

Tomarion 2

I learned about this magical heated rock from my favorite podcast, Add To Cart, and it’s a game changer. The Tomarion 2 is a kidney-shaped portable infrared heating pad made out of tourmaline, a naturally occurring mineral. It’s so great that I own TWO because I’m always loaning one out to one of my pals that has aches and pains.

There is no cord or battery—you simply plug it into a charger for six minutes and then it magically heats up and stays hot for hours. I use it when I have cramps, aches, pains, or just want to warm myself up. If you have someone you know who is always complaining about being cold or achy, this gift is perfect for them!

One caveat: the Tomarion 2 is not soft and smushy—it’s a hard object covered with a soft fabric that you press against your body. I love it and recommend it to all of my friends with cold extremities or chronic muscle pain.
