What does whisky taste like before it touches oak? Technically, we shouldn’t even be calling this a whisky. Japan’s Nagahama offers an opportunity to explore the character of their lightly peated new make spirit, stripping away the influence of maturation and puts the distillate itself front and center.

What is New Make?

New make (or white dog in the American whisky world) is essentially whisky post-distillation, pre-maturation. The majority of the flavors you find in your glass are a result of maturation and its many variables, however, there are several production choices made before the spirit enters the cask that have an enormous effect on flavor, mouthfeel, texture, and weight.

Nagahama Lightly Peated New Make Review

The nose demonstrates a pronounced malty character, with fresh cereal sweetness and a faint smoky edge. This expression is lightly peated, but does not carry any aggressively medicinal notes. There really isn’t much more to share here, and I’d be lying if I told you I’m picking up loads of flavor here – but that’s exactly the point of this exercise.

At 59% abv, this spirit is to be approached with caution. The absence of maturation brings additional heat to an already-aggressive abv. In terms of flavor, the malt-forward profile is joined by the light smoke we discovered on the nose. On my second taste after dilution, some orchard fruit reveals itself, but without oak to contribute vanilla, tannin, or dried fruit richness, the focus remains firmly on the underlying spirit.

The finish echoes the malty and gently smoky base, with fresh fruit becoming more apparent as the alcohol is brought into balance.

New make isn’t for everyone, but it certainly can be a fascinating to whisky enthusiasts interested in production processes. Without years in a cask to soften the spirit, integrate flavor, extract from the oak, and develop complexity, the distillate has nowhere to hide. This bottle makes me wish it were easier to get hold of new make from well-known distilleries to achieve a deeper understanding of their house style.

This expression also offers a useful opportunity to experience peated spirit before oak reduces some of the impact.

Overall, this is an educational bottle rather than a conventional sipping whisky.

In 2012, Eugen Beer, a British publicist, and Barbara McMahon, a Scottish journalist, were working on a project at home in the U.K. While searching through the University of Nevada’s online archives, they came across an old menu from Café Anglais in Paris, which closed in 1913. It featured a little boy feeding a piece of meat to the moon

Courtesy of Vintage Menu Art

“We were just completely charmed,” Beer says. “It opened a giant rabbit hole for us.”

The couple became engrossed in a world of menu collectors, archivists, and dealers they never knew existed. More than a decade later, they’ve pored over thousands of menus pulled from cardboard boxes and library shelves across the U.S.

They’ve found hand-painted cards from late-19th-century New York banquets, keepsakes from the golden age of cruise liners, and mid-century masterpieces made for Hawaiian hotels. They’ve unearthed forgotten commercial work by Saul Bass and Paul Rand, two of the 20th century’s most influential graphic designers. They even found a menu from 1947 that William Steig—the creator of Shrek—designed for Welton’s in Worcester, Massachusetts, listing Cognac at 75 cents.

Today, Beer and McMahon run the business Vintage Menu Art, selling menu prints from their ever-growing, 1,500-strong catalog. But if you ask Beer, the commercial appeal is almost beside the point. It’s more about their passion for preserving dining culture. “It’s a slice of Americana that Americans forgot,” he says.

Dining at restaurants became a part of American life just before the turn of the 20th century. As manifest destiny led Americans westward, cities sprang up across the heartland, creating new demand for hotels, restaurants, and bars.

“There was no dining-out culture in the U.K.—except for the very rich—until the ’60s,” Beer explains. “But in the U.S., whenever a place like Dodge City emerged, one of the first things they did was establish a hotel and import a bartender from the East Coast.”

Then, after Prohibition, restaurants boomed nationwide, and their owners began commissioning artists to make their menus stand out in an increasingly crowded field.

“The food at that time was all the same. It was steak, it was lobster, it was burgers,” Beer says. Whether it was promoting the zombie cocktail in Waikiki or the art of enjoying wine, the menus were a colorful form of marketing that helped turn ordinary meals into nights to remember.

Courtesy of Vintage Menu Art

In retrospect, they also reveal how restaurants kept drinks cheap to compete with other spots.

“Immediately after Prohibition, in 99% of places, cocktails would have started at 25 cents,” Beer says. “We have one menu from Tail o’ the Cock in the 1940s. The most expensive dish, a filet mignon, was $4.95, and a bottle of Château Margaux was $5.75.”

The U.S. was fertile ground for menu hunting for another reason: Americans held onto them. For generations, people kept menus tucked into scrapbooks, a practice Beer attributes to both rising purchasing power and a distinctly American appetite for souvenirs.

It’s a slice of Americana that Americans forgot.

Eugen Beer

The golden age of menu design flew under the radar for decades, but some of them eventually found their way into private and institutional collections. By the early 2010s, the Culinary Institute of America in New York alone had up to nearly 50,000 menus. Getting access to them was surprisingly difficult, though.

“At the New York Public Library, you’d have to book an appointment. You could only see two menus at a time,” Beer says.

Courtesy of Vintage Menu Art

Private collectors offered another way in. One was Henry Voigt, a former DuPont executive who started buying menus at auctions in the 1990s and eventually amassed more than 10,000. Beer considers him one of the foremost custodians of American menu art, and he agreed to let the couple work with his collection.

Ever since, Voigt has been a key inspiration and resource for Vintage Menu Art, which sells prints from his collection. Voigt has also donated more than 2,600 pre-20th-century menus to the American Antiquarian Society. Voigt would say he’s simply preserving “the history of everyday life.”

“[These menus] not only reflect what the people were eating but also what they were doing and what they valued—the symbolism of food,” Voigt tells me.

He says a menu can transport you to a moment in history, like one in his collection from Springfield, Massachusetts, dated April 16, 1865, the day after Abraham Lincoln’s assassination. Its black border looked like a printing error at first. Then Voigt realized it was printed that way to mourn the president’s death.

“You put yourself in that dining room,” he says, “and through dinner, you’re hearing church bells all over Springfield because they rang all day that Sunday.”

Courtesy of Vintage Menu Art Courtesy of Vintage Menu Art

For Beer and McMahon, rescuing forgotten menus is a labor of love. First, they scour auctions and follow leads for unique pieces. “We don’t care if it comes from Poughkeepsie or Punxsutawney. Does the menu speak to us?” Beer says of their search criteria.

Those that make the cut are scanned and cleaned up, pixel by pixel, removing creases, stains, and other damage. They also research the stories behind the designs, packing as much history as they can into each reproduction, including original menus, prices, and all. Then, they print them on archival paper and sell them online, at their permanent booth in L.A.’s Farmers Market, and at art and wine fairs on the West Coast.

Now, between museums and commercial print sales, these menus are reaching new audiences again. Beer says they still achieve what their creators intended.

“These were designed to entertain, to make people anticipate a nice time, and they’re still doing that,” he says. “When people pick up a print [at our stall in L.A.], they go, ‘Wow, have you seen this?’ They whip themselves up into a frenzy of excitement.”

This Buttermilk Pound Cake is a classic southern dessert with a rich, buttery flavor, a tender crumb, and a perfectly golden crust. The buttermilk adds a subtle tanginess and also helps create an incredibly moist cake that stays fresh for days.

Whether served plain, dusted with powdered sugar, or topped with fresh berries, this old-fashioned pound cake is perfect for holidays, potlucks, summer cookouts, family gatherings and Sunday suppers.

What Makes Buttermilk Pound Cake So Good

Buttermilk helps create a soft, tender crumb while adding a slight tang that complements the sweetness. Combined with plenty of butter and eggs, it produces a pound cake that’s rich without being overly heavy. There’s also some almond extract added which will please almond lovers. The result is a pound cake that’s moist, flavorful, and perfect for any occasion.

How To Make Buttermilk Pound Cake

(More detailed instructions in recipe card below.)

  1. Preheat the oven.
  2. Mix dry ingredients. Whisk together flour, baking powder, baking soda, and salt.
  3. Cream the butter, shortening, and sugar. Use an electric mixer (preferably stand mixer) to beat the butter, shortening, and sugar until light and fluffy.
  4. Add eggs one at a time.
  5. Beat in extracts.
  6. Add the flour mixture, alternating with the buttermilk.
  7. Spray a Bundt pan with baking spray with flour and add batter.
  8. Bake for 1 hour 15 minutes to 1 hour 30 minutes at 325. Place a piece of foil over it halfway through.
  9. Cool in pan 15 minutes and then invert onto a cake stand.

Can I Use Low Fat Buttermilk?

Yes. It will not be quite as rich, but you can use lowfat buttermilk.

Recipe Tips

  • Use room temperature ingredients. Butter should be softened and eggs and buttermilk at room temperature.
  • Don’t overmix once the flour is added.
  • Measure the flour correctly. Fluff the flour up some in the bag or container. Use a large spoon the gently scoop it into a dry measuring cup, overfilling it slightly. Use the back of a knife to run across the top and get rid of excess.
  • Properly grease the pan. Make sure the pan is fully coated with baking spray with flour.

Why Is My Pound Cake Dry?

Overbaking or measuring the flour incorrectly are the most likely reasons for a dry pound cake.

Buttermilk Pound Cake

This Buttermilk Pound Cake is a classic southern dessert with a rich, buttery crumb, a tender crumb, and a soft, moist texture. Buttermilk gives the cake a subtle tang that balances the sweetness. Enjoy a slice plain, dusted with powdered sugar, or topped with fresh berries and a dollop of whipped cream. Course Dessert Prep Time 20 minutes minutes Cook Time 1 hour hour 15 minutes minutes Total Time 1 hour hour 35 minutes minutes Servings 16 Calories 422kcal

Equipment

  • 10-inch Bundt Pan
  • Baking spray with flour

Ingredients

  • 3 cups all-purpose flour
  • ½ teaspoon baking powder
  • ½ teaspoon baking soda
  • ½ teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup salted butter softened
  • ½ cup vegetable shortening
  • 3 cups sugar
  • 5 large eggs room temperature
  • 2 teaspoons vanilla extract
  • 1 teaspoon almond extract
  • 1 cup buttermilk room temperature
  • whipped cream and berries for serving optional

Instructions

  • Preheat oven to 325 degrees.
  • Whisk together the flour, baking powder, baking sods, and salt. Set aside.
  • Use an electric mixer (preferably stand) to beat the butter, shortening, and sugar until light and fluffy, about 5 minutes.
  • Beat in eggs one at a time, stopping several times to scrape down the sides of the bowl.
  • Beat in vanilla extract and almond extract.
  • Add the flour mixture in 3 additions, alternating with the buttermilk in 2 additions, beating on low speed just until incorporated.
  • Spray bundt pan with baking spray with flour. Pour batter into pan.
  • Bake for 1 hour 15 minutes to 1 hour 30 minutes. Place a piece of aluminum foil over the cake halfway through the baking time so the top doesn't get too brown.
  • Let cool in pan for 10 to 15 minutes and then invert onto a cake stand. Serve with whipped cream and berries if desired.

Notes

You can use unsalted butter instead of salted butter. If you do, add 1/4 teaspoon more salt to the recipe.

Squash Casserole with Stuffing Top Recipe

This recipe is a wonderful way to turn yellow squash into a hearty side dish that pairs well with everything from Fried Chicken to Baked Ham. Best of all it uses simple ingredients and takes just 30 minutes in the oven.

In addition to squash and sweet onion, this Squash Casserole with Stuffing Top also has shredded carrot for a little color and extra flavor. A can of condensed cream of chicken soup makes it really creamy and hearty. Cream of Mushroom soup could be used instead.

How To Make Squash Casserole with Stuffing Top

(More detailed instructions and video in recipe card below.)

  1. Steam the squash and onion. Steam just until crisp-tender and then blot dry with paper towels. Place in prepared baking dish and season with salt and pepper.
  2. Mix the creamy filling. Stir together the shredded carrot, condensed soup, and sour cream. Add to casserole dish and mix into the squash and onions.
  3. Add the cheese. Arrange the cheese slices on top of the casserole.
  4. Add the stuffing. Mix the dry stuffing with melted butter and sprinkle on top.
  5. Bake until golden. Bake for 30 minutes or until golden on top.
  6. Serve.

Recipe Tips

  • Don’t overcook the squash. I like to steam the squash and onions before adding them to the casserole since it only bakes for 30 minutes. Just until they are crisp-tender. This ensures they will be tender and soft in the finished dish, but still have some texture.
  • Drain the squash well. Yellow squash contains a lot of moisture. After steaming, drain it thoroughly and dry it with paper towels to help prevent a watery casserole.
  • Try a combination of squash. Use a combo of yellow squash and zucchini to make it more colorful.
  • Customize the condensed soup. Condensed Cream of Mushroom or Cream of Celery Soup can be used instead of Cream of Chicken.
  • Watch the topping as it browns. Bake uncovered so the stuffing can brown. If it needs more color, allow a little more baking time.

Can I Make Squash Casserole Ahead Of Time?

Yes! The casserole can be completely assembled ahead of time and refrigerated until ready to bake. I recommend adding the stuffing just before baking so it doesn’t get soggy.

Storage

Leftovers will keep for about 4 days in an airtight container in the refrigerator. Reheat in a 350 degree oven to het the stuffing crispy again.

Squash Casserole with Stuffing Top

Squash Casserole with Stuffing Top is a creamy, cheesy casserole made from summer squash, onions, and sour cream. On top it has a delicious layer of stuffing. This is a vegetable casserole that's great to eat anytime of year.

 Course Side Dish Cuisine Southern Keyword Casserole Prep Time 20 minutes minutes Cook Time 30 minutes minutes Total Time 50 minutes minutes Servings 8 Calories 419kcal

Ingredients

  • 4-5 medium yellow squash
  • 1 medium sweet onion coarsely chopped
  • 1 shredded carrot
  • 1 (10.5-ounce) can condensed cream of chicken soup
  • 1/4 cup sour cream
  • 7 American cheese slices
  • 2 cups dry herbed stuffing mix
  • 4 tablespoons melted butter

Instructions

  • Preheat oven to 350 degrees F.
  • Steam squash and onion for 8 minutes. Blot dry with paper towels and place in a 2 1/2-quart baking dish that has been lightly greased. Lightly season with salt and pepper.
  • Add carrot, condensed soup, and sour cream to the casserole dish and mix together with the squash and onion.
  • Lay American cheese slices on top.
  • In a bowl, stir together stuffing mix and melted butter. Sprinkle over cheese slices.
  • Bake uncovered for 30 minutes.

Video

Notes

I use Pepperidge Farm Herb-Seasoned Stuffing. Stovetop stuffing also works. Use dry stuffing mix. Do not make the stuffing according to package directions. Just steam the squash until crisp-tender. It will continue to cook in the oven.
